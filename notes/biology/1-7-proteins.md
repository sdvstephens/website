## Proteins

Proteins are the workhorses of the cell, performing most cellular functions: catalysis (enzymes), structure, transport, signaling, immune defense, and movement. Proteins are polymers of **amino acids**.

### Amino Acid Structure

**Greek letter carbon naming.** In organic chemistry, carbons are labeled with Greek letters based on their distance from a reference functional group (usually a carboxyl or carbonyl). The carbon directly attached to the functional group is the **alpha carbon** ($C_{\alpha}$), the next one is **beta** ($C_{\beta}$), then **gamma** ($C_{\gamma}$), and so on:

$\ce{C_{\delta}$-C_{\gamma}-C_{\beta}-C_{\alpha}-COOH}

**The alpha carbon in amino acids.** In amino acids, the alpha carbon is the central carbon—the one directly bonded to the carboxyl group. It is the "hub" to which all four characteristic groups attach:

![Structure 27](/notes/biology/figures/chemfig-027.svg)
 

\small (the central C is $C_\alpha$)

The alpha carbon is bonded to four groups:

- **Amino group** ($\ce{-NH2}$): Basic; accepts protons
- **Carboxyl group** ($\ce{-COOH}$): Acidic; donates protons
- **Hydrogen atom** ($\ce{-H}$)
- **R group** (side chain): Varies among the 20 amino acids and determines properties

**How to find $C_{\alpha**$ in any amino acid structure:}

1. Locate the carboxyl group ($\ce{-COOH}$ or $\ce{-COO^-}$)
2. Find the carbon directly attached to the carboxyl carbon—that is $C_{\alpha}$
3. Verify: $C_{\alpha}$ should also be bonded to the amino group ($\ce{-NH2}$ or $\ce{-NH3+}$)

**Chirality at $C_{\alpha**$.} Because the alpha carbon has four *different* groups attached (H, $\ce{NH2}$, $\ce{COOH}$, R), it is a **chiral center**—meaning the molecule cannot be superimposed on its mirror image. This gives rise to L- and D- forms of amino acids. In biology, nearly all amino acids are the **L-form**. The only exception is **glycine** (Gly, G), where R = H, so the alpha carbon has two identical hydrogen substituents and is therefore achiral.

The R group determines whether an amino acid is:

- Nonpolar/hydrophobic (e.g., valine, leucine, isoleucine)
- Polar/hydrophilic (e.g., serine, threonine)
- Charged (e.g., aspartate, glutamate, lysine, arginine)

**The R group (side chain)** is what distinguishes one amino acid from another. The "R" stands for the *residue*—the variable portion attached to the alpha carbon. While the backbone ($\ce{NH2}$-$C_{\alpha}$-$\ce{COOH}$) is identical in all amino acids, the R group determines the amino acid's chemical behavior, how it interacts with water, and how it contributes to protein structure.

**1. Nonpolar (hydrophobic) R groups** contain only C and H, or have minimal electronegativity differences. These residues tend to cluster in the interior of proteins, away from water.

### Peptide Bonds

Amino acids are linked by **peptide bonds**, formed by a condensation reaction between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another:

$\ce{H2N-CH(R1)-COOH + H2N-CH(R2)-COOH ->[-H2O] H2N-CH(R1)-CO-NH-CH(R2)-COOH}$

A chain of amino acids is called a **polypeptide**. The sequence of amino acids (determined by DNA) is the **primary structure**.

### Levels of Protein Structure

Proteins have four levels of structure:

1. **Primary structure**: The linear sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide chain. Determined by genes.
2. **Secondary structure**: Local folding patterns stabilized by hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms:
    
- **$\alpha$-helix**: Coiled structure; H-bonds between every 4th amino acid
- **$\beta$-sheet**: Pleated sheet; H-bonds between adjacent strands
3. **Tertiary structure**: The overall 3D shape of a single polypeptide, determined by interactions between R groups:
    
- Hydrophobic interactions (nonpolar R groups cluster inside)
- Hydrogen bonds
- Ionic bonds
- Disulfide bridges (covalent bonds between cysteine residues)
4. **Quaternary structure**: The arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits into a functional protein complex (e.g., hemoglobin has 4 subunits).

:::note
A protein's function depends on its shape. If the shape is disrupted (**denaturation**) by heat, pH changes, or chemicals, the protein loses function.
:::
