## Conjugation and the class equation

Key observation for classifying finite groups: **$G$ acts on itself by conjugation**: $g$ acts by $h\mapsto ghg^{-1}$. We’ve seen that this does define a group homomorphism $G\to\operatorname{Aut}(G)\subset\operatorname{Perm}(G)$, so it is indeed an action.

- The orbits of this action are the **conjugacy classes** in $G$, and the stabilizer of an element $h\in G$ is
$$
\operatorname{Stab}(h)=\{g\in G\mid ghg^{-1}=h\}=\{g\in G\mid gh=hg\},
$$
the subgroup of elements which commute with $h$. This is called the **centralizer** of $h$, $Z(h)\subset G$. Note $\bigcap_{h\in G}Z(h)=Z(G)$, the center of $G$, is the kernel of the action (i.e. the subgroup of elements which act trivially).
So: the action is trivial when $G$ is abelian; it is faithful iff $Z(G)=\{e\}$.

**How does this help?**

- The conjugacy classes form a partition of $G$, so
$$
\boxed{\;|G|=\sum_{\substack{C\subset G\\ \text{conj. class}}}|C|\;}\qquad(*)
$$
- For each conjugacy class, $|C_h|=\dfrac{|G|}{|Z(h)|}$ divides $|G|$.
- Moreover $|C_e|=1$ for the identity element, and $|C_h|=1$ iff $h\in Z(G)$.

$(*)$ is called the **class equation** of the group $G$.

This is extremely useful. For example:

:::theorem
If $|G|=p^2$ for $p$ prime, then $G$ must be abelian.
:::

:::proof
- Conjugacy classes have order $|C|\in\{1,p,p^2\}$, and $\sum|C|=p^2$. Thus the number of conjugacy classes such that $|C|=1$, i.e. of central elements of $G$, must be a multiple of $p$. Hence $p\mid|Z(G)|$.
- $Z(G)$ is a subgroup of $G$, so $|Z(G)|$ divides $p^2$: it’s $p$ or $p^2$. If $|Z(G)|=p^2$ then $G$ is abelian!
- Now assume $|Z(G)|=p$, and let $g\notin Z(G)$. Then $g$ commutes with itself and with $Z(G)$, so $Z(g)\supset Z(G)\cup\{g\}$, hence $|Z(g)|>p$. But $Z(g)$ is a subgroup of $G$, so $|Z(g)|\mid p^2$. This implies $Z(g)=G$, i.e. $g$ commutes with all elements of $G$, i.e. $g\in Z(G)$: contradiction. So $Z(G)=G$, $G$ is abelian. $\square$
:::

(Hence the only groups of order $p^2$ up to isomorphism are $\ZZ/p^2$ and $\ZZ/p\times\ZZ/p$.)

:::proposition
There are exactly 5 groups of order 8 up to isomorphism.
:::

We know the 3 abelian ones: $\ZZ/8$, $\ZZ/2\times\ZZ/4$, $(\ZZ/2)^3$. We know $D_4$ = the symmetries of the square. Finally: the quaternion group $\{\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}$ with
$$
i^2=j^2=k^2=-1,\qquad ij=k,\quad jk=i,\quad ki=j
$$
(multiplication by $-1$ flips signs).

Two ways to show there are only two nonabelian groups of order 8:

- **“By hand”** (see the HW hint): suppose $|G|=8$ and $G$ is not abelian.
  - Step 1: a group where every element has $g^2=1$ must be abelian, so there must be an element $a$ of order 4 (order 8 would make $G\simeq\ZZ/8$).
  - Step 2: the order 4 subgroup generated by $a$ is normal. Work out the possibilities for multiplication by an element $b$ such that $ab\neq ba$. (Need: $b^2$? $bab^{-1}$?)
- **Using conjugacy and the class equation:**
  - Step 1: class equation $8=\sum|C|$, $|C|\in\{1,2,4,8\}$, $|C_e|=1$ $\Rightarrow Z(G)=\{g\mid |C_g|=1\}$ has order 2, 4, or 8. $8\Rightarrow G$ abelian. 4 is impossible by the same argument as for $p^2$ above. So $|Z(G)|=2$.
  - Step 2: if $g\notin Z(G)$ then $Z(g)\subsetneq G$, but $Z(G)\cup\{g\}\subset Z(g)$. So $|Z(g)|=4$, and $|C_g|=2$. Hence the class equation is
  $$
  8=\underbrace{1+1}_{e\text{ and the other central element}}+\underbrace{2+2+2}_{3\text{ other conj. classes}}.
  $$
  Then work out the possibilities!

## Conjugacy classes in the symmetric group $S_n$

- A **$k$-cycle** $\sigma=(a_1\,a_2\,\dots\,a_k)\in S_n$, where $a_1,\dots,a_k$ are distinct elements of $\{1,\dots,n\}$, is a permutation mapping
$$
a_1\mapsto a_2,\quad a_2\mapsto a_3,\quad\dots,\quad a_k\mapsto a_1,
$$
and all other elements to themselves.
- Two cycles are **disjoint** if the subsets of elements they cycle are disjoint. Disjoint cycles commute.

:::proposition
Any permutation can be expressed as a product of disjoint cycles, uniquely up to reordering the factors (disjoint cycles commute, so the order doesn’t matter).
:::

Algorithm: look at the successive images of 1 under $\sigma$; this gives a subset of elements that are cyclically permuted by $\sigma$. Then consider the elements not in this subset, and repeat.

In other terms: the various cycles are the restrictions of $\sigma$ to the **orbits** of $\langle\sigma\rangle\subset S_n$ on $\{1,\dots,n\}$.

:::example
$$
\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3&4&5&6\\ \downarrow&\downarrow&\downarrow&\downarrow&\downarrow&\downarrow\\ 3&5&6&4&2&1\end{pmatrix}=(1\,3\,6)(2\,5).
$$
Here $(1\,3\,6)$ = the successive images of 1 under $\sigma$ until it returns to 1; $(2\,5)$: the same for the other elements not in the previous cycles.
:::

:::proposition
Let $\sigma=(a_1\,\dots\,a_k)$ be a $k$-cycle and $\tau\in S_n$ any permutation; then $\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}=(\tau(a_1)\,\dots\,\tau(a_k))$.
:::

:::proof
Calculate: $\tau(a_i)\mapsto a_i\mapsto a_{i+1}\mapsto\tau(a_{i+1})$ (under $\tau^{-1}$, then $\sigma$, then $\tau$), so the action on $\{\tau(a_i)\}$ is as claimed. Other elements: $\tau(b)\mapsto b\mapsto b\mapsto\tau(b)$.
:::

:::corollary
All $k$-cycles are conjugate in $S_n$.

More generally, $\sigma,\tau\in S_n$ are conjugate iff they have the same cycle lengths in their disjoint cycle decompositions.

Hence, conjugacy classes in $S_n$ correspond to **partitions of $n$**, i.e. ways to write $n$ as a sum of positive integers (up to reordering the terms).
:::

:::example $n=3$
| partition | description | size of conj. class |
|---|---|---|
| $3=1+1+1$ | identity (only “1-cycles”) | 1 |
| $3=2+1$ | transpositions $(i\,j)$ | 3 |
| $3=3$ | 3-cycles | 2 |
:::

:::example $n=4$
| partition | description | size of conj. class |
|---|---|---|
| $1+1+1+1$ | id | 1 |
| $2+1+1$ | transposition | 6 |
| $2+2$ | 2 transpositions | 3 |
| $3+1$ | 3-cycle | 8 |
| $4$ | 4-cycle | 6 |

The class equation of $S_4$ is $24=1+3+6+6+8$.

This helps us find **normal subgroups of $S_4$**: $H\subset G$ is normal iff $aHa^{-1}=H$ $\forall a\in G$. So a normal subgroup is a union of conjugacy classes! Also, it must include id, and $|H|$ divides $|G|$. Here: apart from $\{\mathrm{id}\}$ and $S_4$, the only candidates are

- $1+3=4\mid 24$: $\{\mathrm{id}\}\cup\{(i\,j)(k\,l)\}$. This is indeed a normal subgroup ($\simeq\ZZ/2\times\ZZ/2$).
- $1+3+8=12\mid 24$: $\{\mathrm{id}\}\cup\{(i\,j)(k\,l)\}\cup\{3\text{-cycles}\}$. This is the alternating group $A_4\subset S_4$.
:::

:::example $n=5$
| partition | description | size of conj. class |
|---|---|---|
| $1+1+1+1+1$ | id | 1 |
| $2+1+1+1$ | transposition | 10 |
| $2+2+1$ | 2 transpositions | 15 |
| $3+1+1$ | 3-cycle | 20 |
| $3+2$ | 3-cycle + transposition | 20 |
| $4+1$ | 4-cycle | 30 |
| $5$ | 5-cycle | 24 |

Class equation: $120=1+10+15+20+20+24+30$.

Search for normal subgroups (besides $\{\mathrm{id}\}$ and $S_5$): the only options are

- $1+15+24=40$: $\{\mathrm{id}\}\cup\{(i\,j)(k\,l)\}\cup\{5\text{-cycles}\}$. This is *not* a subgroup (not closed under composition): $(1\,2\,3\,4\,5)(1\,2)(3\,4)=(1\,3\,5)$.
- $1+15+20+24=60$: id, $(i\,j)(k\,l)$, 5-cycles, and either 3-cycles or (3-cycle)(transposition) (2 possibilities for the 20). Only the first option (3-cycles) works, and gives $A_5\subset S_5$. (The other isn’t closed under composition.)
:::

## The alternating group

Recall we’ve defined the sign homomorphism $S_n\to\{\pm1\}$ by
$$
\sgn\Big(\prod_{i=1}^k\text{transpositions}\Big)=(-1)^k,
$$
using that transpositions generate $S_n$; we still need to check this is independent of how we express $\sigma$ as a product of transpositions. I mentioned: $\sgn(\sigma)=(-1)^{\#\text{inversions}}$, where
$$
\text{inversions}=\{(i,j)\mid 1\le i<j\le n\text{ and }\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\}
$$
(but then… check it’s a homomorphism?).

Now we can do better: take a vector space $V\simeq\RR^n$ with basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$; then to each $\sigma\in S_n$ we associate an element of $GL(V)=GL(n)$: the linear map $T_\sigma:V\to V$ such that $e_i\mapsto e_{\sigma(i)}$. This gives an injective homomorphism $S_n\hookrightarrow GL(n)$ (with image the subgroup of “permutation matrices”).

Now, $T_\sigma$ has finite order (since $\sigma$ does), hence $\det(T_\sigma)\in\RR$ is a root of unity, hence $\in\{\pm1\}$. We can define $\sgn(\sigma)=\det(T_\sigma)$: this is clearly well defined *and* a homomorphism.

Concretely, to compute the sign: $\Lambda^nT_\sigma$ acts on $\Lambda^nV$ by $e_1\wedge\dots\wedge e_n\mapsto e_{\sigma(1)}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{\sigma(n)}$, and the sign is $(-1)^{\#\text{transpositions}}$, counting the transpositions needed to switch these back in order; so this agrees with the other definition.

:::outline Notes not reached in this lecture, covered at the start of {{LINK:L25}}
:::note Observe
A $k$-cycle has sign $(-1)^{k-1}$ (since $(i_1\,\dots\,i_k)=(i_1\,i_2)(i_2\,i_3)\cdots(i_{k-1}\,i_k)$).

So if $\sigma\in S_n$ has cycle lengths $k_1,\dots,k_\ell$ (including the 1’s), i.e. corresponds to the partition $n=k_1+\dots+k_\ell$, then
$$
\sgn(\sigma)=(-1)^{\sum(k_i-1)}=(-1)^{n-\ell}.
$$
:::

:::definition
$A_n=\ker(\sgn)\subset S_n$, the **alternating group** (a normal subgroup of index 2 in $S_n$).
:::

:::proposition
If $C\subset S_n$ is a conjugacy class, then either

1. $C$ is odd, $C\cap A_n=\emptyset$; or
2. (a) $C\subset A_n$ is a conjugacy class in $A_n$; or (b) $C\subset A_n$ splits into 2 conjugacy classes in $A_n$.
:::

Case 2a vs. 2b: take $\sigma\in C$ and its centralizer $Z(\sigma)=\{\tau\in S_n\mid\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}=\sigma\}$. Is $Z(\sigma)\subset A_n$ or not? If yes, then the conjugates of $\sigma$ by odd permutations are different from the conjugates by even permutations, and these form two conjugacy classes in $A_n$. If not, then all conjugates of $\sigma$ in $S_n$ are conjugates by elements of $A_n$.

:::example $n=5$
$$
A_5=\underbrace{\{\mathrm{id}\}}_{1}\cup\underbrace{\{(i\,j)(k\,l)\}}_{15}\cup\underbrace{\{3\text{-cycles}\}}_{20}\cup\underbrace{\{5\text{-cycles}\}}_{24}.
$$

- 3-cycles still form a single conjugacy class in $A_5$ (because $(4\,5)\in Z((1\,2\,3))$); also for the $(i\,j)(k\,l)$’s ($(i\,j)\in Z((i\,j)(k\,l))$).
- 5-cycles split into 2 conjugacy classes in $A_5$.

So the class equation of $A_5$ is $60=1+15+20+12+12$.

We can now look for normal subgroups of $A_5$. We can’t reach a divisor of 60 in any nontrivial way, hence the only ones are $\{1\}$ and $A_5$.
:::

Hence:

:::proposition
$A_5$ is simple.
:::
:::
