## Generators, presentations, and Cayley graphs

**Recall.**

- The **free group** $F_n$ on $n$ generators $a_1,\dots,a_n$. Its elements are all **reduced words**
$$a_{i_1}^{m_1}\cdots a_{i_k}^{m_k},\qquad k\ge 0\ (\text{the empty word is } e),\quad i_1,\dots,i_k\in\{1,\dots,n\},\ i_j\neq i_{j+1},\quad m_1,\dots,m_k\in\ZZ\setminus\{0\}.$$
(Non-reduced words: reduce them by
  - if $i_j=i_{j+1}$, combining $a_i^m a_i^{m'}\rightsquigarrow a_i^{m+m'}$;
  - if an exponent is zero, removing $a_i^0$.

  Repeat until the word is reduced.)

- This is the “largest” group with $n$ generators: all others are $\cong$ quotients of $F_n$. If $G$ is generated by $g_1,\dots,g_n\in G$, define a homomorphism $\varphi:F_n\surjto G$ by setting $a_i\mapsto g_i$ (and so $\prod a_{i_j}^{m_j}\mapsto\prod g_{i_j}^{m_j}$).

- A finitely generated group is said to be **finitely presented** if the kernel of $\varphi$ is the smallest normal subgroup of $F_n$ containing some finite subset $\{r_1,\dots,r_k\}\subset F_n$ (words in the generators), i.e. the subgroup generated by the $r_j$’s and their conjugates $x^{-1}r_jx$. Write
$$G\cong\langle\underbrace{a_1,\dots,a_n}_{\text{generators}}\mid\underbrace{r_1,\dots,r_k}_{\text{relations}}\rangle;\qquad\text{then } G\cong F_n/\langle\text{conjugates of } r_1,\dots,r_k\rangle.$$

:::example
$\ZZ^n\cong\langle a_1,\dots,a_n\mid a_ia_ja_i^{-1}a_j^{-1}\ \forall i,j\rangle$.
:::

:::example
$S_3\cong\langle s_1,s_2\mid s_1^2,\ s_2^2,\ (s_1s_2)^3\rangle$.
:::

- Concretely, given generators $g_1,\dots,g_n\in G$, it is not hard to find relations $r_1,\dots,r_k$, i.e. words in the free group $F_n$ such that under $\varphi:F_n\to G$, $a_i\mapsto g_i$, we have $r_j\mapsto e$. If these relations hold in $G$, then $\varphi$ induces a *surjective* homomorphism
$$\langle a_1,\dots,a_n\mid r_1,\dots,r_k\rangle=F_n/\langle\text{conjugates of the } r_j\rangle\surjto G.$$
This is an isomorphism once we have found a *complete* set of relations among the $g_i$, i.e. when $r_1,\dots,r_k$ and their conjugates generate $\ker(\varphi)$.

- How to work with a group described by generators and relations? Sometimes we know what $G$ is, but sometimes we don’t. Two useful ideas (among many): (1) the Cayley graph; (2) normal forms.

## The Cayley graph

:::definition
Given generators $g_1,\dots,g_n\in G$, the **Cayley graph** of $G$ has
- vertices = the elements of the group;
- two vertices $s,t$ connected by an edge labelled $g_i$ when $t=sg_i$.
:::

[Here we’re doing right multiplication; one could do left multiplication instead.]

:::example
$\ZZ$ with its usual generator $1$:

![Cayley graph of Z with generator 1: a line of vertices ..., -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, ... with an arrow labelled 1 from each to the next](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-z-cayley.svg)
:::

:::example
$S_3$ with generators $s_1=(1\,2)$, $s_2=(2\,3)$. (Here $s_i^{-1}=s_i$, so the edges are undirected.)

![Cayley graph of S3 with generators s1=(12), s2=(23): a hexagon with vertices e, s1=(12), s1s2=(123), (13), s2s1=(132), s2=(23), edges alternately labelled s1 and s2](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-s3-cayley.svg)

The fact that this closes up shows the relation $s_1s_2s_1=s_2s_1s_2$ ($\iff(s_1s_2)^3=e$). Since any word in $s_1,s_2$ with relations $s_1^2=s_2^2=e$ can be reduced to $(\cdots s_1s_2s_1\cdots)$, one can use this to check $S_3\cong\langle s_1,s_2\mid s_1^2,s_2^2,(s_1s_2)^3\rangle$.
:::

:::example
$S_4$ with generators $s_i=(i\ i{+}1)$, $1\le i\le 3$ (the “permutohedron”):

![Cayley graph of S4 with generators s1, s2, s3: the permutohedron (truncated octahedron), 24 vertices labelled by permutations in one-line notation from 1234 at the bottom to 4321 at the top, with square and hexagonal faces](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-s4-permutohedron.svg)

The faces are squares, giving the relation $s_1s_3=s_3s_1$, and hexagons, giving $s_1s_2s_1=s_2s_1s_2$ and $s_2s_3s_2=s_3s_2s_3$.
:::

More generally, $S_n$ has the following presentation:
$$S_n\cong\langle s_1,\dots,s_{n-1}\mid s_i^2=1\ \forall i,\quad s_is_j=s_js_i\ \forall\,|i-j|\ge 2,\quad s_is_{i+1}s_i=s_{i+1}s_is_{i+1}\rangle.$$

:::proposition Fact
$G$ acts on its Cayley graph by “left multiplication”: on vertices $s\mapsto gs$, on edges $(s\to sg_i)\mapsto(gs\to gsg_i)$.
:::

This action is transitive on vertices (and on edges of a given label $g_i$): the graph is very symmetric!

- The **word length** of an element of $G$ (in the given generators) $:=$ the shortest distance from $e$ to $g\in G$ in the Cayley graph.

- For *infinite* groups, we can ask about the **growth rate** of $G$: given a set of generators $g_i$, how does the number of elements represented by words of length $\le N$ grow with $N$? Especially: does it grow *polynomially* or *exponentially*?

  Even if we change our set of generators to some other $g'_j$, the word length of a given element changes by a bounded factor only (bound = word lengths of the new generators in terms of the old ones and vice versa). So the exponential or polynomial nature of the growth is independent of the set of generators.

:::example
Finitely generated abelian groups have polynomial growth; free groups have exponential growth.
:::

- For finite groups, the Cayley graph is finite and growth isn’t relevant, but questions about word length remain interesting!

:::example
In $S_n$, with generators $\{s_i=(i\ i{+}1)\}$: the longest element is $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\cdots&n\\ \downarrow&\downarrow&&\downarrow\\ n&n-1&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}$, of word length $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$, and the word length of $\sigma\in S_n$ is the number of inversions (pairs $i<j$ such that $\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)$).

This is best understood by representing permutations as diagrams:

![A permutation drawn as a diagram: points 1, 2, ..., n on a top line joined by strands to points on a bottom line, the strand from 1 ending at position sigma(1) and the strand from 2 ending at position sigma(2)](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-perm-diagram.svg)

- Composition = stacking diagrams.
- The expression in terms of the $s_i$ comes from decomposing the diagram into layers with a single crossing:

![The generator s_i as a diagram: vertical strands, except the strands at positions i and i+1 which cross](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-si-layer.svg)

- The presentation of $S_n$ $\iff$ any two diagrams for $\sigma$ are related by the moves

![The three moves on permutation diagrams: (1) two consecutive crossings of the same strands cancel, s_i^2=e; (2) crossings of far-apart strands slide past each other, s_i s_j = s_j s_i for |i-j|>=2; (3) a strand moves across the crossing of two others, s_i s_{i+1} s_i = s_{i+1} s_i s_{i+1}](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-sn-moves.svg)

- The word length $w(\sigma)=\#\text{inversions}$ is now clear.
- We can even list all the shortest words that can represent a given permutation! Namely:
$$\sigma\in S_n\text{ has a shortest word ending with } s_i\iff w(\sigma s_i)<w(\sigma)\iff\sigma(i+1)<\sigma(i).$$
Call the set of such $i$ the “ending set” of $\sigma$. Then for each $i\in$ ending set, repeat the process for $\sigma s_i^{-1}=\sigma s_i$.
- For each $\sigma\in S_n$ we can find a preferred expression of $\sigma$ as a word in $s_1,\dots,s_{n-1}$ by choosing at each step the smallest $i$ such that $\sigma(i+1)<\sigma(i)$ to end the word.

  This gives a **normal form** for elements of $S_n$ (i.e. a preferred word representing each element), and hence a solution to the **word problem**: when do two words represent the same element? ($\iff$ when does a word represent $e\in G$?)
:::

$\bigstar$ For $S_n$, or other groups where it’s well understood how to “calculate elements” (e.g. groups of matrices, etc.), we don’t need fancy algorithms or normal forms to solve the word problem. In many groups, however, this is all we have!

## The braid group

:::example
The **braid group**
$$B_n=\langle s_1,\dots,s_{n-1}\mid s_is_j=s_js_i\ \forall\,|i-j|\ge 2,\quad s_is_{i+1}s_i=s_{i+1}s_is_{i+1}\rangle$$
(but $s_i^2\neq 1$).

![Braid generators: s_i, where the strand from position i crosses over the strand from position i+1; s_i^{-1}, where it crosses under; and the braid relation, two three-strand braids with three crossings each that are equivalent](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-braid-gens.svg)

Thus a braid is something like the picture below, up to isotopy, where strands never turn back up (important in knot theory, etc.!).

![Left: a braid on four strands between two horizontal lines, with strands crossing over and under each other. Right: its closure, obtained by joining each bottom endpoint to the corresponding top endpoint by an arc going around the right side](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-braid-closure.svg)
:::

:::theorem Markov’s theorem
Every knot or link in $\RR^3$ can be represented as the closure of a braid, and two braids have isotopic closures iff they’re related by a sequence of moves of 2 types:
- conjugation in $B_n$: $\sigma\in B_n\sim g\sigma g^{-1}\in B_n$ for all $g\in B_n$;
- stabilization: $B_n\cong\langle s_1,\dots,s_{n-1}\rangle\subset B_{n+1}$, and $\sigma\in B_n\sim\sigma s_n^{\pm1}\in B_{n+1}$.
:::

$B_n$ is much bigger than $S_n$, but its algorithmic aspects can be approached by the same method, using:

:::definition
**Permutation braids** are braids in which any 2 strands cross at most once, and all crossings are positive (of the same kind as in $s_i$, not $s_i^{-1}$). For example:

![A permutation braid on four strands: every pair of strands crosses at most once and at every crossing the strand moving to the right passes over](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-permutation-braid.svg)

These form a finite set, in bijection with $S_n$.
:::

Let $\Delta=$ the longest permutation braid. Since its shortest word can start/end with any $s_i$, $s_i^{-1}\Delta$ is still a permutation braid. Also note $s_i^{\pm1}\Delta=\Delta s_{n-i}^{\pm1}$, which lets us move $\Delta$’s to the left.

Thus any element of $B_n$ can be written as
$$g=\Delta^{-k}P_1\cdots P_r,\qquad P_j=\text{permutation braids}.$$
“Moving to the left everything that can be” $\implies$ we can find an expression such that
$$\{\text{ending set of } P_j\}\supset\{\text{starting set of } P_{j+1}\}\quad\forall j,$$
i.e. any way of adding the initial letter of a shortest word of $P_{j+1}$ to the end of $P_j$ would cause it to no longer be a permutation braid.

This gives rise to the “**Garside normal form**” and a solution to the word problem in $B_n$ (Garside 1969 + Thurston and Elrifai–Morton, early 1990s).

**$SL_2(\ZZ)$ and $PSL_2(\ZZ)$**

One more example:
$$SL_2(\ZZ)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\ \middle|\ a,b,c,d\in\ZZ,\ ad-bc=1\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad PSL_2(\ZZ)=SL_2(\ZZ)/\{\pm I\}.$$

:::proposition
$SL_2(\ZZ)$ is generated by $S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.
:::

:::proof
Given $M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL_2(\ZZ)$, we want to express it in terms of $S$ and $T$.

- If $c=0$, then $a,d=\pm1$, and $M$ is either $\begin{pmatrix}1&n\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=T^n$ or $\begin{pmatrix}-1&-n\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=S^2T^n$.
- Now assume $c\neq0$, and repeatedly apply the following algorithm to modify $M$:
  - if $|a|\ge|c|$, use Euclidean division to write $a=nc+r$, $|r|<|c|$. Then
$$T^{-n}M=\begin{pmatrix}1&-n\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a-nc&b-nd\\c&d\end{pmatrix}.$$
This decreases $\max(|a|,|c|)$.
  - if $|a|<|c|$,
$$S^{-1}M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}c&d\\-a&-b\end{pmatrix}$$
brings us back to $|a|>|c|$.

After finitely many steps we find that the product of $M$ with some word in $S$ and $T$ has $c=0$ and $|a|=1$, hence is $T^n$ or $S^2T^n$.
:::

- There is a different, geometric proof, based on the fact that $PSL_2(\ZZ)$ acts on the upper half plane $\mathbb{H}=\{z\in\CC\mid\operatorname{Im}z>0\}$ by
$$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}:\ z\mapsto\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.$$
Now $S$ acts by $z\mapsto-\frac1z$ and $T$ by $z\mapsto z+1$.

The region $\Delta=\{|z|\ge1,\ |\operatorname{Re}z|\le\frac12\}$ is a **fundamental domain** of this action, in the sense that $\Delta$ and its images under $PSL_2(\ZZ)$ exactly tile $\mathbb{H}$.

![The tiling of the upper half plane by images of the fundamental domain under PSL2(Z): the shaded region I above the unit circle between Re z = -1/2 and 1/2, the regions T^{±1}, T^{±2} beside it, the regions S, T^{±1}S, T^{±2}S below the unit circles, and smaller regions STS, ST, ST^{-1}, TST, etc. bounded by circles of radius 1/3; the real axis is marked from -2 to 2](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-modular-tiling.svg)

Since the regions immediately adjacent to $\Delta$ are $T^{\pm1}(\Delta)$ and $S(\Delta)$, the regions immediately next to $g(\Delta)$ are $gT^{\pm1}(\Delta)$ and $gS(\Delta)$. The structure of the tiling reproduces exactly the Cayley graph of $PSL_2(\ZZ)$ with generators $S,T$, and the fact that all regions can be reached from $\Delta$ in finitely many steps is equivalent to: $S,T$ generate $(P)SL_2(\ZZ)$.

- **Other generators**: instead of $S$ and $T$, we could use
  - $S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $R=ST=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. These have finite order! $S^4=R^6=I$.
  - $T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $T'=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=(TST)^{-1}$. These are conjugates! $T'=STS^{-1}$.
  - The images of these matrices in $PSL_2(\ZZ)=SL_2(\ZZ)/\{\pm I\}$ also generate $PSL_2(\ZZ)$.

:::theorem
$$PSL_2(\ZZ)\cong\langle S,R\mid S^2,R^3\rangle.$$
:::
