## Review

- Cosets of $K \subset G$: $aK$.
- Normal subgroup: $aK = Ka$.
- $G/K$ (a quotient group iff $K$ is normal).

:::definition Simple group
A group $G$ is a **simple group** if its normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and $G$.
:::

## Short exact sequences

:::definition Short exact sequence
A **short exact sequence** of groups is a diagram
$$
\{e\} \longrightarrow K \xrightarrow{\ \psi\ } G \xrightarrow{\ \varphi\ } H \longrightarrow \{e\}
$$
of groups mapping to each other by homomorphisms, such that the image of each homomorphism equals the kernel of the next one. (So $\varphi \circ \psi$ is trivial, $= \{e\}$.)
:::

Unpacking this:
- $\ker(\psi) = \{e\} \subset K$, so $\psi$ is injective.
- $\Img(\psi) = \ker(\varphi)$: for all $g \in G$, $\varphi(g) = e_H \iff g = \psi(k)$ for some $k \in K$.
- $\Img(\varphi) = H$, so $\varphi$ is surjective.

Consequently:
1. $K$ is isomorphic via $\psi$ to a subgroup $N = \Img(\psi) = \ker(\varphi) \subseteq G$, a normal subgroup of $G$.
2. $G/N = G/\ker(\varphi) \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } H$, $aN \mapsto \varphi(a)$, is a group isomorphism.

So the sequence looks like (girl what?)
$$
\{e\} \longrightarrow N \xrightarrow{\ \text{incl.}\ } G \xrightarrow{\ \text{quo.}\ } G/N \longrightarrow \{e\}.
$$

1. $0 \to \ZZ/3 \to \ZZ/6 \to \ZZ/2 \to 0$, with

[n] \mapsto [2n] \quad (n + 3\ZZ \mapsto 2n + 6\ZZ), \qquad [m] \mapsto [m \bmod 2] \quad (m + 6\ZZ \mapsto m + 2\ZZ).
$$
Here $N = \{0, 2, 4\} \subset \ZZ/6$ (a normal subgroup) is isomorphic to $\ZZ/3$, and the quotient is therefore $\cong \ZZ/2$ (how??).

## More about $S_n$

:::definition Cycle
A **cycle** ($k$-cycle) $\sigma = (a_1, a_2, \dots, a_k) \in S_n$ is the permutation that maps $a_1 \mapsto a_2, \dots, a_k \mapsto a_1$ and all others to themselves. A $2$-cycle is a **transposition**.
:::

:::proposition
Every permutation can be expressed as a product of disjoint cycles (cycling disjoint subsets of $\{1, \dots, n\}$), uniquely up to reordering the factors. Disjoint cycles commute.
:::

:::example
$$
\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 & 4 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = (1\,3\,6)(2\,5)(4) = (1\,3\,6)(2\,5).
$$
:::

- A $k$-cycle can be written as a product of $(k-1)$ transpositions:
$$
(a_1 a_2 \dots a_k) = (a_1 a_2) \circ (a_2 a_3) \circ \dots \circ (a_{k-1} a_k).
$$
So $S_n$ is generated by the transpositions $(i, j)$, $1 \leq i < j \leq n$.
- Also:
$$
(i, j) = (i\ i{+}1)(i{+}1\ i{+}2) \cdots (j{-}1\ j)(j{-}2\ j{-}1) \cdots (i\ i{+}1),
$$
for example $(1\,4) = (1\,2)(2\,3)(3\,4)(2\,3)(1\,2)$. Girl what? Girl yassss! Yassen queenen (little German boy). (Margin: parity: $2n$ or $2n+1$.) [Bubble sort algorithm.]

Permutations are odd or even depending on the length of an expression of $\sigma$ as a product of transpositions $\iff$ the parity of
$$
\#\{ (i, j) : 1 \leq i < j \leq n,\ \sigma(i) > \sigma(j) \}.
$$

:::proposition Claim
Even permutations form a normal subgroup $A_n$, called the **alternating subgroup** of $S_n$:
$$
\{e\} \to A_n \to S_n \xrightarrow{\ \text{sign}\ } \ZZ/2 \to \{e\}.
$$
:::

$A_3 \cong \ZZ/3$. $A_4$ has a normal subgroup $\cong \ZZ/2 \times \ZZ/2$ (?), but for $n \geq 5$, $A_n$ is simple.

There is no formula for the roots of polynomials of degree $n \geq 5$: Galois theory (Math 123 and 129).

## Noncommutativity of a group

:::definition Center
The **center** is the set of all elements that commute with everyone (the “central elements”):
$$
Z(G) = \{ z \in G : az = za \ \forall a \in G \}.
$$
:::

If $z_1, z_2 \in Z(G)$, then $a z_1 z_2 = z_1 a z_2 = z_1 z_2 a$ for all $a$, so $z_1 z_2 \in Z(G)$. This is a normal abelian subgroup, and $Z(G) = G \iff G$ is abelian.

:::definition Commutator subgroup
The **commutator subgroup** is
$$
C(G) = [G, G] = \Big\{ \prod_{i=1}^{k} [a_i, b_i] \;\Big|\; k \in \NN,\ a_i, b_i \in G \Big\}, \qquad [a, b] = aba^{-1}b^{-1} = ab(ba)^{-1}.
$$
It is always a normal subgroup.
:::

:::example
$[a, b]^{-1} = [b, a]$, and $[G, G] = \{e\}$ iff $G$ is abelian.
:::

The quotient $G/[G, G]$ is called the **abelianization** of $G$. Writing $C = [G, G]$:
$$
aC\,bC = abC, \qquad bC\,aC = baC, \qquad\text{and}\qquad abC = baC
$$
since $ba = ab(b^{-1}a^{-1}ba) = ab[b^{-1}, a^{-1}] \in abC$.
