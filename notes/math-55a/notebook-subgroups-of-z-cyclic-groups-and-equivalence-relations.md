## Recall

:::definition Subgroup
A **subgroup** is an $H \subset G$ such that $G$ is a group and $H$ is a nonempty subset which is closed under $*$ and inverses.
:::

:::definition Group homomorphism
Given groups $G, H$, a **homomorphism** $\varphi : G \to H$ is a map which takes products to products, $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$, and $\varphi(e_G) = e_H$, $\varphi(a^{-1}) = \varphi(a)^{-1}$.
:::

:::definition Kernel and image
The **kernel** of a group homomorphism is $\ker(\varphi) = \{ a \in G \mid \varphi(a) = e_H \}$. $\ker(\varphi)$ is a subgroup of $G$ (a “normal subgroup” (?)). $\varphi$ is injective $\iff \ker(\varphi) = \{e_G\}$.

The **image** of a group homomorphism $\varphi$ is
$$
\Img(\varphi) = \varphi(G) = \{ b \in H \mid \exists a \in G \text{ such that } \varphi(a) = b \}.
$$
This is a subgroup of $H$. $\varphi$ is surjective when $\Img(\varphi) = H$.
:::

:::remark
If $\varphi$ is injective, $G$ is isomorphic to $\Img(\varphi) \subset H$ (by $G \to \Img(\varphi)$, $a \mapsto \varphi(a)$).

## Subgroups of $(\ZZ, +)$

Here $+$ is simply addition. Given $a \in \ZZ^+$, $\ZZ a = \{ na : n \in \ZZ \} \subset \ZZ$ is a subgroup. This leads to:

:::proposition
All nontrivial subgroups of $(\ZZ, +)$ are of this form.
:::

:::proof
Given a nontrivial subgroup $\{0\} \neq H \subset \ZZ$, there exists $a \in H$ with $a > 0$. Let $a_0$ be the smallest positive element of $H$. Then all the multiples of $a_0$ are in $H$ ($\ZZ a_0 \subset H$). Given any $b \in H$, by Euclidean division there exist $q \in \ZZ$ (the quotient) and $0 \leq r < a_0$ (the remainder) such that $b = q a_0 + r$. Since $b \in H$ and $a_0 \in H$,
$$
q a_0 = \underbrace{a_0 + \dots + a_0}_{q \text{ times}} \in H,
$$
thus $r = b - q a_0 \in H$. We can’t have $0 < r < a_0$ (because of how $a_0$ was chosen), so $r = 0$ and $b = q a_0 \in \ZZ a_0$. Hence $H \subset \ZZ a_0$, so $H = \ZZ a_0$.
:::

So every subgroup of $\ZZ$ is generated by a single element. **Generation**: the element that builds all other elements in the group by repeating the group operation.

## The subgroup generated by a set

:::question
Given a subset $S \subset G$, what is the smallest subgroup of $G$ which contains $S$? This is denoted $\langle S \rangle$ and is called the **subgroup generated by $S$**.
:::

Answer to the question:
$$
\langle S \rangle = \bigcap_{\substack{H \in \mathcal{P}(G),\ S \subset H \subset G \\ H \text{ a subgroup of } G}} H.
$$
This is an intersection of subgroups, hence a subgroup. (Confused about this :-|)

A more constructive answer:
$$
\langle S \rangle = \{ a_1 \cdots a_k \mid k \in \ZZ_{\geq 0},\ a_1, \dots, a_k \in S \cup S^{-1} \}, \qquad S^{-1} = \{ s^{-1} \mid s \in S \},
$$
where the empty product ($k = 0$) is $e$. This contains $S$, is a subgroup of $G$, and is therefore the smallest.

## Cyclic groups and order

:::definition Cyclic group
A group is said to be **cyclic** if it is generated by a single element.
:::

:::example
$(\ZZ, +)$ and $(\ZZ/n, +)$ are both generated by $1$. But in $\ZZ/n$, when we take $\underbrace{1 + 1 + \dots + 1}_{n \text{ times}}$ we get the identity ($= 0$). These are the only cyclic groups “up to isomorphism”.
:::

:::example
Let $a \in G$ be any element. Then $\varphi : (\ZZ, +) \to G$, $n \mapsto a^n$ is a homomorphism since
$$
\varphi(n + m) = a^{n+m} = a^n a^m = \varphi(n)\varphi(m),
$$
with image $\langle a \rangle$, the cyclic subgroup of $G$ generated by $a$.
:::

:::definition Order of an element
The **order** of $a \in G$ is the smallest positive $n$ such that $a^n = e$; if there is none, $a$ is of infinite order. (Not $|G|$, which is the order of the group!)
:::

If $a$ has infinite order then the powers of $a$ are all distinct, $\varphi : n \mapsto a^n$ is injective, and $\langle a \rangle$ is isomorphic to $\ZZ$. If, however, $a$ has finite order, then $\ker(\varphi) = \ZZ n$ ($n$ = the smallest positive element of the kernel). Also $\langle a \rangle = \{ a^k \mid k = 0, \dots, n-1 \}$ is isomorphic to $\ZZ \bmod n$.

:::example
$\ZZ/6$ is isomorphic to $\ZZ/2 \times \ZZ/3$ via $a \mapsto (a \bmod 2, a \bmod 3)$. Observe: $(1,1) \in \ZZ/2 \times \ZZ/3$ has order $6$ (the smallest multiple of both $2$ and $3$). Similarly $\ZZ_{nm} \cong \ZZ_m \times \ZZ_n$ when $\gcd(m, n) = 1$ (Chinese remainder theorem), but $\ZZ_2 \times \ZZ_2 \not\cong \ZZ_4$ since in $\ZZ_2 \times \ZZ_2$ every nonzero element has order $2$.
:::

### Classifying finite groups up to isomorphism?

Increasingly difficult as $|G|$ increases.

- Order 1: $\cong \{e\}$
- Order 2: $\cong \ZZ_2$
- Order 3: $\cong \ZZ_3$
- Order 4: $\cong \ZZ_4$, $\ZZ_2 \times \ZZ_2$
- $\vdots$

The classification of finite simple groups was completed in the 1980s, but we will learn the key tools and methods. That end goal was finished, thus it is a closed question.

## Equivalence relations

(Artin §2.7 and Halmos.)

:::definition Equivalence relation
An **equivalence relation** on a set $S$ is a **binary relation**, i.e. a subset of $S \times S$ (the set of tuples $(a, b) \in S \times S$ with $a \sim b$), such that
1. reflexive: $a \sim a$ for all $a \in S$;
2. symmetric: $a \sim b \iff b \sim a$;
3. transitive: if $a \sim b$ and $b \sim c$, then $a \sim c$.
:::

:::definition Equivalence class, quotient set
The **equivalence class** of $a \in S$ is $\{ a' \in S \mid a \sim a' \}$, often denoted $[a]$. Using our axioms, if $a \sim a'$ then $[a] = [a']$.
- The equivalence classes **partition** $S$ into mutually disjoint subsets.
- The **quotient set** $S/{\sim}$ is the set of equivalence classes. It comes with the surjective map $S \surjto S/{\sim}$, $a \mapsto [a]$.
:::

:::example Congruence mod $n$
Let $S = \ZZ$. Given $n \in \ZZ_{>0}$, set $a \sim b$ iff $n$ divides $b - a$ (congruence mod $n$). This is an equivalence relation. There are $n$ equivalence classes:
$$
\begin{aligned}
[0] &= \{ \dots, -n, 0, n, \dots \} = \ZZ n, \\
[1] &= \{ \dots, 1 - n, 1, n + 1, \dots \} = 1 + \ZZ n, \\
&\ \ \vdots
\end{aligned}
$$
So $\ZZ/{\sim} \cong \ZZ/n$. The formal definition of $\ZZ/n$ uses equivalence classes. Note: equivalence classes may have many different names.
:::

## Lecture 3 continued: maps and quotients

Given a surjective map of sets $f : S \to T$, we can set $a \sim b$ iff $f(a) = f(b)$. This is an equivalence relation.

:::note
The equivalence classes are the subsets $f^{-1}(t) = \{ s : f(s) = t \}$, $t \in T$, and
$$
S/{\sim} \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } T, \qquad [a] \mapsto f(a).
$$
Since $[a] = [a']$ for all $a \sim a'$, this could also send $[a]$ to $f(a')$; that may cause trouble in general, but is fine here because $a \sim a' \implies f(a) = f(a')$.
:::

Generally, given a map $g : S \to T$ and an equivalence relation on $S$, when does $g$ **induce** a map
$$
\bar g : S/{\sim} \to T, \qquad [a] \mapsto g(a)\,?
$$
Necessary and sufficient: $g$ is **compatible** with $\sim$, in the sense that $a \sim a' \implies g(a) = g(a')$. Then $g$ factors through the quotient:
$$
\begin{CD}
S @>>> S/{\sim} @>{\bar g}>> T
\end{CD}
\qquad a \mapsto [a] \mapsto g(a) = \bar g([a]).
$$

:::example
The addition operation $+ : \ZZ^2 \to \ZZ$ induces an addition operation on $\ZZ/{\sim} \cong \ZZ/n$, $\bar{+} : \ZZ_n^2 \to \ZZ_n$, because it is compatible with the equivalence relation:
$$
\begin{CD}
\ZZ \times \ZZ @>{+}>> \ZZ \\
@VVV @VVV \\
\ZZ/n \times \ZZ/n @>{\bar{+}\ \text{(claim)}}>> \ZZ/n
\end{CD}
\qquad
\begin{CD}
(a, b) @>>> a + b \\
@VVV @VVV \\
([a], [b]) @>>> [a + b]
\end{CD}
$$
and the diagonal composite sends $(a, b) \mapsto [a + b]$. (I’m going to cry.)
:::

## Cosets of a kernel

Assume we have a surjective group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$, and $K = \ker(\varphi) = \{ g \in G : \varphi(g) = e_H \}$, a subgroup of $G$. Then
$$
\varphi(a) = \varphi(b) \iff \varphi(a)^{-1}\varphi(b) = e_H \iff \varphi(a^{-1}b) = e_H \iff a^{-1}b \in K \iff b = ak,\ k \in K.
$$
So for the relation $a \sim a' \iff \varphi(a) = \varphi(a')$, the equivalence class of $a \in G$ consists of the $b \in aK = \{ ak \mid k \in K \}$: it is the **left coset** $aK$.
