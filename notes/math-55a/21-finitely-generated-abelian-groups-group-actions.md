## Linear algebra over $\ZZ$

Recall: every abelian group $(G,+)$ is also a $\ZZ$-module, i.e. it has an operation
$$
\begin{aligned}
\ZZ\times G&\to G\\
n,g&\mapsto ng.
\end{aligned}
$$
Today: linear algebra over $\ZZ$ and the classification of **finitely generated abelian groups** (Artin §14.4–14.7).

:::theorem
Any finitely generated abelian group is isomorphic to a product of cyclic groups
$$
G\cong(\ZZ/n_1\times\cdots\times\ZZ/n_k)\times\ZZ^\ell.
$$
:::

(And using $\ZZ/mn\cong\ZZ/m\times\ZZ/n$ iff $\gcd(m,n)=1$, we can rearrange the finite factors, e.g. to arrange that all $n_i$ are powers of primes.)

The strategy for the classification is as follows.

:::proposition Prop. 1
If $M$ is a finitely generated $\ZZ$-module, then there exist $m,n$ and $T\in\Hom(\ZZ^m,\ZZ^n)$ such that $M\cong\ZZ^n/\operatorname{Im}T$. (Equivalently: there is an exact sequence $\ZZ^m\xrightarrow{\ T\ }\ZZ^n\to M\to0$.)
:::

(The quotient by a $\ZZ$-submodule is the same as the quotient of an abelian group by a subgroup.)

This relies on:

:::lemma
Any submodule ($=$ subgroup) of $\ZZ^n$ is finitely generated (in fact, free of rank $\le n$).
:::

:::proof
By induction on $n$. True for $n=1$: the subgroups of $(\ZZ,+)$ are $0$ and $\ZZ a$, $a\in\ZZ-\{0\}$.

Assume the result holds for $\ZZ^{n-1}$, and consider a submodule $M\subset\ZZ^n$. The map
$$
\begin{aligned}
\ZZ^n&\to\ZZ^{n-1}\\
(a_1,\dots,a_n)&\mapsto(a_2,\dots,a_n)
\end{aligned}
$$
restricts to a homomorphism $\pi:M\to\ZZ^{n-1}$, where

- $\operatorname{Im}\pi$ is a submodule of $\ZZ^{n-1}$, hence finitely generated (free) by induction;
- $\ker\pi=M\cap(\ZZ\times0\times\cdots\times0)$ is a subgroup of $\ZZ$, hence free (of rank 0 or 1).

And if $\ker(\pi)$ and $\operatorname{Im}(\pi)$ are finitely generated (resp. free), then so is $M$! The proof is just as in the proof of the rank theorem: let $e_1,\dots,e_k$ be generators (resp. a basis) of $\ker\pi$, and $g_1=\pi(f_1),\dots,g_m=\pi(f_m)$ generators of $\operatorname{Im}\pi$. Then for all $x\in M$ there exist $a_i\in\ZZ$ such that $\pi(x)=\sum a_ig_i$, so $\pi\big(x-\sum a_if_i\big)=0$, so
$$
x-\sum a_if_i\in\ker\pi=\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_k),
$$
hence $x\in\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_k,f_1,\dots,f_m)$: the $(e_i,f_j)$ generate. (Basis: left as an exercise; we won’t need it anyway.)
:::

:::proof Proof of the proposition
If $M$ is finitely generated, with generators $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$, then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi:\ZZ^n&\to M\\
(a_1,\dots,a_n)&\mapsto\sum a_ie_i
\end{aligned}
$$
is surjective, and $\ker(\varphi)=N\subset\ZZ^n$ is a subgroup/submodule of $\ZZ^n$, hence finitely generated by the lemma. Let $f_1,\dots,f_m$ be generators of $\ker\varphi$; then
$$
\ker\varphi=\operatorname{Im}\Big(T:\ZZ^m\to\ZZ^n,\ (b_1,\dots,b_m)\mapsto\sum b_if_i\Big),
$$
and now we have an exact sequence $\ZZ^m\xrightarrow{\ T\ }\ZZ^n\xrightarrow{\ \varphi\ }M\to0$, with $\ker\varphi=\operatorname{Im}T$, inducing an isomorphism $M\cong\ZZ^n/\operatorname{Im}T$.
:::

## Divisibility and primitive elements

The next ingredient is the notion of divisibility of an element of $\ZZ^n$ (or of a free $\ZZ$-module).

:::definition Divisibility
The **divisibility** of a nonzero element $x=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in\ZZ^n$ is the largest $d\in\ZZ_+$ for which there exists $y$ such that $x=d\cdot y$ (i.e. $d=\gcd(a_1,\dots,a_n)$).

An element of $\ZZ^n$ is **primitive** if its divisibility is $1$.
:::

:::lemma
An element of a free finitely generated $\ZZ$-module (e.g. $\ZZ^n$) can be chosen to be part of a basis iff it is primitive (or: it is $d$ times a basis element iff its divisibility is $d$).
:::

:::proof
- Clearly, elements of a basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ are primitive (linear independence prevents $e_i=d\big(\sum a_je_j\big)$ for some $d>1$).
- Converse: the Euclidean division algorithm. Let $v=a_1e_1+\cdots+a_ne_n$ be primitive. Without loss of generality assume $a_1\neq0$, $|a_1|=\min\{|a_i|:a_i\neq0\}$. Then let $a_k=q_ka_1+r_k$ (Euclidean division with remainder), and change basis to
  $$
  \Big(e_1'=e_1+\sum_{k\ge2}q_ke_k,\ e_2,\dots,e_n\Big)
  $$
  to get $v=a_1e_1'+r_2e_2+\cdots+r_ne_n$, which makes all other coefficients $<|a_1|$. Repeat this process; in finitely many steps we’re left with $v=d$ times a basis vector.

:::

:::proposition Prop. 2
For every $T\in\Hom(\ZZ^m,\ZZ^n)$, there exist bases $(e_1,\dots,e_m)$ of $\ZZ^m$ and $(f_1,\dots,f_n)$ of $\ZZ^n$, $r\le\min(m,n)$ (the rank of $T$) and positive integers $d_1,\dots,d_r$ such that
$$
T(e_i)=\begin{cases}d_i\,f_i & \text{if } 1\le i\le r\\ 0 & \text{if } i>r,\end{cases}
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad
\mathcal M(T)=\left(\begin{array}{ccc|c}
d_1 & & 0 & \\
 & \ddots & & 0\\
0 & & d_r & \\ \hline
 & 0 & & 0
\end{array}\right).
$$
:::

:::proof
If $T=0$ the statement is obvious for all $m,n$. Otherwise, proceed by induction on $m$.

Case $m=1$: let $d=\operatorname{div}(T(1))$; by the lemma there is a basis of $\ZZ^n$ such that $T(1)=d\,f_1$.

Assume the result is proved for $\ZZ^{m-1}$, and consider $T:\ZZ^m\to\ZZ^n$ (we can assume $T\neq0$). Let
$$
d_1=\min\{\operatorname{div}T(x)\mid x\notin\ker T\},
$$
and $e_1$ such that $\operatorname{div}T(e_1)=d_1$. Then $e_1$ is necessarily primitive (if it is divisible by $d$, then $\operatorname{div}T(\tfrac1d e_1)=\tfrac1d\operatorname{div}T(e_1)$), and write $T(e_1)=d_1f_1$, $f_1\in\ZZ^n$ primitive.

Using the lemma, complete to bases $(e_1,\dots,e_m)$ of $\ZZ^m$ and $(f_1,\dots,f_n)$ of $\ZZ^n$. Now
$$
\mathcal M\big(T,(e_i),(f_i)\big)=\left(\begin{array}{c|c}
d_1 & *\\ \hline
0 & \mathcal M(T')
\end{array}\right)
$$
where $T'$ is the restriction of $T$ to $\operatorname{span}(e_2,\dots,e_m)\cong\ZZ^{m-1}$, composed with the projection to $\operatorname{span}(f_2,\dots,f_n)\cong\ZZ^{n-1}$.

Use the induction hypothesis: replacing $(e_2,\dots,e_m)$ and $(f_2,\dots,f_n)$ with some other bases of their span, we can assume
$$
T'(e_j)=\begin{cases}d_jf_j & \text{for } j\le r\\ 0 & \text{for } j>r.\end{cases}
$$
Then
$$
\mathcal M(T)=\left(\begin{array}{c|ccc}
d_1 & a_2 & \cdots & a_m\\ \hline
 & d_2 & & 0\\
0 & & \ddots & \\
 & 0 & & d_r
\end{array}\right),
$$
i.e. $T(e_j)=d_jf_j+a_jf_1$ for some $a_j\in\ZZ$, for $j\ge2$ (with $d_j=0$ for $j>r$).

Write $a_j=q_jd_1+r_j$, and change basis to $(e_1,\ e_2'=e_2-q_2e_1,\ \dots,\ e_m'=e_m-q_me_1)$. Then
$$
\mathcal M(T)=\left(\begin{array}{c|ccc}
d_1 & r_2 & \cdots & r_m\\ \hline
 & d_2 & & 0\\
0 & & \ddots & \\
 & 0 & & d_r
\end{array}\right)\quad\text{with } 0\le r_2,\dots,r_m<d_1.
$$
Now $r_j\neq0$ would give $\operatorname{div}T(e_j')\mid r_j<d_1$, contradicting our choice of $d_1$. So $r_j=0$ for all $j\ge2$, and we’re done.
:::

:::note
The construction actually gives $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots$, because
$$
d_1=\min\{\operatorname{div}T(x)\}=\gcd\{\operatorname{div}T(x)\}=\gcd\text{ of the entries in }\mathcal M(T).
$$
:::

:::proof Proof of the theorem
Prop. 1 implies any finitely generated $\ZZ$-module $M$ is $\cong\ZZ^n/\operatorname{Im}(T)$ for some $T\in\Hom(\ZZ^m,\ZZ^n)$, and Prop. 2 implies that after a change of basis $(f_j)$ of $\ZZ^n$, we can assume $\operatorname{Im}(T)$ is spanned by $d_1f_1,\dots,d_rf_r$ for some $d_i>0$, $r\le n$. So
$$
M\cong\ZZ^n/\operatorname{Im}(T)\cong\ZZ/d_1\times\cdots\times\ZZ/d_r\times\ZZ^{n-r}.
$$
:::

**Group actions**

(Artin §6.7.)

:::definition Group action
An **action** of a group $G$ on a set $S$ is a homomorphism $\rho:G\to\operatorname{Perm}(S)$. Equivalently, we have a map
$$
\begin{aligned}
G\times S&\to S\\
(g,s)&\mapsto g\cdot s
\end{aligned}
\qquad\text{such that}\qquad
\begin{aligned}
e\cdot s&=s\quad\forall s\in S,\\
(gh)\cdot s&=g\cdot(h\cdot s).
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

This generalizes the idea of groups as symmetries of geometric objects. Understanding what sets a group $G$ acts on (and in what way) gives information about $G$!

:::definition Faithful action
An action is **faithful** if $\rho$ is injective.
:::

(Otherwise, the group that “really” acts on $S$ is $G/\ker\rho$...)

:::definition Orbit
The **orbit** of $s\in S$ under $G$ is $\mathcal O_s=G\cdot s=\{g\cdot s\mid g\in G\}\subset S$.
:::

Observe: $t\in\mathcal O_s\iff\exists g\in G$ such that $g\cdot s=t$, and then $s=g^{-1}\cdot t\in\mathcal O_t$. So the orbits of the $G$-action form a **partition** of $S=\bigsqcup\mathcal O_s$.

Equivalently: $s\sim t\iff\exists g\in G$ such that $g\cdot s=t$ is an equivalence relation:

- $s\sim s$ since $e\cdot s=s$;
- $s\sim t$ implies there is $g$ with $g\cdot s=t$; then $s=g^{-1}\cdot t$, so $t\sim s$;
- $s\sim t$ and $t\sim u$ imply there are $g$ with $g\cdot s=t$ and $h$ with $h\cdot t=u$; then $(hg)\cdot s=h\cdot(g\cdot s)=u$, hence $s\sim u$.

Orbits are the equivalence classes of this relation.

:::definition Transitive action
An action is **transitive** if there is only one orbit, i.e. for all $s,t\in S$ there exists $g$ such that $g\cdot s=t$.
:::

Note: given any $G$-action on $S$, by restriction we get a $G$-action separately on each orbit. Each of these is transitive (by definition), so we can break up any group action into a disjoint union of transitive actions!

:::definition Stabilizers and fixed points
- The **stabilizer** of $s\in S$ is $\operatorname{Stab}(s)=\{g\in G\mid g\cdot s=s\}$. This is a subgroup of $G$!
- The **fixed points** of $g\in G$ are the subset $S^g:=\{s\in S\mid g\cdot s=s\}$.

:::

:::proposition
If $s'=g\cdot s$ then $\operatorname{Stab}(s')=g\operatorname{Stab}(s)g^{-1}$. So: elements in the same orbit have conjugate stabilizers.
:::

:::proof
$h\cdot s=s$ implies $(ghg^{-1})\,g s=g(hs)=gs$, so $g\operatorname{Stab}(s)g^{-1}\subset\operatorname{Stab}(s')$. Conversely, the same argument for $s=g^{-1}s'$ gives $g^{-1}\operatorname{Stab}(s')g\subset\operatorname{Stab}(s)$, hence equality.
:::

:::example
Given a subgroup $H\subset G$, we have a set $G/H=\{\text{cosets }aH\}$. To avoid notational confusion, write $[H],[aH],\dots$ for elements of $G/H$.

$G$ acts on $G/H$ by left multiplication: $g\cdot[aH]=[gaH]$. This action is transitive ($ba^{-1}$ maps $[aH]$ to $[bH]$). $\operatorname{Stab}([H])=H$ itself, and $\operatorname{Stab}([aH])=aHa^{-1}$.
:::

Claim: this is what a general group action looks like when restricted to an orbit!

:::proposition
If $G$ acts on a set $S$, given $s\in S$, let $H=\operatorname{Stab}(s)\subset G$. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon: G/H&\to\mathcal O_s\\
[aH]&\mapsto a\cdot s
\end{aligned}
$$
is a **bijection**, and **equivariant**, i.e. it intertwines the $G$-actions: $\varepsilon(g\cdot[aH])=g\cdot\varepsilon([aH])$.
:::
:::
