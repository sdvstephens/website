## Conjugacy classes in $A_n$

Last time: every element of $S_n$ can be expressed as a product of a unique collection of disjoint cycles. Conjugacy classes in $S_n$ correspond to **partitions of $n$**, i.e. ways to express $n$ as a sum of positive integers (the lengths of the cycles).

The **alternating group** $A_n=\ker(\sgn: S_n\to\{\pm1\})$ is a nontrivial normal subgroup of $S_n$. We’ll see that for $n\ge 5$ it’s the only one.

:::remark
A $k$-cycle has sign $(-1)^{k-1}$, since
$$
(i_1\,\dots\,i_k)=(i_1\,i_2)(i_2\,i_3)\cdots(i_{k-1}\,i_k).
$$
So $\sigma\in A_n$ iff its cycle decomposition has an even number of cycles of even length.
:::

:::proposition
If $C\subset S_n$ is a conjugacy class, then either $C\cap A_n=\emptyset$ or $C\subset A_n$. In the latter case, either $C$ is a conjugacy class in $A_n$, or it splits into 2 conjugacy classes in $A_n$.

$C$ is a single conjugacy class in $A_n$ iff, given $\sigma\in C$, there exists an odd permutation $\tau$ that commutes with $\sigma$.
:::

:::proof
- All elements of $C$ have the same cycle lengths, hence the same sign. So $C\subset A_n$ or $C\cap A_n=\emptyset$. (Or: $A_n$ is a normal subgroup of $S_n$, hence a union of conjugacy classes.)
- Assume $C_\sigma=\{g\sigma g^{-1}\mid g\in S_n\}\subset A_n$. Then split $S_n$ into the 2 (right) cosets of $A_n$: $S_n=A_n\cup A_n\tau$ for any $\tau$ with $\sgn(\tau)=-1$. Then
  $$
  \begin{aligned}
  C_\sigma&=\{h\sigma h^{-1}\mid h\in A_n\}\cup\{h\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}h^{-1}\mid h\tau\in A_n\tau\}\\
  &=(\text{conj. class of }\sigma\text{ in }A_n)\cup(\text{conj. class of }\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}\text{ in }A_n).
  \end{aligned}
  $$
  These 2 conjugacy classes are either equal or disjoint; they are equal iff $\sigma$ is in the latter conjugacy class, i.e. there exists $g=h\tau$ (odd) such that $g\sigma g^{-1}=\sigma$, i.e. $g\sigma=\sigma g$.
:::

In other terms: for $\sigma\in C$, let $Z(\sigma)=\{\tau\in S_n\mid \tau\sigma\tau^{-1}=\sigma\}$ be the **centralizer**. If $Z(\sigma)\subset A_n$, then the conjugates of $\sigma$ by odd permutations are different from the conjugates by even permutations, and they form two conjugacy classes in $A_n$; if $Z(\sigma)\not\subset A_n$, then all conjugates of $\sigma$ in $S_n$ are conjugates by elements of $A_n$.

:::example
$n=5$:
$$
A_5=\underset{1}{\{\id\}}\;\cup\;\underset{15}{\{(i\,j)(k\,\ell)\}}\;\cup\;\underset{20}{\{3\text{-cycles}\}}\;\cup\;\underset{24}{\{5\text{-cycles}\}}.
$$
The 3-cycles still form a single conjugacy class in $A_5$ (because $(4\,5)\in Z((1\,2\,3))$); so do the $(i\,j)(k\,\ell)$’s (because $(i\,j)\in Z((i\,j)(k\,\ell))$). But the 5-cycles split into 2 conjugacy classes in $A_5$. So the class equation of $A_5$ is
$$
60=1+15+20+12+12.
$$
:::

More generally:

:::proposition
For $\sigma\in A_n$, $C_\sigma=\{g\sigma g^{-1}\mid g\in S_n\}$ splits into 2 conjugacy classes in $A_n$ iff the cycle lengths of $\sigma$ are all odd and distinct.
:::

:::proof
- $\sigma$ commutes with the cycles in its own cycle decomposition. So any even-length cycle in $\sigma$ gives an odd permutation in $Z(\sigma)$, and then $C_\sigma$ doesn’t split.
- If two odd cycles $(a_1\,\dots\,a_k)$ and $(b_1\,\dots\,b_k)$ of the same length appear in the cycle decomposition of $\sigma$, then $(a_1\,b_1)(a_2\,b_2)\cdots(a_k\,b_k)\in Z(\sigma)$ is odd. (This includes the case $k=1$! We can’t have 2 fixed points.)
- If the cycle lengths are all distinct, then an element of $Z(\sigma)$ must preserve each of the corresponding subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$; now, on a $j$-element subset,
  $$
  Z((1\,2\,\dots\,j))=\{\text{cyclic subgroup of }S_j\text{ generated by }(1\,2\,\dots\,j)\}\subset A_j \quad(j\text{ odd}).
  $$
  So $Z(\sigma)\subset A_n$.
:::

## Simplicity of $A_n$

Now: the class equation of $A_5$ is $60=1+15+20+12+12$. We can now look for normal subgroups of $A_5$. A normal subgroup is a union of conjugacy classes including $\{\id\}$, and its order divides 60; we can’t reach a divisor of 60 in any nontrivial way as such a union, except by taking all of them. Hence:

:::proposition
$A_5$ is **simple**, i.e. its only normal subgroups are $\{\id\}$ and itself.
:::

:::theorem
$A_n$ is simple for all $n\ge 5$.
:::

:::corollary
The only normal subgroups of $S_n$ ($n\ge5$) are $\{\id\}$, $A_n$, and $S_n$.
:::

:::proof
$H\subset S_n$ normal subgroup $\Rightarrow$ $H\cap A_n$ is a normal subgroup of $S_n$, hence of $A_n$, hence it is $\{1\}$ or $A_n$. So either $A_n\subset H$ (then $|S_n/H|\le 2$, so $H=A_n$ or $S_n$), or $H\cap A_n=\{1\}$. In the latter case, there can’t be 2 distinct odd permutations in $H$ ($h_1^{-1}h_2$ would be in $H\cap A_n$), so $|H|\le 2$; but a subgroup with $|H|=2$ can’t be normal.
:::

Proof of the theorem? $A_5$ we’ve just seen; $A_6$ is a similar argument using the class equation (on HW 9)! However, the result is false for $A_4$ ($\{\id\}\cup\{(i\,j)(k\,\ell)\}\subset A_4$ is normal). The general case relies on:

:::lemma
$A_n$ is generated by 3-cycles.
:::

:::proof
Induction on $n$: this is true for $A_3=\{\id,\ 3\text{-cycles}\}\subset S_3$.

Now assume $A_{n-1}$ is generated by 3-cycles. Let $\sigma\in A_n$. If $\sigma(n)=n$, then it belongs to the subgroup $\{\tau\in A_n\mid \tau(n)=n\}\simeq A_{n-1}$, so it’s a product of 3-cycles by the induction hypothesis. Else, let $i=\sigma(n)$ and $j=$ any element distinct from $i$ and $n$; then $\tau=(j\,i\,n)\sigma\in A_n$ and $\tau(n)=n$, so by the induction hypothesis $\tau=\prod(3\text{-cycles})$, and so is $\sigma=(i\,j\,n)\tau$.
:::

Moreover: for $n\ge5$, the 3-cycles form a single conjugacy class in $A_n$, since $(j_1\,j_2\,j_3)$ and $(k_1\,k_2\,k_3)$ are conjugate by any permutation $j_i\mapsto k_i$, and some of these are in $A_n$. So: to prove that a normal subgroup $H\subset A_n$, $H\ne\{e\}$, is all of $A_n$, it suffices to show that it contains a 3-cycle.

:::proof Proof of the theorem
Let $H\subset A_n$, $H\ne\{e\}$, be a normal subgroup. As just noted, it’s enough to show that $H$ contains a 3-cycle (hence all 3-cycles by conjugation, hence $H=A_n$).

Let $\sigma\in H$, $\sigma\ne e$. Replacing $\sigma$ by some power of $\sigma$, we can assume that it has prime order: let $m=\operatorname{order}(\sigma)$ and $p$ a prime with $p\mid m$; then $\sigma^{m/p}\in H$ has order $p$. Since the order of $\sigma$ is the lcm of its cycle lengths, this implies $\sigma$ is a product of disjoint $p$-cycles. We now look at cases depending on $p$.

1. **If $p\ge5$:** $\sigma=(i_1\,\dots\,i_p)\tau$, where $\tau$ fixes $i_1,\dots,i_p$ and permutes the remaining elements. Let $g=(i_4\,i_3\,i_2)$; then $H$ normal $\Rightarrow$ $g\sigma g^{-1}$ and $g\sigma g^{-1}\sigma^{-1}\in H$. Now
   $$
   g\sigma g^{-1}\sigma^{-1}=(i_4\,i_3\,i_2)\circ\big[(i_1\,i_2\,i_3\,i_4\,i_5\,\dots\,i_p)\tau\big]\circ(i_2\,i_3\,i_4)\circ\big(\tau^{-1}(i_p\,\dots\,i_5\,i_4\,i_3\,i_2\,i_1)\big)
   $$
   takes
   $$
   \begin{array}{ccccccccc}
   & \sigma^{-1} & & g^{-1} & & \sigma & & g & \\
   i_1&\mapsto& i_p&\mapsto& i_p&\mapsto& i_1&\mapsto& i_1\\
   i_2&\mapsto& i_1&\mapsto& i_1&\mapsto& i_2&\mapsto& i_4\\
   i_3&\mapsto& i_2&\mapsto& i_3&\mapsto& i_4&\mapsto& i_3\\
   i_4&\mapsto& i_3&\mapsto& i_4&\mapsto& i_5&\mapsto& i_5\\
   i_5&\mapsto& i_4&\mapsto& i_2&\mapsto& i_3&\mapsto& i_2
   \end{array}
   $$
   so $g\sigma g^{-1}\sigma^{-1}=(i_2\,i_4\,i_5)$, and $H$ contains a 3-cycle.
2. **$p=3$:** if $\sigma$ is a 3-cycle we’re done. Else it is a product of at least two disjoint 3-cycles: write $\sigma=(i_1\,i_2\,i_3)(i_4\,i_5\,i_6)\tau$ and let $g=(i_4\,i_3\,i_2)$. We find $g\sigma g^{-1}\sigma^{-1}=(i_1\,i_5\,i_2\,i_4\,i_3)$, a 5-cycle in $H$; this reduces to the previous case. ✓
3. **$p=2$, and $\sigma$ is a product of only 2 transpositions** (a single $(i\,j)\notin A_n$!): $\sigma=(i_1\,i_2)(i_3\,i_4)$. Let $i_5\notin\{i_1,\dots,i_4\}$ and $g=(i_5\,i_3\,i_1)$. Then $g\sigma g^{-1}\sigma^{-1}=(i_1\,i_5\,i_2\,i_4\,i_3)\in H$; back to the first case.
4. **$p=2$, and $\sigma$ is a product of at least 3 transpositions** (in fact $\ge4$): $\sigma=(i_1\,i_2)(i_3\,i_4)(i_5\,i_6)\tau$. Again let $g=(i_5\,i_3\,i_1)$; then $g\sigma g^{-1}\sigma^{-1}=(i_1\,i_5\,i_3)(i_2\,i_4\,i_6)\in H$ has order 3, which reduces to case 2.
:::

## The Sylow theorems

Our next topic, still very much related to understanding finite groups, is the **Sylow theorems**.

:::outline Notes not reached in this lecture, covered at the start of {{LINK:L26}}
If $|G|=n$ and $k\mid n$, then in general there is no reason for $G$ to contain an element of order $k$, or even a subgroup of order $k$: the “converse to Lagrange’s theorem” fails.

:::example
$A_4$ (resp. $A_5$) has no subgroup of order 6 (resp. 30): such a subgroup would be normal.
:::

The first Sylow theorem says: if $|G|=p^\ell m$, $p$ prime, $p\nmid m$, then there exist subgroups of order $p^\ell$.

Fix a prime $p$ (which divides $|G|$) and write $|G|=p^e m$, $p\nmid m$.

:::definition
A subgroup $H\subset G$ of order $|H|=p^e$ is called a **Sylow $p$-subgroup** of $G$.
:::

:::theorem Sylow theorems (Sylow, 1872)
1. For every prime $p$, a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$ exists.
2. All Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugates of each other: if $H,H'\subset G$ are Sylow $p$-subgroups, then there exists $g\in G$ such that $H'=gHg^{-1}$. Moreover, any subgroup $K\subset G$ with $|K|$ a power of $p$ is contained in a Sylow $p$-subgroup.
3. Let $s_p$ be the number of Sylow $p$-subgroups of $G$. Then $s_p\equiv1\bmod p$, and $s_p\mid |G|$ (or equivalently, $s_p\mid m=|G|/p^e$).
:::

:::example Classify groups of order 15
:::solution
If $|G|=15$, then there exist Sylow subgroups $H,K\subset G$ with $|H|=3$, $|K|=5$. The numbers of such Sylow subgroups:
$$
\begin{cases}
s_3\mid 5\ \text{and}\ s_3\equiv 1\bmod 3 &\Rightarrow\ s_3=1,\\
s_5\mid 3\ \text{and}\ s_5\equiv 1\bmod 5 &\Rightarrow\ s_5=1.
\end{cases}
$$
This implies $H$ and $K$ are normal! (Since their conjugates $gHg^{-1}$, $gKg^{-1}$ are also Sylow subgroups, but $H$ and $K$ are the unique such.) Using a criterion for direct products coming up next time, this implies
$$
G\simeq H\times K\simeq \ZZ/3\times\ZZ/5\simeq\ZZ/15.
$$
Every group of order 15 is cyclic!
:::
:::

**Digression: extensions and semidirect products**

If $N\subset G$ is a normal subgroup, then we have an exact sequence
$$
1\to N\to G\xrightarrow{\ p\ }H\to 1,\qquad\text{where } H\simeq G/N.
$$
This does *not* imply that $G\simeq N\times H$, or even that $G$ contains a subgroup isomorphic to $H$!

:::example
$\ZZ p\subset\ZZ$ is a subgroup, $0\to\ZZ p\to\ZZ\to\ZZ/p\to0$, but $\ZZ$ has no subgroup $\simeq\ZZ/p$.
:::

If $H$ can in fact be identified with a subgroup of $G$ containing one element of each right coset of $N$, then every element of $G$ is uniquely $nh$ for some $n\in N$, $h\in H$. Yet $G$ need not be $\simeq N\times H$:
$$
(n_1h_1)(n_2h_2)=\underbrace{(n_1\,h_1n_2h_1^{-1})}_{\in N\ (N\text{ normal})}\,\underbrace{(h_1h_2)}_{\in H}\qquad\big(\ne(n_1n_2)(h_1h_2)\text{ in general}\big),
$$
i.e. the bijection $N\times H\to G$, $(n,h)\mapsto nh$, isn’t a homomorphism. We’ll study this further next time.
:::
