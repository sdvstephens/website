## Recall

:::theorem
Let $V$ be any representation of a finite group $G$ (over $\CC$, or over a field $k$ of characteristic $0$), and suppose $W \subset V$ is an invariant subspace (i.e., a subrepresentation). Then there exists another invariant subspace $U \subset V$ such that $V = U \oplus W$ (as a direct sum of representations).
:::

:::corollary
Any finite-dimensional representation of a finite group decomposes into a direct sum of irreducibles.
:::

**Goal:** given $G$, find its irreducible representations, and describe how the others decompose into irreducibles.

:::lemma Schur’s Lemma
- If $V, W$ are irreducible representations of $G$, and $\varphi: V \to W$ is any homomorphism of representations, then either $\varphi = 0$ or $\varphi$ is an isomorphism.
- Over $k = \CC$: if $V$ is irreducible and $\varphi: V \to V$ is a homomorphism of representations, then $\varphi$ is a multiple of the identity.
:::

:::proof
- Given $\varphi: V \to W$, $\ker(\varphi)$ is an invariant subspace of $V$, i.e. a subrepresentation. Since $V$ is irreducible, either $\ker(\varphi) = 0$ ($\varphi$ injective) or $\ker(\varphi) = V$ ($\varphi = 0$). Similarly, $\im(\varphi) \subset W$ is invariant, hence either zero ($\varphi = 0$) or $W$ ($\varphi$ surjective). Hence either $\varphi = 0$ or $\varphi$ is an isomorphism.
- Over $k = \CC$, any $\varphi: V \to V$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda$. Then $\varphi - \lambda I : V \to V$ is also equivariant and has nonzero kernel, hence $\varphi - \lambda I = 0$ by the above. Thus $\varphi = \lambda I$.
:::

:::example
Let $V$ be an irreducible representation of $G$, and $h \in Z(G)$, the center of $G$ ($h$ commutes with every $g \in G$). Then the action of $h: V \to V$ satisfies $h(gv) = g\,h(v)$ for all $g \in G$, so $h$ is equivariant, i.e.
$$h \in \Hom_G(V,V) = \CC \cdot \id$$
by Schur’s lemma. So $h$ acts by a multiple of $\id$.

In particular, if $G$ is abelian and $V$ is irreducible, then every element of $G$ acts by a multiple of $\id$. This gives another proof that irreducible representations of finite abelian groups are $1$-dimensional.
:::

## Representations of $S_3$

Next we look at the simplest nonabelian group, $S_3$ ($= \mathfrak{S}_3$ in Fulton–Harris).

- We know the **trivial representation** $U \simeq \CC$ (every $\sigma \in S_3$ acts by $\id$).
- There’s another $1$-dimensional representation $U' \simeq \CC$, coming from the other element of $\Hom(S_3, \CC^*)$: the **alternating representation** (also called the **sign representation**), where $\sigma \in S_3$ acts by $(-1)^\sigma$.
- We also have the **permutation representation** $\simeq \CC^3$ with basis $e_1, e_2, e_3$, on which $S_3$ acts by permutation matrices: $\sigma$ maps $e_i \mapsto e_{\sigma(i)}$.

The permutation representation has an invariant subspace, namely $\operatorname{span}(e_1+e_2+e_3)$, and we easily find a complementary subrepresentation, namely
$$V = \{(z_1,z_2,z_3) \in \CC^3 \mid z_1+z_2+z_3 = 0\}.$$
This is called the **standard representation** of $S_3$; $\dim V = 2$, and it is irreducible.

:::remark
Similarly for $S_n$: the two $1$-dimensional representations are the trivial representation $U = \CC$ and the alternating representation $U' = \CC$ with $\sigma$ acting by $(-1)^\sigma$; and the permutation representation $\CC^n$, with $\sigma$ acting by $e_i \mapsto e_{\sigma(i)}$, has an invariant subspace $\operatorname{span}(e_1 + \dots + e_n) \simeq U$, with complementary subrepresentation
$$V = \Big\{(z_1,\dots,z_n) \in \CC^n \;\Big|\; \textstyle\sum z_i = 0\Big\}.$$
It turns out $V$ is irreducible: it is the **standard representation** of $S_n$, with $\dim V = n-1$.
:::

What is specific to $S_3$ is that this is the whole story (over $\CC$). ($S_n$ has more irreducible representations; in fact the number of irreducible representations of $S_n$ is $p(n)$, the number of partitions of $n$…)

:::proposition
$U$, $U'$ and $V$ are the only irreducible representations of $S_3$ (over $\CC$). Hence any representation of $S_3$ is isomorphic to a direct sum $U^{\oplus a} \oplus U'^{\oplus b} \oplus V^{\oplus c}$ for some (unique) $a, b, c \in \NN$.
:::

:::proof
Let $W$ be any (finite-dimensional, over $\CC$) representation of $S_3$. Restrict first to the abelian subgroup $A_3 \simeq \ZZ/3 \subset S_3$: let $\tau \in S_3$ be any $3$-cycle, and $\sigma \in S_3$ any transposition. Then
$$\tau^3 = \sigma^2 = \id, \qquad \sigma^{-1}\tau\sigma = \tau^2.$$
Restricting the representation to the subgroup generated by $\tau$ ($\simeq \ZZ/3$), $W$ has a basis of eigenvectors $(v_j)$, where $\tau(v_j) = \lambda_j v_j$ with $\lambda_j = e^{2\pi i k_j/3}$ a root of unity. Now let’s see how $\sigma$ acts.

If $v \in W$ is an eigenvector for $\tau$, $\tau(v) = \lambda v$, then
$$\tau(\sigma v) = \sigma(\tau^2 v) = \lambda^2 \sigma(v).$$
So $\sigma$ maps the $\lambda$-eigenspace of $\tau$ to its $\lambda^2$-eigenspace.

(Remark: for every eigenvector $v$ of $\tau$, $\operatorname{span}(v, \sigma v)$ is an invariant subspace, since both generators $\sigma$ and $\tau$ preserve it. So now we know irreducible representations have $\dim \le 2$.)

Let’s now specialize to the case $W$ irreducible, and choose $v \in W$ an eigenvector of $\tau$.

*Case $\lambda = 1$:* $\tau(v) = v$, and by the above, $\tau(\sigma(v)) = \sigma(v)$. If $\sigma(v)$ is linearly independent of $v$, then $w = v + \sigma(v) \neq 0$ satisfies $\sigma(w) = \sigma(v) + \sigma^2(v) = w$ and $\tau(w) = w$, so we get an invariant subspace (a trivial subrepresentation) $\operatorname{span}(w) \simeq U$. This contradicts irreducibility. So $\sigma(v)$ is a scalar multiple of $v$; since $\sigma^2 = \id$, $\sigma(v) = \pm v$. In both cases $\operatorname{span}(v)$ is invariant, and it is
$$\simeq \begin{cases} U & \text{if } \sigma(v) = v,\ \tau(v) = v, \\ U' & \text{if } \sigma(v) = -v,\ \tau(v) = v. \end{cases}$$
If $W$ is irreducible, this is all of $W$.

*Case $\lambda = e^{\pm 2\pi i/3}$:* then $\tau(v) = \lambda v$ and $\tau(\sigma(v)) = \lambda^2 \sigma(v)$ by the above. Since $\lambda \neq \lambda^2$, these two eigenvectors of $\tau$ are linearly independent; $\operatorname{span}(v, \sigma(v))$ is an invariant subspace, hence by irreducibility it equals $W$. We check that $W \simeq V$, the standard representation, by mapping $v$ to the $\lambda$-eigenvector of $\tau$ in the standard representation (i.e. $\{v, \sigma(v)\}$ map to $\{(1,\lambda^2,\lambda), (1,\lambda,\lambda^2)\} \subset V \subset \CC^3$).
:::

## Finding the multiplicities

Given a representation of $S_3$, $W \simeq U^{\oplus a} \oplus U'^{\oplus b} \oplus V^{\oplus c}$, how do we find $a, b, c$?

**Answer:** Look at the eigenvalues of $\tau$: the $1$-eigenspace of $\tau$ is $U^{\oplus a} \oplus U'^{\oplus b}$, so
$$a + b = \dim \ker(\tau - 1),$$
whereas the $e^{\pm 2\pi i/3}$-eigenspaces each have dimension $c$. So the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of $\tau$ give $a+b$ and $c$.

Likewise, $\sigma$ acts by $+1$ on $U$, by $-1$ on $U'$, and by $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \sim \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ on $V$, so the eigenspaces of $\sigma$ have dimension $a + c$ for $1$ and $b + c$ for $-1$. From this we get $a$, $b$, and $c$.

:::example
Consider $V$ the standard representation of $S_3$, and $V^{\otimes 2} = V \otimes V$, also a representation (recall: $g(v \otimes w) = gv \otimes gw$). How does $V^{\otimes 2}$ decompose into irreducibles?

:::solution
Start with a basis $e_1, e_2$ of $V$ with
$$\tau e_1 = \lambda e_1, \quad \tau e_2 = \lambda^2 e_2, \quad \text{where } \lambda = e^{2\pi i/3}, \qquad \sigma e_1 = e_2, \quad \sigma e_2 = e_1.$$
Then $V \otimes V$ has a basis $e_1 \otimes e_1,\ e_1 \otimes e_2,\ e_2 \otimes e_1,\ e_2 \otimes e_2$. These are eigenvectors of $\tau$, with eigenvalues $\lambda^2,\ 1,\ 1,\ \lambda$.

Moreover, on the $1$-eigenspace $\operatorname{span}(e_1 \otimes e_2, e_2 \otimes e_1)$, $\sigma$ swaps these two, so $e_1 \otimes e_2 \pm e_2 \otimes e_1$ is an eigenvector of $\sigma$ with eigenvalue $\pm 1$. Hence
$$V \otimes V \simeq U \oplus U' \oplus V.$$

Similarly, $\operatorname{Sym}^2 V$ has basis $e_1^2,\ e_1 e_2,\ e_2^2$, on which $\tau$ acts by $\lambda^2,\ 1,\ \lambda$, so
$$\operatorname{Sym}^2(V) \simeq U \oplus V$$
(whereas $\Lambda^2 V \simeq U'$, perhaps unsurprisingly considering $\det$ vs. sign).
:::
:::

This generalizes to more complicated groups (we’ll see that eigenvalues go a long way towards classifying representations), but we need some way of organizing the information.

**Digression: symmetric polynomials**

(This is all *motivation* for the study of characters.)

**Observe:** an efficient way to store information about $n$ (complex) numbers, unordered and possibly with repetitions, is to specify the coefficients of the polynomial of which they are the roots, i.e. $\prod_{i=1}^n (x - \lambda_i)$. These coefficients are **symmetric polynomials** in $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$.

$S_n$ acts on the space of polynomials $\CC[z_1,\dots,z_n]$ by permuting the variables.

:::definition
A **symmetric polynomial** is an $f \in \CC[z_1,\dots,z_n]$ that is a fixed point of the $S_n$-action: $\sigma(f) = f$ for all $\sigma \in S_n$.
:::

:::remark
Equality of polynomials means, as usual, equality of coefficients, which over a finite field is a stronger condition than having equality as functions on $k^n$. Of course over $\CC$ there is no difference.
:::

:::definition
The **elementary symmetric polynomials** are
$$\sigma_1(z_1,\dots,z_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n z_i, \qquad \sigma_2(z_1,\dots,z_n) = \sum_{1 \le i < j \le n} z_i z_j, \qquad \dots, \qquad \sigma_k = \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le n} z_{i_1} \cdots z_{i_k}, \qquad \dots, \qquad \sigma_n = \prod_{i=1}^n z_i.$$
:::

**Check:** the coefficient of $x^{n-k}$ in $\prod_{i=1}^n (x - z_i)$ is, up to the sign $(-1)^k$, $\sigma_k(z_1,\dots,z_n)$.

**Hence:** the fundamental theorem of algebra gives a bijection
$$\begin{aligned}
\{\text{unordered } n\text{-tuples of complex numbers, repetitions allowed}\} &\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } \CC^n \ \ (\text{ordered tuples}) \\
[z_1,\dots,z_n] &\longmapsto (\sigma_1(z_i), \dots, \sigma_n(z_i)),
\end{aligned}$$
with inverse
$$(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n) \longmapsto [\text{the roots of } x^n - \sigma_1 x^{n-1} + \dots + (-1)^n \sigma_n].$$
In other terms: $[z_1,\dots,z_n] \longleftrightarrow$ the coefficients of the polynomial $\prod (x - z_i)$.

:::theorem
The subring of symmetric polynomials in $\CC[z_1,\dots,z_n]$, i.e. $\CC[z_1,\dots,z_n]^{S_n}$, is isomorphic to the polynomial algebra in $n$ variables $\CC[\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n]$. I.e., every symmetric polynomial is uniquely a polynomial expression in the elementary symmetric polynomials.
:::

We won’t prove this, but to see why this works, look at the case $n = 2$. The vector space of symmetric polynomials has basis

$$\begin{aligned}
1 &= 1 & \qquad z_1^3 + z_2^3 &= \sigma_1^3 - 3z_1^2 z_2 - 3 z_1 z_2^2 = \sigma_1^3 - 3\sigma_1\sigma_2 \\
z_1 + z_2 &= \sigma_1 & z_1^2 z_2 + z_1 z_2^2 &= \sigma_1 \sigma_2 \\
z_1^2 + z_2^2 &= (z_1+z_2)^2 - 2z_1 z_2 = \sigma_1^2 - 2\sigma_2 & &\ \ \vdots \\
z_1 z_2 &= \sigma_2 & &
\end{aligned}$$

**Observe:** any symmetric polynomial in $2$ variables can be written as
$$\begin{aligned}
p(z_1,z_2) &= \sum a_k (z_1^k + z_2^k) + z_1 z_2\, q(z_1,z_2) \\
&= \sum a_k (z_1 + z_2)^k + z_1 z_2\, q'(z_1,z_2) \\
&= \sum a_k \sigma_1^k + \sigma_2 \cdot q'
\end{aligned}$$
and we work by induction on degree.
:::
