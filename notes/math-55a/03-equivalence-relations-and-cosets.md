## Last time

- A **subgroup** $H$ of a group $G$ is a (nonempty) subset $H \subset G$ which is closed under composition ($a, b \in H \Rightarrow ab \in H$) and inversion ($a \in H \Rightarrow a^{-1} \in H$).
- Given two groups $G$ and $H$, a map $\varphi : G \to H$ is a **homomorphism** if $\forall a, b \in G$, $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$.

## Kernel and image

:::definition Kernel
The **kernel** of a group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$ is
$$
\ker(\varphi) = \{ a \in G \mid \varphi(a) = e_H \}.
$$
:::

:::proposition
- $\ker(\varphi)$ is a subgroup of $G$ (check that it contains $e_G$, products, inverses).
- $\varphi$ is injective iff $\ker(\varphi) = \{e_G\}$ (using: $\varphi(a) = \varphi(b) \Leftrightarrow a^{-1}b \in \ker\varphi$).
:::

:::definition Image
The **image** of a group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$ is
$$
\operatorname{Im}(\varphi) = \varphi(G) = \{ b \in H \mid \exists a \in G \text{ such that } \varphi(a) = b \}.
$$
This is a subgroup of $H$. $\varphi$ is surjective iff $\operatorname{Im}(\varphi) = H$.
:::

:::remark
If $\varphi$ is injective, then $G$ is isomorphic to the subgroup $\operatorname{Im}(\varphi) \subset H$. (The isomorphism is given by the map $G \to \operatorname{Im}(\varphi)$, $a \mapsto \varphi(a)$.)
:::

## Subgroups of $\ZZ$

Given $a \in \ZZ_{>0}$, $\ZZ a = \{ na \mid n \in \ZZ \} \subset \ZZ$ is a subgroup.

:::proposition
All nontrivial subgroups of $(\ZZ, +)$ are of this form.
:::

:::proof
This follows from the Euclidean algorithm. Given a nontrivial subgroup $\{0\} \neq H \subset \ZZ$, there exists $a \in H$ such that $a > 0$. Let $a_0$ be the smallest positive element of $H$. Given any $b \in H$, $b = qa_0 + r$ for some $q \in \ZZ$ and $0 \le r < a_0$ (the remainder). Since $b \in H$ and $qa_0 \in H$, $r \in H$. Since $r < a_0$, by definition of $a_0$, $r$ must be zero. Hence $b \in \ZZ a_0$; so $H \subset \ZZ a_0$, and conversely $\ZZ a_0 \subset H$, so $H = \ZZ a_0$. $\square$
:::

So every subgroup of $\ZZ$ is **generated** by a single element $a_0$, in the following sense.

:::question
Given a (nonempty) subset $S \subset G$, what is the smallest subgroup of $G$ which contains $S$? This is denoted $\langle S \rangle$ and called the **subgroup generated by $S$**.
:::

**Answer:** look at all subgroups of $G$ which contain $S$ (there’s at least $G$ itself!) and take their intersection:
$$
\langle S \rangle = \bigcap_{\substack{S \subset H \subset G \\ \text{subgroup}}} H.
$$

**More useful answer:** $\langle S \rangle$ must contain all products of elements of $S$ and their inverses, and these form a subgroup of $G$, so
$$
\langle S \rangle = \{ a_1 \cdots a_k \mid a_i \in S \cup S^{-1}\ \forall\, 1 \le i \le k \}.
$$

:::definition
A group is **cyclic** if it is generated by a single element.
:::

(Examples: $\ZZ$, $\ZZ/n$. These are in fact the only cyclic groups up to isomorphism.)

:::example
Let $a \in G$ be any element in a group $G$. Then the map $\varphi : \ZZ \to G$, $n \mapsto a^n$ is a homomorphism, with image $\langle a \rangle$, the cyclic subgroup generated by $a$.
:::

:::definition
The **order** of $a \in G$ is the smallest positive $k$ such that $a^k = e$, if it exists. Else, say $a$ has **infinite order**.
:::

:::note
Do not confuse the order of $a \in G$ with the order of $G$ ($= |G|$). Though, $\operatorname{order}(a) = |\langle a \rangle|$.
:::

If $a$ has infinite order, then the powers of $a$ are all distinct, $\varphi : n \mapsto a^n$ is injective, and $\langle a \rangle$ is isomorphic to $\ZZ$. If $a$ has finite order $k$, then $\ker(\varphi) = \ZZ k$, and $\langle a \rangle = \{ a^n \mid n = 0, \dots, k-1 \}$ is isomorphic to $\ZZ/k$. (This completes the classification of cyclic groups, by the way.)

:::example
$$
\ZZ/6 \xrightarrow{\sim} \ZZ/2 \times \ZZ/3, \qquad a \mapsto (a \bmod 2,\ a \bmod 3)
$$
(observe: $(1,1) \in \ZZ/2 \times \ZZ/3$ has order 6, so it generates).

Similarly, $\gcd(m, n) = 1 \Rightarrow \ZZ/m \times \ZZ/n \cong \ZZ/mn$. But $\ZZ/2 \times \ZZ/2 \not\cong \ZZ/4$: $x + x = 0$ for all $x$, versus $1 + 1 \neq 0$.
:::

## Classifying finite groups

An important question in group theory is the **classification of finite groups** up to isomorphism. This becomes increasingly difficult as $|G|$ increases. The beginning:

- every group of order 2 is isomorphic to $\ZZ/2$ (by writing the table of the composition law…);
- similarly, every group of order 3 is $\cong \ZZ/3$;
- for order 4, we know $\ZZ/4$ and $\ZZ/2 \times \ZZ/2$. (These are different: every nonzero element of $\ZZ/2 \times \ZZ/2$ has order 2, while $\ZZ/4$ has an element of order 4.) In fact these are the only two groups of order 4 up to isomorphism.

(The classification of finite *simple* groups was completed in the 1980s, taking thousands of pages. We’ll learn some of the key tools and concepts in the class, but certainly won’t tackle the complete classification!)

## Aside: equivalence relations and partitions

(Cf. Artin §2.7; also Halmos, *Naive Set Theory*.)

An equivalence relation on a set $S$ is a way to declare certain elements equivalent to each other (“$a \sim b$”), yielding a smaller set of **equivalence classes** (“$S/\sim$”, the **quotient** of $S$ by $\sim$).

:::definition
An **equivalence relation** on a set $S$ is a **binary relation** (i.e. a subset of $S \times S$; write $a \sim b$ iff $(a, b)$ is in this subset) which is

1. **reflexive**: $\forall a \in S$, $a \sim a$;
2. **symmetric**: $\forall a, b \in S$, $a \sim b \Rightarrow b \sim a$;
3. **transitive**: $\forall a, b, c \in S$, if $a \sim b$ and $b \sim c$ then $a \sim c$.
:::

- The **equivalence class** of $a \in S$ is $\{ a' \in S \mid a \sim a' \}$ (sometimes denoted $[a]$). (By transitivity, the elements of $[a]$ are all equivalent to each other.)
- The equivalence classes form a **partition** of $S$, i.e. they are mutually disjoint subsets of $S$ whose union is $S$.
- The **quotient** of $S$ by $\sim$ is the set of equivalence classes: $S/\sim\ = \{ [a] \mid a \in S \} \subset \mathcal{P}(S)$. This comes with a *surjective* map
$$
S \to S/\sim, \qquad a \mapsto [a].
$$

:::example Congruence mod $n$
$S = \ZZ$; given $n \in \ZZ_{>0}$, set $a \sim b$ iff $n$ divides $b - a$. This is congruence mod $n$; check it is an equivalence relation.

There are $n$ equivalence classes:
$$
[0] = \{ \dots, -n, 0, n, 2n, \dots \} = \ZZ n, \quad [1] = \{ \dots, 1-n, 1, 1+n, 1+2n, \dots \}, \quad \dots, \quad [n-1].
$$
The quotient is naturally in bijection with $\ZZ/n$:
$$
\ZZ \twoheadrightarrow \ZZ/\sim\ \cong \ZZ/n, \qquad a \mapsto [a].
$$
(We defined $\ZZ/n$ as $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$ only to avoid the language of equivalence classes, but it makes more sense to redefine it as the quotient set.)
:::

:::example Fibres of a surjection
Given a surjective $f : S \to T$, set $a \sim b$ iff $f(a) = f(b)$. This is an equivalence relation; the partition into equivalence classes is
$$
S = \bigsqcup_{t \in T} f^{-1}(t), \qquad f^{-1}(t) = \{ a \in S \mid f(a) = t \}.
$$
Note that $S/\sim$ is in bijection with $T$ via $[a] \mapsto f(a)$.
:::

Using this:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{equivalence relation on } S &\iff \text{partition of } S \text{ into disjoint subsets (equivalence classes)} \\
&\iff \text{surjective map from } S \text{ to another set } T \quad (T \cong S/\sim).
\end{aligned}
$$

More generally, given a map $f : S \to T$ and an equivalence relation $\sim$ on $S$, when does $f$ induce a map $\bar f : S/\sim\ \to T$ defined by $[a] \mapsto f(a)$?

Necessary and sufficient: $f$ is **compatible** with $\sim$, in the sense that $a \sim b \Rightarrow f(a) = f(b)$.

This is exactly what ensures that, given an equivalence class in $S/\sim$, its proposed image under $\bar f$ doesn’t depend on the choice of element in it! We can then factor
$$
f = \bar f \circ q : S \xrightarrow{q} S/\sim\ \xrightarrow{\bar f} T.
$$

Similarly, the reason that the operation $+ : \ZZ \times \ZZ \to \ZZ$ induces an operation on $\ZZ/\sim\ \cong \ZZ/n$ is that it is compatible with $\sim$:
$$
a \sim a',\ b \sim b' \Rightarrow a + b \sim a' + b'
$$
($n$ divides $a' - a$ and $b' - b$ $\Rightarrow$ $n$ divides $(a' + b') - (a + b)$). So we can define $[a] + [b] = [a + b]$.

**Back to groups: cosets**

Assume we have a surjective group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$. Recall the **kernel** $K = \ker(\varphi) = \{ a \in G \mid \varphi(a) = e_H \}$ is a subgroup of $G$.

Let’s look at the partition of $G$ induced by $\varphi$:
$$
\varphi(a) = \varphi(b) \iff \varphi(a)^{-1}\varphi(b) = e_H \iff a^{-1}b \in K \iff b \in aK = \{ ak \mid k \in K \}
$$
(for the last step, let $k = a^{-1}b$; then $b = ak$).

:::definition Cosets
Given *any* subgroup $K$ of a group $G$:

- $aK = \{ ak \mid k \in K \} \subset G$ is called the **(left) coset** of $K \subset G$ containing $a$.
- The relation $a \sim b \Leftrightarrow a^{-1}b \in K$ is an equivalence relation on $G$, whose equivalence classes are the left cosets.
- The quotient (the set of left cosets) is denoted by $G/K$. We have a partition
$$
G = \bigsqcup_{aK \in G/K} aK.
$$
:::

:::proof
- $a^{-1}a = e \in K$, so $a \sim a$ for all $a \in G$.
- If $a \sim b$, then $a^{-1}b \in K$, hence $(a^{-1}b)^{-1} = b^{-1}a \in K$, hence $b \sim a$.
- If $a \sim b$ and $b \sim c$, then $a^{-1}b \in K$ and $b^{-1}c \in K$, so $(a^{-1}b)(b^{-1}c) \in K$, i.e. $a \sim c$.

Also, $b \in aK \iff \exists k \in K$ such that $b = ak$ $\iff \exists k \in K$ such that $a^{-1}b = k$ $\iff a^{-1}b \in K \iff a \sim b$. $\square$
:::

:::example
$\varphi : \ZZ \twoheadrightarrow \ZZ/n$, $a \mapsto a \bmod n$ has kernel $\ZZ n \subset \ZZ$: the cosets are $[k] = k + \ZZ n$ ($0 \le k \le n-1$), and we have a bijection
$$
\ZZ/\ZZ n \cong \ZZ/n, \qquad [k] \mapsto k.
$$
This gives a group law on the quotient! (Addition of cosets $\Leftrightarrow$ addition mod $n$.)
:::

When a subgroup $K$ is the kernel of a homomorphism $\varphi : G \twoheadrightarrow H$, we get a bijection
$$
G/K \cong H, \qquad aK \mapsto \varphi(a)
$$
(recall $\varphi(b) = \varphi(a)$ iff $b \in aK$), and we can use this bijection to get a group structure on $G/K$, essentially $(aK) \cdot (bK) = abK$ (which corresponds via $\varphi$ to $\varphi(a)\varphi(b) = \varphi(ab)$). Then
$$
G \twoheadrightarrow G/K, \qquad a \mapsto aK
$$
is a group homomorphism.

For a general subgroup $K \subset G$, however, trying to make $G/K$ a group by setting $(aK) \cdot (bK) = abK$ might not work. The obstacle to this is: assume $a \sim a'$ ($\Leftrightarrow aK = a'K \Leftrightarrow a^{-1}a' \in K$) and $b \sim b'$ ($\Leftrightarrow bK = b'K \Leftrightarrow b^{-1}b' \in K$). Does it follow that $ab \sim a'b'$ ($\Leftrightarrow abK = a'b'K$)? If not, our operation isn’t well defined.

:::example
$G = D_4$ = symmetries of the square, $H = \{e, h\}$ where $h$ = horizontal flip. Then $e \sim h$ (the coset $eH = hH = \{e, h\}$). But setting $r$ = rotation by $90^\circ$, $h \circ r$ is the reflection across one diagonal, while the coset of $e \circ r = r$ is $\{ r,\ r \circ h \}$, where $r \circ h$ is the reflection across the other diagonal.

![Four squares showing the symmetries h (flip across the vertical axis), r (rotation by 90 degrees), h∘r (reflection across the diagonal from bottom left to top right) and r∘h (reflection across the other diagonal)](/notes/math-55a/figures/e1-square-symmetries.svg)

So $h \circ r \not\sim e \circ r$, even though $h \sim e$ (and $r \sim r$).
:::
:::
