*Last time:* cosets $aK$; normal subgroups ($aK = Ka$, i.e. $aKa^{-1} = K$); the quotient group $G/K$, defined **if** $K$ is normal. Today: miscellaneous things about groups, then on to rings and fields.

## Simple groups and short exact sequences

:::definition Simple group
Say a group $G$ is **simple** if it has no normal subgroups other than $G$ and $\{e\}$.
:::

We use normal subgroups $K \subset G$ to view $G$ as built from hopefully simpler groups $K$ and $G/K$. Simple groups are then the basic building blocks.

:::definition Short exact sequence
A **short exact sequence** of groups is a diagram

$$\{e\} \longrightarrow K \xrightarrow{\ \psi\ } G \xrightarrow{\ \varphi\ } H \longrightarrow \{e\}$$

of groups mapped to each other by homomorphisms (trivial at the start and end) such that the image of each homomorphism equals the kernel of the next one. (One can also consider longer exact sequences.)
:::

In a short exact sequence:

- $\{e_K\} = \ker(\psi)$, i.e. $\psi$ is injective;
- $\operatorname{Im}(\psi) = \ker(\varphi)$ (so: $\forall g \in G$, $\varphi(g) = e_H \iff g = \psi(k)$ for some $k \in K$);
- $\operatorname{Im}(\varphi) = H$, i.e. $\varphi$ is surjective.

In other terms:

1. $K$ is isomorphic (via $\psi$) to $N = \operatorname{Im}(\psi) = \ker(\varphi) \subset G$, a normal subgroup of $G$ (kernels are normal), and
2. $G/N$ (the cosets of $N$; $G/N$ is a group because $N$ is normal) is isomorphic to $H$ via
$$G/N \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } H, \qquad aN \longmapsto \varphi(a).$$

:::example
$$0 \to \ZZ/3 \to \ZZ/6 \to \ZZ/2 \to 0, \qquad n \mapsto 2n, \quad m \mapsto m \bmod 2.$$
Here $N = \{0, 2, 4\} \subset \ZZ/6$ is a normal subgroup $\simeq \ZZ/3$, with quotient $\simeq \ZZ/2$.
:::

:::example
There exists an exact sequence
$$\{e\} \to \ZZ/3 \to S_3 \xrightarrow{\ \text{sign}\ } \ZZ/2 \to \{e\}, \qquad n \mapsto (1\,2\,3)^n.$$
($S_3$ *also* contains a normal subgroup $\simeq \ZZ/3$, namely $\{e, (1\,2\,3), (1\,3\,2)\}$, with quotient $\simeq \ZZ/2$.)

So the first and third terms of a short exact sequence *don’t* determine the second.
:::

## More about $S_n$

:::definition Cycle
A **cycle** $\sigma = (a_1\, a_2 \dots a_k) \in S_n$, where $a_1, \dots, a_k$ are distinct elements of $\{1, \dots, n\}$, is the permutation mapping $a_1 \mapsto a_2$, $a_2 \mapsto a_3$, …, $a_k \mapsto a_1$, and all other elements to themselves.
:::

:::proposition
Any permutation can be expressed as a product of disjoint cycles, uniquely up to reordering the factors. (Disjoint cycles commute, so the order doesn’t matter.)
:::

:::example
$$\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\ \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow & \downarrow \\ 3 & 5 & 6 & 4 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = (1\,3\,6)(2\,5).$$
The cycle $(1\,3\,6)$ lists the successive images of $1$ under $\sigma$ until it returns to $1$; then do the same for the other elements not in the previous cycles, which gives $(2\,5)$.
:::

A $k$-cycle can be written as a product of $(k-1)$ **transpositions** (= 2-cycles):
$$(a_1\, a_2 \dots a_k) = (a_1\, a_2) \circ (a_2\, a_3) \circ \dots \circ (a_{k-1}\, a_k).$$

So $S_n$ is generated by the transpositions $(i\,j)$, $1 \le i < j \le n$. In fact it is generated by $(1\,2), (2\,3), \dots, (n{-}1\ n)$. (We can write $(i\,j)$ in terms of those: $(i\,j) = (i\ i{+}1) \circ (i{+}1\ j) \circ (i\ i{+}1)$, plus induction on $j - i$.) [See also: the *bubble sort* algorithm.]

:::definition Even and odd permutations; the alternating group
Permutations are **odd** or **even** depending on the length of an expression of $\sigma$ as a product of transpositions ($\iff$ the parity of $\#\{(i,j) \mid 1 \le i < j \le n,\ \sigma(i) > \sigma(j)\}$).

The even permutations form a normal subgroup $A_n$, the **alternating group** $\subset S_n$:
$$1 \to A_n \to S_n \to \ZZ/2 \to 1.$$
:::

:::note
That parity is well defined is nontrivial! One needs to show that multiplication by a transposition always changes the parity.
:::

:::theorem Fact
$A_3 \simeq \ZZ/3$, and $A_4$ has a normal subgroup $\simeq \ZZ/2 \times \ZZ/2$, but for $n \ge 5$, $A_n$ is **simple**, i.e. its only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and itself.
:::

:::remark Advertisement for Galois theory (Math 123)
This fact is used to prove that there is no general formula for solving polynomial equations of degree $\ge 5$! The quadratic formula has a $\pm\sqrt{\ }$, and the sign is there because over $\CC$ there’s not a consistent choice of $\sqrt{\ }$ of all complex numbers; the ambiguity is in $\ZZ/2 \simeq S_2$ permuting the two roots. The Cardano formula for cubics has $\sqrt[3]{\dots + \sqrt{\dots}}$ in it. The $\ZZ/2$ and $\ZZ/3$ ambiguities in choosing these roots combine to an $S_3$ permuting the roots. Similarly, the formula for roots of a degree 5 equation should have a built-in $S_5$ symmetry, but any expression involving $\sqrt[k]{\dots}$ will have symmetry group built from cyclic $\ZZ/k$’s. This can’t be $S_5$ since $A_5$ is simple.
:::

## Two constructions that measure non-commutativity

Two constructions help understand the extent of non-commutativity in a group.

:::definition Center
The **center** of $G$ is $Z(G) = \{z \in G \mid az = za\ \ \forall a \in G\}$.
:::

Since elements of the center commute with everyone, they commute with each other, so $Z(G)$ is abelian! Also, $a\,Z(G)\,a^{-1} = Z(G)$, so $Z(G)$ is a normal subgroup of $G$. $G$ is abelian iff $Z(G) = G$.

:::definition Commutator subgroup, abelianization
The **commutator subgroup** is
$$C(G) = [G,G] = \Big\{ \prod_{i=1}^k [a_i, b_i] \ \Big|\ k \in \NN,\ a_i, b_i \in G \Big\},$$
where $[a,b] := aba^{-1}b^{-1}$ (the “commutator” of $a$ and $b$; it equals $e$ iff $ab = ba$).

The quotient $G/[G,G]$ is called the **abelianization** of $G$.
:::

This is a normal subgroup because
$$g^{-1} \Big( \prod_{i=1}^k [a_i, b_i] \Big) g = \prod_{i=1}^k [g^{-1}a_i g,\ g^{-1} b_i g],$$
so $g^{-1}\, C(G)\, g = C(G)$ for all $g \in G$.

$[G,G]$ contains all commutators $[a,b]$, i.e. all commutators lie in the coset of the identity, hence all commutators in $G/[G,G]$ are equal to the identity: $xy = yx$ for all $x, y \in G/[G,G]$.

Since $[G,G]$ is generated by commutators, it is the smallest subgroup of $G$ with that property. The abelianization is the largest abelian group onto which $G$ admits a surjective homomorphism.

## Free groups and presentations

:::definition Free group
The **free group** $F_n$ on $n$ generators $a_1, \dots, a_n$: its elements are all **reduced words**
$$a_{i_1}^{m_1} \cdots a_{i_k}^{m_k},$$
where $k \ge 0$ (the empty word is $e$), $i_1, \dots, i_k \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ with $i_j \ne i_{j+1}$, and $m_1, \dots, m_k \in \ZZ \setminus \{0\}$.
:::

(Non-reduced words: reduce them by

- if $i_j = i_{j+1}$, combining $a_i^m a_i^{m'} \rightsquigarrow a_i^{m+m'}$;
- if an exponent is zero, removing $a_i^0$.

Repeat until the word is reduced.)

This is the “largest” group with $n$ generators; all others are $\simeq$ quotients of $F_n$. If $G$ is generated by $g_1, \dots, g_n \in G$, define a homomorphism
$$F_n \twoheadrightarrow G \quad \text{by} \quad \prod a_{i_j}^{m_j} \longmapsto \prod g_{i_j}^{m_j}. \qquad (\star)$$

:::definition Finitely presented group
A finitely generated group is said to be **finitely presented** if the kernel of $(\star)$ is the smallest normal subgroup of $F_n$ containing some finite subset $\{r_1, \dots, r_k\} \subset F_n$ (words in the generators), i.e. the subgroup generated by the $r_j$’s and their conjugates $x^{-1} r_j x$.

We write $G \cong \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \mid r_1, \dots, r_k \rangle$ (generators | relations); then $G \cong F_n / \langle \text{conjugates of } r_1, \dots, r_k \rangle$.
:::

:::example
$$\ZZ^n \cong \langle a_1, \dots, a_n \mid a_i a_j a_i^{-1} a_j^{-1}\ \ \forall i,j \rangle$$
(the abelianization of $F_n$!).
:::

## Rings and fields

Now we move on to rings and fields on our way to vector spaces (Artin ch. 3 / Axler ch. 1–2). Groups will return later.

:::outline Notes not reached in this lecture, covered at the start of [Lecture 6](/notes/math-55a/06-rings-fields-and-vector-spaces)
:::definition Ring
A (**commutative**) **ring** is a set $R$ with two operations $+, \times$ such that

1. $(R, +)$ is an abelian group with identity $0 \in R$;
2. $(R, \times)$ is a (commutative) semigroup with identity $1 \in R$, namely
   - $1a = a1 = a$ for all $a \in R$,
   - $a(bc) = (ab)c$ for all $a, b, c \in R$,
   - ($ab = ba$ for all $a, b \in R$, if commutative);
3. distributive law: $a(b + c) = ab + ac$ for all $a, b, c \in R$.
:::

:::definition Field
A **field** $K$ is a commutative ring such that for all $a \ne 0$ there exists $b = a^{-1}$ such that $ab = 1$, i.e. $(K \setminus \{0\}, \times)$ is an abelian *group* rather than a semigroup.
:::

:::remark
- The ring axioms imply $0a = a0 = 0$ for all $a$. ($a0 = a(0 + 0) = a0 + a0$, plus cancellation.)
- The trivial ring $R = \{0\}$ is the only case where $0 = 1$. By convention this is *not* a field.
- Most rings of interest to us are commutative. (Matrices are the main exception.)
- In a field, $ab = 0 \implies a = 0$ or $b = 0$. This is not necessarily true in a ring.
- Hence, in a field, we have the usual properties of cancellation (simplification) for both addition and multiplication.
:::

:::definition Ring / field homomorphism
A **ring/field homomorphism** is a map $\varphi : R \to S$ that respects both operations:
- $\varphi(a + b) = \varphi(a) + \varphi(b)$ (we’ve seen this implies $\varphi(0) = 0$, $\varphi(-a) = -\varphi(a)$);
- $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$;
- $\varphi(1_R) = 1_S$ (this doesn’t follow from $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)$, even for fields: consider $\varphi = 0$!).
:::

:::proposition
If $\varphi : R \to S$ is a field homomorphism, then $\varphi$ is injective.
:::

:::proof
If $a \ne 0$ then there exists $b$ such that $ab = 1_R$, so $\varphi(a)\varphi(b) = \varphi(ab) = 1_S \ne 0_S$, which implies $\varphi(a) \ne 0_S$. So $\ker(\varphi) = \{0\}$ (as an additive group homomorphism), hence $\varphi$ is injective.
:::

:::example
- $\ZZ$, $\ZZ/n$ are rings.
- $\QQ, \RR, \CC$ are fields. So is $\ZZ/p$ for $p$ prime!! This is denoted $\FF_p$ when viewed as a field.

Why $\ZZ/p$ is a field: if $k \ne 0$ in $(\ZZ/p, +)$, then its order is $p$ (it divides $p$ and is $\ne 1$), so $\{0, k, 2k, \dots, (p-1)k\} = \ZZ/p$. Hence there exists $\ell \in \{0, \dots, p-1\}$ such that $\ell k = 1 \bmod p$. This gives the inverse!
:::

**Polynomials and rational functions**

:::definition Polynomial ring
Given a field $k$, the **ring of polynomials** in one formal variable $x$ is
$$k[x] := \{a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_n x^n \mid a_i \in k,\ n \in \NN\}.$$
:::

:::remark
$x$ is a **formal** variable, i.e. not an element of anything, though we can **evaluate** a polynomial at an element of $k$ or of any field containing $k$.

So a polynomial $\iff$ a finite tuple of elements $(a_0, \dots, a_n, 0, 0, \dots)$ of $k$, with componentwise addition [but *not* componentwise multiplication! $x^i x^j = x^{i+j}$].
:::

:::definition Field of rational functions
$k[x]$ isn’t a field, but it can be turned into a field by considering fractions (just like the ring $\ZZ \rightsquigarrow$ the field $\QQ$): the **field of rational functions** is
$$k(x) = \Big\{ \frac{p}{q} \ \Big|\ p, q \in k[x],\ q \ne 0 \Big\} \Big/ \sim, \qquad \frac{p}{q} \sim \frac{p'}{q'} \text{ iff } pq' = qp'.$$
:::

(This generalizes to polynomials and rational functions in any number of variables.)

**Power series and Laurent series**

:::definition Formal power series
The ring of **formal power series** in $x$ is
$$k[[x]] = \Big\{ \sum_{i=0}^\infty a_i x^i \ \Big|\ a_i \in k \Big\}.$$
(Add and multiply just like polynomials, term by term. Check that each coefficient in $\left(\sum a_i x^i\right)\left(\sum b_j x^j\right)$ is a finite expression.)
:::

:::lemma
$\sum a_i x^i$ has a multiplicative inverse in $k[[x]]$ iff $a_0 \ne 0$.
:::

:::proof
We want $\sum_{i \ge 0} b_i x^i$ such that $\left(\sum_{i \ge 0} a_i x^i\right)\left(\sum_{i \ge 0} b_i x^i\right) = 1$. This gives
$$\begin{aligned}
a_0 b_0 &= 1 \\
a_0 b_1 + a_1 b_0 &= 0 \\
a_0 b_2 + a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_0 &= 0 \\
&\ \ \vdots
\end{aligned}$$
If $a_0 = 0$, clearly there is no solution; if $a_0 \ne 0$, we can solve inductively: $b_0 = \frac{1}{a_0}$, $b_1 = -\frac{a_1 b_0}{a_0}$, … (each step is $b_i = -(\dots)/a_0$ ✓).
:::

Since every nonzero element of $k[[x]]$ is of the form
$$a_m x^m + a_{m+1} x^{m+1} + \dots = x^m \underbrace{(a_m + a_{m+1} x + \dots)}_{\text{invertible}},$$
where $a_m$ is the first nonzero coefficient, to get a field we just need to allow $x^{-m}$:

:::definition Laurent series
The field of **Laurent series** is
$$k((x)) = \Big\{ \sum_{i=m}^\infty a_i x^i \ \Big|\ m \in \ZZ,\ a_i \in k \Big\}.$$
:::

**Adjoining roots**

Given a field $k$ and a polynomial $f \in k[x]$ (of degree $> 0$), we can evaluate $f(r)$, $r \in k$, and look for roots $r \in k$ such that $f(r) = 0$. If there are none in $k$, we can form a field $K \supset k$ in which $f$ has a root.

:::example
$k = \QQ$: $x^2 - 2$ has no roots, but we can form
$$\QQ(\sqrt 2) := \{a + b\sqrt 2 \mid a, b \in \QQ\},$$
which is a field: $\dfrac{1}{a + b\sqrt 2} = \dfrac{a - b\sqrt 2}{a^2 - 2b^2} \in \QQ(\sqrt 2)$.
:::

:::example
$k = \RR$, $x^2 + 1 \rightsquigarrow \RR(\sqrt{-1}) = \CC$, where $\sqrt{-1}$ is usually called $i$.
:::
:::
