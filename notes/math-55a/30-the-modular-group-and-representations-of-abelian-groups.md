## $SL_2(\ZZ)$ and $PSL_2(\ZZ)$

One more example:
$$SL_2(\ZZ)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\ \middle|\ a,b,c,d\in\ZZ,\ ad-bc=1\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad PSL_2(\ZZ)=SL_2(\ZZ)/\{\pm I\}.$$

:::proposition
$SL_2(\ZZ)$ is generated by $S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.
:::

:::proof
Given $M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL_2(\ZZ)$, we want to express it in terms of $S$ and $T$.

- If $c=0$, then $a,d=\pm1$, and $M$ is either $\begin{pmatrix}1&n\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=T^n$ or $\begin{pmatrix}-1&-n\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}=S^2T^n$.
- Now assume $c\neq0$, and repeatedly apply the following algorithm to modify $M$:
  - if $|a|\ge|c|$, use Euclidean division to write $a=nc+r$, $|r|<|c|$. Then
$$T^{-n}M=\begin{pmatrix}1&-n\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a-nc&b-nd\\c&d\end{pmatrix}.$$
This decreases $\max(|a|,|c|)$.
  - if $|a|<|c|$,
$$S^{-1}M=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}c&d\\-a&-b\end{pmatrix}$$
brings us back to $|a|>|c|$.

After finitely many steps we find that the product of $M$ with some word in $S$ and $T$ has $c=0$ and $|a|=1$, hence is $T^n$ or $S^2T^n$.
:::

- There is a different, geometric proof, based on the fact that $PSL_2(\ZZ)$ acts on the upper half plane $\mathbb{H}=\{z\in\CC\mid\operatorname{Im}z>0\}$ by
$$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}:\ z\mapsto\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.$$
Now $S$ acts by $z\mapsto-\frac1z$ and $T$ by $z\mapsto z+1$.

The region $\Delta=\{|z|\ge1,\ |\operatorname{Re}z|\le\frac12\}$ is a **fundamental domain** of this action, in the sense that $\Delta$ and its images under $PSL_2(\ZZ)$ exactly tile $\mathbb{H}$.

![The tiling of the upper half plane by images of the fundamental domain under PSL2(Z): the shaded region I above the unit circle between Re z = -1/2 and 1/2, the regions T^{±1}, T^{±2} beside it, the regions S, T^{±1}S, T^{±2}S below the unit circles, and smaller regions STS, ST, ST^{-1}, TST, etc. bounded by circles of radius 1/3; the real axis is marked from -2 to 2](/notes/math-55a/figures/e9-modular-tiling.svg)

Since the regions immediately adjacent to $\Delta$ are $T^{\pm1}(\Delta)$ and $S(\Delta)$, the regions immediately next to $g(\Delta)$ are $gT^{\pm1}(\Delta)$ and $gS(\Delta)$. The structure of the tiling reproduces exactly the Cayley graph of $PSL_2(\ZZ)$ with generators $S,T$, and the fact that all regions can be reached from $\Delta$ in finitely many steps is equivalent to: $S,T$ generate $(P)SL_2(\ZZ)$.

- **Other generators**: instead of $S$ and $T$, we could use
  - $S=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $R=ST=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. These have finite order! $S^4=R^6=I$.
  - $T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $T'=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=(TST)^{-1}$. These are conjugates! $T'=STS^{-1}$.
  - The images of these matrices in $PSL_2(\ZZ)=SL_2(\ZZ)/\{\pm I\}$ also generate $PSL_2(\ZZ)$.

:::theorem
$$PSL_2(\ZZ)\cong\langle S,R\mid S^2,R^3\rangle.$$
:::

:::proof
$S^2=-I$ and $R^3=-I$, so $S,R$ have orders 2 and 3 in $PSL_2(\ZZ)$. These relations $S^2=R^3=e$ reduce any word in $S^{\pm1},R^{\pm1}$ to the form
$$\cdots SR^{\pm1}SR^{\pm1}SR^{\pm1}\cdots$$
(the word can start and end with either $S$ or $R^{\pm1}$).

Mapping $F_2=\langle s,r\rangle$ to $PSL_2(\ZZ)$ by $r\mapsto R$, $s\mapsto S$ induces a surjective homomorphism
$$\langle s,r\mid s^2,r^3\rangle=F_2/\langle\text{conjugates of } s^2,r^3\rangle\surjto PSL_2(\ZZ),$$
and the kernel consists of all elements (words $\cdots sr^{\pm1}sr^{\pm1}\cdots$) such that the corresponding expression in $S$ and $R$ equals $e$ in $PSL_2(\ZZ)$, i.e. $\pm I$ in $SL_2(\ZZ)$.

Observe: $S\neq e$, $R^{\pm1}\neq e$. Assume some longer word $w$ in $S$ and $R^{\pm1}$’s (alternating between these) simplifies to $e\in PSL_2(\ZZ)$.
- If it starts and ends with $S$, we get a shorter word $w'$ by conjugating by $S$: since $S^2=e$, $Sw'S=e\iff w'=e$.
- If it starts with $R^{\pm1}$, conjugate it to get another word that doesn’t: $R^{\pm1}w'=e\iff w'R^{\pm1}=e$.

Iterating, we eventually get some word $SR^{\pm1}\cdots SR^{\pm1}=\pm I$.

But $SR=-T=-\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $SR^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. So some product of the matrices $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ equals $\pm I$.

Observe: when multiplying a matrix with entries $\ge0$ by $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ or $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, its entries remain $\ge0$, and the sum of all entries increases.

So we can’t get $\pm I$. Hence no word in $SR^{\pm1}$ simplifies to $e$ in $PSL_2(\ZZ)$, and $PSL_2(\ZZ)\cong\langle S,R\mid S^2,R^3\rangle$.
:::

- This presentation can be rewritten in terms of other generators:
$$PSL_2(\ZZ)\cong\langle S,T\mid S^2,(ST)^3\rangle\cong\langle T,T'\mid (TT')^3=e,\ TT'T=T'TT'\rangle,$$
with $S,T=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ in the first presentation and $T,T'=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$ in the second; and
$$SL_2(\ZZ)\cong\langle T,T'\mid (TT')^6=1,\ TT'T=T'TT'\rangle.$$

(And to connect two strands of our discussion: the center of the braid group $B_3=\langle s_1,s_2\mid s_1s_2s_1=s_2s_1s_2\rangle$ is generated by $\Delta^2=(s_1s_2)^3$ and is $\cong\ZZ$, so mapping $s_1\mapsto T$, $s_2\mapsto T'$ gives
$$1\to\ZZ=\langle\Delta^2\rangle\injto B_3\surjto PSL_2(\ZZ)\to1.)$$

## Representation theory

Next topic (through the end of the semester): **representation theory** ($\sim$ Artin chapter 10). Recommended text: Fulton and Harris, *Representation Theory: A First Course*, part I.

Representation theory = the study of group actions on *vector spaces*, i.e. homomorphisms $G\to GL(V)$ (for us, mostly over $k=\CC$).

Historically, groups first arose as geometric symmetries, so in the 19th century groups were mostly thought of as subgroups of $GL(n)$, rather than abstract groups! The more modern viewpoint, rather, splits this into the study of groups on their own (what we’ve studied) + how to think of an abstract group $G$ as a subgroup of $GL(n)$ (what we’ll see now).

We’ll focus on representations of finite groups, but the problem is also interesting for discrete infinite groups (e.g. $SL_2(\ZZ)$, braid groups, …), or continuous ones (Lie groups: $S^1$, $SO(3)$, …).

:::outline Notes not reached in this lecture, covered at the start of [Lecture 31](/notes/math-55a/31-complete-reducibility-and-schurs-lemma)
:::definition
A **representation** of a group $G$ is a vector space $V$ (usually finite-dimensional; we mostly consider $k=\CC$) + an action of $G$ on $V$ by linear operators, i.e. $G\times V\to V$ such that for all $g\in G$, $g:V\to V$ is a linear map.

Equivalently: a homomorphism $\rho:G\to GL(V)$, the group of invertible linear operators $V\to V$.
:::

:::definition
- A **subrepresentation** is a subspace $W\subset V$ which is invariant under $G$, i.e. $gW=W$ for all $g\in G$.
- A representation is **irreducible** if it has no nontrivial subrepresentations.
:::

:::example
If $G\cong\ZZ/n$ is a cyclic group, then a representation of $G$ is a vector space $V$ together with $\varphi=\rho(1):V\to V$ such that $\varphi^n=\id_V$.
:::

Return briefly to linear algebra:

:::lemma
Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional $\CC$-vector space and $\varphi:V\to V$ of finite order, $\varphi^n=\id$. Then $\varphi$ is diagonalizable.
:::

:::proof
This is because the minimal polynomial of $\varphi$ divides $\varphi^n-1$, hence has simple roots.

Explicitly: over $\CC$, $\varphi^n-1=0$ factors as $\prod_k(\varphi-\lambda_k)=0$, where $\lambda_k=e^{2\pi ik/n}$. So the eigenvalues of $\varphi$ are $n$-th roots of unity ($\varphi(v)=\lambda v\implies v=\varphi^n(v)=\lambda^nv$), and the generalized eigenspaces $V_{\lambda_k}=\ker(\varphi-\lambda_k)^N$ ($N>\dim V$) give $V=\bigoplus V_{\lambda_k}$, a decomposition of $V$ into invariant subspaces of $\varphi$.

Since $\prod_{j\neq k}(\varphi-\lambda_j)$ is invertible on $V_{\lambda_k}$, we have $(\varphi-\lambda_k)|_{V_{\lambda_k}}=0$, i.e. $\varphi|_{V_{\lambda_k}}=\lambda_k\,\id$. Hence $\varphi$ is diagonalizable.
:::

Returning to $G=\ZZ/n$: invariant subspaces of $\varphi=\rho(1)$ are subrepresentations, and $V$ splits into a direct sum of 1-dimensional (irreducible) representations $V_i=\operatorname{span}(e_i)$, for $(e_i)$ a basis of eigenvectors of $\varphi$, each given by a homomorphism
$$\ZZ/n\to\CC^*=GL_1(\CC),\qquad 1\mapsto\lambda=e^{2\pi ik/n}\qquad(n\text{ such}).$$

- Now, if $V$ is a $\CC$-representation of a finite abelian group $G$, $\rho:G\to GL(V)$, with $G\cong\ZZ/m_1\times\cdots\times\ZZ/m_r$, the $G$-action is equivalent to the data of $\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_r:V\to V$ such that $\varphi_i^{m_i}=\id_V$ and which pairwise commute, $\varphi_i\varphi_j=\varphi_j\varphi_i$ (then $\sum a_ie_i\mapsto\prod\varphi_i^{a_i}$, with $e_i$ the generators of the factors $\ZZ/m_i$).

  By the lemma each $\varphi_i$ is diagonalizable, and by the homework, commuting diagonalizable operators are *simultaneously* diagonalizable. In fact: the eigenspaces of $\varphi_i$ are invariant under all $\varphi_j$, and the restriction of $\varphi_j$ to an eigenspace of $\varphi_i$ is of finite order, hence diagonalizable by the lemma. Proceed by induction on $r$.

  This shows that $V$ splits into a $\bigoplus$ of 1-dimensional subrepresentations. Those now correspond to homomorphisms $G\to GL_1(\CC)\cong\CC^*$.

:::definition
For $G$ a finite abelian group, define its **dual** $\wh G=\Hom(G,\CC^*)$.
:::

This is an abelian group using pointwise multiplication: if $\rho,\rho':G\to\CC^*$ are homomorphisms, then so is
$$\rho\rho':G\to\CC^*,\qquad g\mapsto\rho(g)\rho'(g).$$
(⚠ This uses the fact that $\CC^*$ is abelian $\implies(\rho\rho')(g_1g_2)=(\rho\rho')(g_1)(\rho\rho')(g_2)$.)

Concretely, for $G=\ZZ/n$, $\wh G\cong\ZZ/n$ as well, via $\rho\mapsto\rho(1)\in\{e^{2\pi ik/n}\}\cong\ZZ/n$, though there is no canonical map $G\to\wh G$.

Similarly, $G=\ZZ/m_1\times\cdots\times\ZZ/m_r\implies\wh G\cong$ the same ($\rho$ is determined by the images of the generators of $G$, which are roots of 1 in $\CC^*$).

This completes the classification of (complex) representations of finite abelian groups!
:::
