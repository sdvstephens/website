## Part 1: Topology

### Homotopy and fundamental group

*(Munkres ch. 9, §51–55 + 58–60)*

- **Homotopy**: for $f_0,f_1\colon X\to Y$ continuous, a homotopy is $F\colon X\times I\to Y$ continuous with $F|_{X\times0}=f_0$, $F|_{X\times1}=f_1$.
- Paths $f_0,f_1\colon I\to Y$ are **path-homotopic** if there is a homotopy $F\colon I\times I\to Y$ *fixing end points*. *(Lec. 12)*
- Path-homotopy classes of paths in $X$ form a groupoid for **path composition** $f*g$ ($f$ from $x$ to $y$, then $g$ from $y$ to $z$). *(Lec. 13)*
- Path-homotopy classes of **loops** based at $x_0$ (= paths $x_0\to x_0$) form the **fundamental group** $\pi_1(X,x_0)$ (product = composition, identity = constant loop, inverse = reverse loop). *(Lec. 14)*
- $x_0,x_1$ in the same path component of $X$ $\Rightarrow$ $\pi_1(X,x_0)\cong\pi_1(X,x_1)$ (by attaching a path: $\alpha*f*\alpha^{-1}$).
- $f\colon(X,x_0)\to(Y,y_0)$ induces a homomorphism $f_*\colon\pi_1(X,x_0)\to\pi_1(Y,y_0)$. **Functorial**: $(f\circ g)_*=f_*\circ g_*$.
- Examples: $\RR^n$, convex subsets of $\RR^n$, and $S^n$ for $n\ge2$ are simply connected ($\pi_1=\{1\}$). $\pi_1(S^1,b_0)\simeq\ZZ$, $\pi_1(\text{torus})\simeq\ZZ^2$, $\pi_1(\text{figure eight})\simeq$ the free group $\langle a,b\rangle$. *(Lec. 15–16)*
- Applications of $\pi_1(S^1)=\ZZ$:
  - there is no **retraction** $r\colon B^2\to S^1$ (i.e. $r$ continuous, $r|_{S^1}=\id_{S^1}$);
  - every continuous $f\colon B^2\to B^2$ has a fixed point ($f(x)=x$) (Brouwer).
- **Deformation retraction**: a retraction $r\colon X\to A$ ($r|_A=\id_A$) such that $i\circ r$ is homotopic to $\id_X$ among maps that leave $A$ fixed, i.e. $H\colon X\times I\to X$ with
  $$H(x,0)=x,\qquad H(x,1)\in A\ \ \forall x\in X,\qquad H(a,t)=a\ \ \forall a\in A.$$
  Then $\pi_1(A,a_0)\simeq\pi_1(X,a_0)$ ($i_*$, $r_*$ inverse isomorphisms). *(Lec. 13–14)*
- The same holds more generally for **homotopy equivalences** $X\underset{g}{\overset{f}{\rightleftarrows}}Y$, $g\circ f\simeq\id_X$, $f\circ g\simeq\id_Y$.
- **Covering spaces**: $p\colon E\to B$ such that every $b\in B$ has a neighborhood $U\ni b$ evenly covered by $p$ ($p^{-1}(U)$ = disjoint union of slices $V_\alpha$, each $p|_{V_\alpha}\colon V_\alpha\to U$ a homeomorphism). *(Lec. 15)*
- Every path $f\colon I\to B$ starting at $b_0$ has a unique lift $\tilde f\colon I\to E$ starting at $e_0\in p^{-1}(b_0)$. (Path) homotopies lift to (path) homotopies.
- Looking at end points of lifts of loops in $(B,b_0)$, we get the lifting map $\pi_1(B,b_0)\to p^{-1}(b_0)$.
- Those loops which lift to a loop in $(E,e_0)$ form a subgroup $H\subset\pi_1(B,b_0)$, and $p_*\colon\pi_1(E,e_0)\xrightarrow{\sim}H\subset\pi_1(B,b_0)$. *(Lec. 16–17)*
- A map $g\colon(Y,y_0)\to(B,b_0)$ lifts to $\tilde g\colon(Y,y_0)\to(E,e_0)$ iff $g_*(\pi_1(Y,y_0))\subset H$.
- Classification of covering spaces (up to equivalence) $\leftrightarrow$ classification of subgroups $H\subset\pi_1(B)$ (up to conjugacy). *(Munkres §79–80)*
- Universal cover: simply connected $E$ (i.e. $H=\{1\}$).
- **Van Kampen**: $X=U\cup V$, $U,V$ open, $U\cap V\ni x_0$ path-connected $\Rightarrow$ *(Lec. 18; Munkres §70)*
  - $\pi_1(X,x_0)$ is generated by the images of $j_{1*}\colon\pi_1(U)\to\pi_1(X)$ and $j_{2*}\colon\pi_1(V)\to\pi_1(X)$;
  - if $\pi_1(U\cap V)=\{1\}$ then $\pi_1(X)$ is the free product $\pi_1(U)*\pi_1(V)$;
  - otherwise, it is the quotient of $\pi_1(U)*\pi_1(V)$ by the smallest normal subgroup that makes $i_{1*}(g)=i_{2*}(g)$ for all $g\in\pi_1(U\cap V)$.

## Part 2: Real analysis

### 1. Sequences and series

*(Rudin ch. 3; McMullen §4; Lec. 19)*

- In a metric space $(X,d)$, $x_n\to x$ iff $d(x_n,x)\to0$, i.e. $\forall\eps\ \exists N$ such that $\forall n\ge N$, $d(x_n,x)<\eps$.
- $(x_n)$ is a **Cauchy sequence** $:\iff\forall\eps\ \exists N$ such that $\forall m,n\ge N$, $d(x_n,x_m)<\eps$.
- Convergent $\Rightarrow$ Cauchy; $\Leftrightarrow$ if $(X,d)$ is **complete** (e.g. $\RR,\RR^n,\CC$; $C^0([a,b])$ with the sup norm; compact metric spaces; …).
- Compactness of $[-M,M]$ $\Rightarrow$ every bounded sequence in $\RR,\RR^n,\CC$ has a convergent subsequence.
- A monotonic sequence in $\RR$ (e.g. $a_n\le a_{n+1}$) converges iff it is bounded ($\Rightarrow\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=\sup\{a_n\}$).
- $a_n\to+\infty$ means $\forall M\ \exists N$ such that $n\ge N\Rightarrow a_n>M$ ($\Leftrightarrow$ converges in $\RR\cup\{\pm\infty\}$).
- If $(a_n)$ is bounded then $\limsup a_n:=$ the largest limit of a convergent subsequence of $(a_n)$. Similarly $\liminf$.
- A **series** $\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n$ converges iff the partial sums $s_n=\sum_{k=0}^na_k$ are a convergent sequence.
- $\sum a_n$ converges $\Rightarrow a_n\to0$. For $a_n\ge0$, $\sum a_n$ converges iff the partial sums are bounded.
- Comparison criterion: $0\le a_n\le b_n$, $\sum b_n$ convergent $\Rightarrow\sum a_n$ convergent.
- $\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n=\frac1{1-x}$ converges iff $|x|<1$; $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^\alpha}$ converges iff $\alpha>1$.
- $\sum a_n$ converges **absolutely** if $\sum|a_n|$ converges; absolutely convergent $\Rightarrow$ convergent, but not $\Leftarrow$: e.g. alternating series ($\sum(-1)^na_n$, $a_n\searrow0$) always converge.
- **Root test**: $\limsup|a_n|^{1/n}<1\Rightarrow\sum a_n$ converges (absolutely); $>1\Rightarrow$ diverges.

### 2. Continuous real functions of 1 variable

*(Rudin ch. 4 and 7; McMullen §3; Lec. 19)*

- Continuity at $x$ ($\forall\eps>0\ \exists\delta$ such that $\forall y$, $|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\eps$) $\Leftrightarrow\lim_{t\to x}f(t)=f(x)$. Infinite limits, limits at $\infty$: work in $\RR\cup\{\pm\infty\}$.
- Compactness of $[a,b]$ $\Rightarrow$ continuous functions on $[a,b]$ are **uniformly continuous**, i.e. $\forall\eps\ \exists\delta$ such that $\forall x,y$, $|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\eps$ (the same $\delta$ works for all $x$).
- **Intermediate value theorem**: $f([a,b])$ is connected $\Rightarrow$ it contains all reals between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$.
- **Extreme value theorem**: $f([a,b])$ is compact $\Rightarrow$ it is bounded and contains its inf and sup.
- $f_n\to f$ **pointwise** if $\forall x$, $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ (= product topology).
- $f_n\to f$ **uniformly** if $\|f_n-f\|_\infty=\sup_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|\to0$.
- If the $f_n$ are continuous and $f_n\to f$ uniformly then $f$ is continuous, i.e. $C^0\subset\{\text{functions}\}$ is closed in the uniform topology; $(C^0,\|\cdot\|_\infty)$ is complete.
- Uniform Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence $\leadsto$ Weierstrass M-test for series: if $\sup|f_n|\le M_n$ and $\sum M_n$ converges then $\sum f_n$ converges uniformly.
- **Power series**: $f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nz^n$. Radius of convergence:
  $$R=\frac1{\limsup|a_n|^{1/n}}\in[0,\infty];$$
  the series converges for $|z|<R$, uniformly on $\{|z|\le r\}$ for all $r<R$, and diverges for $|z|>R$. $f$ is continuous on $\{|z|<R\}$ (… and differentiable to all orders, see below).

### 3. Derivatives in 1 real variable

*(Rudin ch. 5; McMullen §5; Lec. 20)*

$$f'(x)=\lim_{t\to x}\frac{f(t)-f(x)}{t-x}.$$

- Differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous.
- **Mean value theorem**: $f\colon[a,b]\to\RR$ differentiable $\Rightarrow\exists c\in(a,b)$ such that $f(b)-f(a)=f'(c)(b-a)$.
- **Taylor’s theorem**: $f$ $n$ times differentiable $\Rightarrow\exists c\in(a,b)$ such that
  $$f(b)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(b-a)^k+\frac{f^{(n)}(c)}{n!}(b-a)^n.$$
- Most $C^\infty$ functions cannot be expressed as power series (the Taylor series $\not\to f$).
- $f_n\in C^1$, $f_n\to f$ pointwise, $f_n'\to g$ uniformly $\Rightarrow f\in C^1$, $f'=g$, and $f_n\to f$ in the $C^1$ topology.
- $C^k([a,b],\RR)=\{f\ k\text{ times differentiable},\ f^{(k)}\text{ continuous}\}$ with $\|f\|_{C^k}=\sum_{j=0}^k\|f^{(j)}\|_\infty$ is a complete metric space.
- $f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nx^n$ a power series $\Rightarrow f(x)$ is $C^\infty$ on $(-R,R)$, and $f'(x)=\sum na_nx^{n-1}$.

### 4. Riemann integral

*(Rudin ch. 6; McMullen §6; Lec. 21)*

- $f\colon[a,b]\to\RR$ bounded, $a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n=b$ $\Rightarrow$ $s_i=\inf f([x_{i-1},x_i])$, $S_i=\sup f([x_{i-1},x_i])$. Lower/upper Riemann sums:
  $$I_-(f)=\sup\left\{\sum s_i(x_i-x_{i-1})\right\},\qquad I_+(f)=\inf\left\{\sum S_i(x_i-x_{i-1})\right\}.$$
  $f$ is **Riemann integrable** on $[a,b]$ if $I_-(f)=I_+(f)$ $\left(\defeq\int_a^bf(x)\,dx\right)$.
- $f\le g\Rightarrow\int_a^bf\,dx\le\int_a^bg\,dx$; $a<c<b\Rightarrow\int_a^b=\int_a^c+\int_c^b$; etc.
- $f$ (piecewise) continuous on $[a,b]$ $\Rightarrow$ integrable.
- If $f\in C^0([a,b])$ then $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$ is differentiable and $F'=f$ (fundamental theorem of calculus).
- $\left|\int_a^bf\,dx-\int_a^bg\,dx\right|\le\int_a^b|f-g|\,dx\le(b-a)\|f-g\|_\infty$ $\Rightarrow$ if $f_n\to f$ uniformly then $\int_a^bf_n\,dx\to\int_a^bf\,dx$.
- The $L^p$ norm: for $p\ge1$,
  $$\|f\|_{L^p}=\left(\int_a^b|f(x)|^p\,dx\right)^{1/p},$$
  *coarser* than the uniform topology ($f_n\to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$ $\Rightarrow f_n\to f$ in $L^p$, but not $\Leftarrow$).

  $L^2$ inner product: $\langle f,g\rangle_{L^2}=\int_a^bfg\,dx$.
