:::remark Last time
- A **topology** on a set $X$ is a collection $\mcT\subset\mcP(X)$ of **open sets** such that (1) $\emptyset$, $X$ are open; (2) $U_i$, $i\in I$ open $\implies\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i$ open; (3) $U_1,\dots,U_n$ open $\implies U_1\cap\dots\cap U_n$ open.
- A **basis** for $\mcT$ is $\mcB$ such that open sets = unions of elements of $\mcB$ (or: $U$ is open $\iff\forall p\in U$ $\exists B\in\mcB$ such that $p\in B\subset U$).
:::

This can simplify the description of a topology, and also verifications of continuity, convergence, etc. E.g., if $X,Y$ have topologies $\mcT_X,\mcT_Y$ with bases $\mcB_X,\mcB_Y$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f:X\to Y\text{ is continuous}&\iff\forall U\subset Y\text{ open},\ f^{-1}(U)\subset X\text{ is open}\\
&\iff\forall B\in\mcB_Y\text{ (basis open set)},\ f^{-1}(B)\subset X\text{ is open}\\
&\iff\forall B\in\mcB_Y,\ \forall x\in f^{-1}(B),\ \exists B'\in\mcB_X\text{ such that }x\in B'\subset f^{-1}(B)\\
&\iff\forall x\in X,\ \forall B\in\mcB_Y\text{ containing }f(x),\ \exists B'\in\mcB_X\text{ containing }x\text{ such that }f(B')\subset B.
\end{aligned}
$$
(For the second equivalence, going back up: write $U=\bigcup B_i$; then $f^{-1}(U)=\bigcup f^{-1}(B_i)$.) Compare the definition of continuity via balls in metric spaces.

## Subspaces and products

- **Subspace topology** on $Y\subset X$: $\mcT_Y=\{U\cap Y\mid U\subset X\text{ open}\}$.

:::definition
Let $(X,\mcT_X)$, $(Y,\mcT_Y)$ be topological spaces. The **product topology** on $X\times Y$ is the topology generated by the basis
$$
\mcB=\{U\times V\mid U\subset X\text{ open},\ V\subset Y\text{ open}\}.
$$
:::

(Check that $\mcB$ is a basis. Why isn’t $\mcB$ already a topology?)

![Two open rectangles U_1 x V_1 and U_2 x V_2 in X x Y whose intersection is again a rectangle](/notes/math-55b/figures/a1-product-basis.svg)

- When $X,Y$ are metric spaces, this is also a metric topology, defined e.g. by
$$
d^\infty_{X\times Y}((x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2))=\max\big(d_X(x_1,x_2),\,d_Y(y_1,y_2)\big)
$$
(check! Key observation: $B^{X\times Y}_r((x,y))=B^X_r(x)\times B^Y_r(y)$). Or in fact
$$
d^2_{X\times Y}=\sqrt{d_X(x_1,x_2)^2+d_Y(y_1,y_2)^2},\qquad d^1_{X\times Y}=d_X(x_1,x_2)+d_Y(y_1,y_2)
$$
define the same topology on $X\times Y$ (see HW for the case of $\RR^2$). So this gives the usual topology on $\RR^n$.
- $f=(f_1,f_2):Z\to X\times Y$ is continuous $\iff f_1:Z\to X$ and $f_2:Z\to Y$ are continuous.
- A sequence in $X\times Y$ converges, $(x_n,y_n)\to(x,y)$, $\iff x_n\to x$ and $y_n\to y$.
- Similarly for finite products $X_1\times\dots\times X_n$. For infinite products there are several different natural topologies; see next week.

## Homeomorphisms

When are two topological spaces “the same”?

:::definition
$X,Y$ are **homeomorphic** if there exist continuous maps $f:X\to Y$ and $g:Y\to X$ such that $f\circ g=\id_Y$, $g\circ f=\id_X$.

Equivalently, a **homeomorphism** $f:X\to Y$ is a continuous bijection such that $f^{-1}$ is continuous. I.e., a bijection $X\leftrightarrow Y$ under which $\mcT_X\leftrightarrow\mcT_Y$.
:::

:::remark
- A continuous bijection need not be a homeomorphism. E.g. take $X$ with two topologies, $\mcT'$ strictly finer than $\mcT$ ($\mcT'\supsetneq\mcT$). Then $\id_X:(X,\mcT')\to(X,\mcT)$ is a bijection, continuous since $U\in\mcT\implies\id^{-1}(U)=U\in\mcT'$, but not a homeomorphism.
- Say a metric space $(X,d)$ is **bounded** if $\diam(X)=\sup\{d(p,q)\mid p,q\in X\}<\infty$. This is *not* a topological property: e.g. $f:(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2)\to\RR$, $x\mapsto\tan x$ is a homeomorphism ($\tan$ and $\arctan$ are continuous), so $\RR$ is homeomorphic to $(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2)$ (or any open interval in $\RR$).

![Graph of tan x on the interval from minus pi over 2 to pi over 2](/notes/math-55b/figures/a1-tan-graph.svg)
:::

## Closed sets

:::definition
A subset $A$ of a topological space $X$ is **closed** if $X\setminus A$ is open.
:::

**Warning:** subsets can be both closed and open, e.g. $\emptyset$ and $X$, or neither (e.g. $[0,1)$ or $\QQ$ in $\RR$).

The axioms of open sets imply:

- $\emptyset$, $X$ are closed;
- arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed;
- finite unions of closed sets are closed.

:::definition
Let $A\subset X$ be any subset. We define:

1. the **closure** of $A$: $\overline{A}$ = the smallest closed set containing $A$ $=\bigcap_{F\supset A,\ F\text{ closed}}F$ ($\overline{A}\supset A$, and $\overline{A}$ is closed since it’s an intersection of closed sets);
2. the **interior** of $A$: $\operatorname{int}(A)$ = the largest open set contained in $A$ $=\bigcup_{U\subset A,\ U\text{ open}}U$ (open);
3. the **boundary** of $A$: $\partial A=\overline{A}\setminus\operatorname{int}(A)$.
:::

![A set A with a hair attached, its closure, its interior and its boundary](/notes/math-55b/figures/a1-closure-interior-boundary.svg)

:::example
$A=[0,1)\subset\RR$, usual topology $\implies\overline{A}=[0,1]$, $\operatorname{int}(A)=(0,1)$, $\partial A=\{0,1\}$.
:::

:::remark
- $A$ is closed iff $\overline{A}=A$; open iff $\operatorname{int}(A)=A$.
- $\overline{X\setminus A}=X\setminus\operatorname{int}(A)$ and $\operatorname{int}(X\setminus A)=X\setminus\overline{A}$. $\quad(\ast)$
:::

:::definition
Say $U\subset X$ is a **neighborhood** of $p\in X$ if $U$ is open and $p\in U$.
:::

:::proposition
1. $p\in\operatorname{int}(A)$ iff $A$ contains a neighborhood of $p$.
2. $p\in\overline{A}$ iff every neighborhood of $p$ intersects $A$ nontrivially.
:::

:::proof
Check this! (1) follows from the definitions: $p\in\operatorname{int}(A)\iff\exists U$ open such that $p\in U\subset A$.

(2) follows from (1) and $(\ast)$: $p\in\overline{A}\iff p\notin\operatorname{int}(X\setminus A)\iff\forall U\ni p$ open, $A\cap U\ne\emptyset$.
:::

:::definition
Say $A$ is **dense** if $\overline{A}=X$ (i.e. every nonempty open subset of $X$ intersects $A$ nontrivially).
:::

:::example
$\QQ$ is dense in $\RR$ (for the usual topology).
:::

## Closed sets and limit points

:::definition
$x\in X$ is a **limit point** of $A\subset X$ if for every neighborhood $U\ni x$, $U\cap(A\setminus\{x\})\ne\emptyset$.
:::

![A set A, with a point inside it and a point on its edge that are limit points, and a point away from A that is not](/notes/math-55b/figures/a1-limit-points.svg)

:::example
In $\RR_{\text{std}}$, $1$ is a limit point of $(0,1)$ and of $[0,1]$. $1$ is not a limit point of $\{\frac1n,\ n\ge1\}\cup\{0\}$, but $0$ is.
:::

:::proposition
$\overline{A}=A\cup\{\text{limit points of }A\}$.
:::

:::proof
$A\subset\overline{A}$ by definition, so it’s enough to consider points not in $A$. If $x\notin A$, then for every neighborhood $U\ni x$,
$$
U\cap(A\setminus\{x\})=U\cap A.
$$
$x$ is a limit point if the left-hand side is always $\ne\emptyset$, and $x\in\overline{A}$ if the right-hand side is always $\ne\emptyset$. So $x\in\overline{A}$ iff $x$ is a limit point.
:::

:::corollary
$A$ is closed iff $A$ contains all of its limit points.
:::

**Warning:** what is the connection between limit points and limits of sequences?

Recall: a sequence $\{p_n\}$ in $X$ converges to $p\in X$ if $\forall U$ neighborhood of $p$, $\exists N$ such that $n\ge N\implies p_n\in U$.

:::proposition
Let $p\in X$ and $A\subset X$.

- If $\exists\{p_n\}$ sequence in $A$ such that $p_n\to p$, then $p\in\overline{A}$.
- If $\exists\{p_n\}$ sequence in $A$ with $p_n\ne p$ for infinitely many $n$ and $p_n\to p$, then $p$ is a limit point of $A$.
:::

:::proof
Any neighborhood $U\ni p$ contains $p_n$ for all large $n$, hence contains points of $A$ (distinct from $p$ in the second case).
:::

The converse is true in **metric spaces**: if $p\in\overline{A}$ (resp. $p$ is a limit point of $A$) then $\forall n>0$ $\exists p_n\in B_{1/n}(p)\cap A$ (resp. with $p_n\ne p$), so there is a sequence in $A$ such that $p_n\to p$.

This holds more generally in spaces whose points have countable bases of neighborhoods $U_1\supset U_2\supset\cdots$ (i.e. $\forall p$ $\exists$ neighborhoods $U_1,U_2,\dots$ such that for every neighborhood $U\ni p$, $\exists n$ such that $p\in U_n\subset U$), but not in arbitrary topological spaces!

## Hausdorff spaces

In a metric space, a sequence converges to at most one limit. This is not true in an arbitrary topological space!

:::example
$X=\RR$ with the **finite complement topology**: open subsets are $\emptyset$ and $\RR\setminus\{\text{finite sets}\}$. Let $a_1,a_2,\dots$ be a sequence in $X$ with all $a_i$ distinct. Then $\forall x\in X$, every neighborhood $U\ni x$ contains all but finitely many of the $a_i$, hence $\exists N$ such that $a_n\in U$ $\forall n\ge N$. Thus the sequence converges to *every* point of $X$!
:::

To avoid such pathological behavior:

:::definition
A topological space is **Hausdorff** (or $T_2$) if $\forall x_1\ne x_2\in X$, $\exists$ neighborhoods $U_1\ni x_1$, $U_2\ni x_2$ such that $U_1\cap U_2=\emptyset$.
:::

:::example
1. Any metric space is Hausdorff: given $x_1\ne x_2$, choose $0<\eps<\frac12d(x_1,x_2)$; then $U_i=B_\eps(x_i)$ are disjoint neighborhoods of $x_i$.

   ![Disjoint balls of radius epsilon around two distinct points x_1 and x_2](/notes/math-55b/figures/a1-hausdorff-balls.svg)

2. The finite complement topology on $\RR$ is not Hausdorff, since any two nonempty open sets intersect (in infinitely many points).
3. The discrete topology is always Hausdorff ($U_i=\{x_i\}$ are disjoint neighborhoods of $x_i$).
4. One can show: $X$ Hausdorff, $Y\subset X\implies$ the subspace topology is Hausdorff; $X,Y$ Hausdorff $\implies X\times Y$ Hausdorff. (Homework!)
:::

:::outline Notes not reached in this lecture, covered at the start of [Lecture 4](/notes/math-55b/04-infinite-products-and-connectedness)
:::theorem
If $X$ is Hausdorff then every sequence in $X$ converges to at most one limit.
:::

:::proof
Assume $x_1,x_2,\dots$ converges to $x\in X$, and let $y\ne x$. Choose disjoint neighborhoods $U_x\ni x$, $U_y\ni y$. Since $x_n\to x$, $\exists N$ such that $\forall n\ge N$, $x_n\in U_x$. Hence $x_n\notin U_y$ for $n\ge N$, so the sequence doesn’t converge to $y$.
:::

:::remark
- There’s in fact a whole hierarchy of “separation axioms”. E.g. a weaker one is: a topological space is **$T_1$** if $\forall x\ne y\in X$, $\exists U_y\ni y$ neighborhood such that $x\notin U_y$. Equivalently: $X$ is $T_1\iff\{x\}$ is closed in $X$ $\forall x\in X$ (exercise!). Hausdorff ($T_2$) $\implies T_1$, but e.g. ($\RR$, finite complement topology) is $T_1$ but not Hausdorff.
- Hausdorff spaces are fairly nice to work with, and we will generally be working with this assumption. There are more subtle reasons why not every Hausdorff topology comes from a metric, but one can give pretty good criteria for a topology to be metrizable involving further separation conditions (“normal” or $T_4$) (plus a countability condition). We’ll see the Urysohn metrization theorem.
:::

**Manifolds and CW complexes**

Metric spaces are nice, but they can still be pretty nasty. (We’ll see conditions such as local connectedness, local compactness etc. come up.) Algebraic topologists like to focus on even nicer spaces. For example:

:::definition
An **$n$-dimensional topological manifold** is a topological space $X$ such that every point $p\in X$ has a neighborhood homeomorphic to $\RR^n$ (or equivalently, to an open ball in $\RR^n$).
:::

:::example
$S^1\subset\RR^2$ is a 1-dimensional topological manifold; the sphere and the torus in $\RR^3$ are 2-dimensional topological manifolds.

![A circle in the plane, and a sphere and a torus in space](/notes/math-55b/figures/a1-manifolds.svg)
:::

:::example
![A Y-shaped graph: three segments meeting at one vertex](/notes/math-55b/figures/a1-tripod.svg)

This Y-shaped graph isn’t a topological manifold (the vertex looks wrong), but it is part of a more general class of spaces called **CW complexes**, built by attaching “cells” (closed balls of dimension $0,1,\dots$) onto each other inductively.
:::

We’ll see more on this later when we get to algebraic topology. In decreasing order of generality:
$$
\{\text{top. spaces}\}\supset\{\text{Hausdorff}\}\supset\{\text{metrizable}\}\supset\{\text{(loc. finite) CW complexes}\}\supset\{\text{manifolds}\}.
$$

:::
