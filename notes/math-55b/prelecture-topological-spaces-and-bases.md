:::definition
A **topology** on a set $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{T}$ of subsets of $X$ having the following properties:

1. $\varnothing$ and $X$ are in $\mathcal{T}$.
2. The union of the elements of any subcollection of $\mathcal{T}$ is in $\mathcal{T}$.
3. The intersection of the elements of any finite subcollection of $\mathcal{T}$ is in $\mathcal{T}$.

(A topology together with its set is written $(X,\mathcal{T})$.)
:::

:::note
A set $X$ for which a topology $\mathcal{T}$ has been specified is called a **topological space**.
:::

Let $U\subset X$ and $U\in\mathcal{T}$. Then we say $U$ is an **open set**.

On a topological space, finite intersections of open sets are open.

:::example
Let $X$ be any three-element set $X=\{a,b,c\}$. The following are topologies on $X$:
$$\{\varnothing,X\},\quad \{\varnothing,\{a\},\{a,b\},X\},\quad\{\varnothing,\{b\},X\},$$
$$\{\varnothing,\{a\},\{b,c\},X\},\quad\{\varnothing,\{b\},\{a,b\},\{b,c\},X\}.$$

![Five topologies on the three-point set {a, b, c}, with the open sets circled in red](/notes/math-55b/figures/b3-three-point-topologies.svg)

And so on. Once the list is complete, you can get all topologies on $X$ by permuting $a$, $b$, and $c$.

:::

The **discrete topology** is $\mathcal{P}(X)$, the collection of all subsets of $X$; the **trivial** (or **indiscrete**) **topology** on $X$ is $\{\varnothing,X\}$.

:::definition The finite complement topology
Let $X$ be a set and let $\mathcal{T}_f$ be the collection of all subsets $U\subseteq X$ such that $X-U$ either is finite or is all of $X$.

Both $\varnothing$ and $X$ are in $\mathcal{T}_f$, since $X-X$ is finite and $X-\varnothing$ is all of $X$. If $\{U_\alpha\}$ is an indexed family of nonempty elements of $\mathcal{T}_f$, to show that $\bigcup U_\alpha$ is in $\mathcal{T}_f$ we compute
$$X-\bigcup U_\alpha=\bigcap\,(X-U_\alpha),$$
which is finite because each set $X-U_\alpha$ is finite. If $U_1,\dots,U_n$ are nonempty elements of $\mathcal{T}_f$, to show that $\bigcap U_i\in\mathcal{T}_f$ we compute
$$X-\bigcap_{i=1}^n U_i=\bigcup_{i=1}^n\,(X-U_i),$$
the latter set being finite since it is a finite union of finite sets.
:::

## Comparing topologies

:::definition
Suppose that $\mathcal{T}$ and $\mathcal{T}'$ are two topologies on a given set $X$. If $\mathcal{T}'\supset\mathcal{T}$, we say that $\mathcal{T}'$ is **finer** than $\mathcal{T}$. If $\mathcal{T}'$ properly contains $\mathcal{T}$, we say that $\mathcal{T}'$ is **strictly finer** than $\mathcal{T}$. We also say that $\mathcal{T}$ is **coarser** than $\mathcal{T}'$, or **strictly coarser**, in these two respective situations. We say $\mathcal{T}$ is **comparable** with $\mathcal{T}'$ if either $\mathcal{T}'\supset\mathcal{T}$ or $\mathcal{T}\supset\mathcal{T}'$.

Another term that is used, though less vivid, is **larger** and **smaller**, respectively. Rarely, the terms “**weak**” and “**strong**” topologies are invoked for the same purpose.
:::

## Bases

:::definition
If $X$ is a set, a **basis** for a topology on $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{B}$ of subsets of $X$ (called **basis elements**) such that:

1. For all $x\in X$, there is $B\in\mathcal{B}$ such that $x\in B$.
2. If $x$ belongs to the intersection of two basis elements $B_1$ and $B_2$, then there is a $B_3$ containing $x$ such that $B_3\subset B_1\cap B_2$.
:::

Let us restate the basis in words of our own: *”A basis $\mathcal{C}$ is a collection of sets in $X$ such that for all $x\in X$, there exists a basis element $C\in\mathcal{C}$ such that $x\in C$; furthermore, for any $x\in C_1\cap C_2$ with $C_1,C_2\in\mathcal{C}$ as basis elements, there is another basis element $C_3$ such that $C_3\subseteq C_1\cap C_2$ and $x\in C_3$.”*

It naturally follows, then, that the **topology generated by the basis** $\mathcal{B}$ is defined as follows: a subset $U$ is said to be open in $X$ (i.e. $U\in\mathcal{T}$) if for all $x\in U$ there is $B\in\mathcal{B}$ such that $x\in B$ and $B\subset U$. This is to say that we can generate any element in the topology only using basis elements.

Of note: the collection of all unions of basis elements is exactly the topology on the set.

## Math Major:

Open intervals in $\RR$ $\to$ open sets in $\RR$ $\to$ open sets in a topological space.

**Open interval:** $(a,b)=\{x\in\RR\mid a<x<b\}$.

![The open interval (a, b) on the number line with a smaller interval (x - delta, x + delta) inside it, centred at x](/notes/math-55b/figures/b3-interval-around-x.svg)

For any point $x$ in an open interval, we can find another interval, completely contained in the original interval, centered at $x$.

:::proposition Claim
For all $x\in(a,b)$, there is $\delta>0$ such that $(x-\delta,x+\delta)\subseteq(a,b)$.
:::

:::proof
Given $x\in(a,b)$, let $\delta=\min(x-a,\,b-x)$. Take some $y\in(x-\delta,x+\delta)$; we want to show $y\in(a,b)$. Observe that $\delta\leq x-a$ and $\delta\leq b-x$, so $-\delta\geq a-x$. Notice $a=x+(a-x)$, so
$$a\leq x-\delta<y<x+\delta\leq x+(b-x)=b\implies a<y<b\implies y\in(a,b)\implies(x-\delta,x+\delta)\subseteq(a,b).$$
:::

:::definition
A set $U\subseteq\RR$ is **open** if for all $x\in U$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $(x-\delta,x+\delta)\subseteq U$.
:::

:::example
The following sets are open in $\RR$:

1. $\RR$
2. $\varnothing$
3. $(a,\infty)$
4. $(-\infty,b)$
5. $\bigcup_{n=0}^\infty(2n,2n+1)$ (a union of open sets, so open)

:::solution
- $\RR$: given $x\in\RR$, note that for all $x$, $(x-1,x+1)\subseteq\RR$.
- $\varnothing$: vacuously true.
- $(a,\infty)$: take $x\in(a,\infty)$; for all such $x$, $\big(x-(x-a),\,x+(x-a)\big)\subseteq(a,\infty)$.
- $(-\infty,b)$: same.
:::
:::

We say a set $A\subseteq\RR$ is **not open** if there is some $x\in A$ such that for all $\delta>0$, $(x-\delta,x+\delta)\not\subseteq A$.

:::example
The following sets are not open in $\RR$:

1. $\ZZ$
2. $[a,b]$
3. $[a,b)$ and $(a,b]$
4. $[a,\infty)$
5. $(-\infty,b]$
6. $\QQ$

:::solution
1. Consider $x=1\in\ZZ$ and any $\delta>0$. There is no $y\in\ZZ$ such that $1<y<2$, so the points of $(x-\delta,x+\delta)$ just to the right of $1$ are not integers, and $(x-\delta,x+\delta)\not\subseteq\ZZ$.

2. Consider the endpoint $a$ of $[a,b]$: let $x=a$; then $x-\delta/2\notin[a,b]$.
3. Same, but with the closed endpoint: $a$ for $[a,b)$ and $b$ for $(a,b]$.
4. and 5. Same.
6. Consider $(3,4)$ and the rational point $x=3\in\QQ$. For every $\delta>0$, the interval $(3-\delta,3+\delta)$ contains irrational numbers (such as $\pi$, once $\delta>\pi-3$), which are not in $\QQ$; so $(x-\delta,x+\delta)\not\subseteq\QQ$.

:::
:::

## More on bases

When I say $\mathcal{T}'$ is finer than $\mathcal{T}$ (for topologies generated by bases $\mathcal{B}'$ and $\mathcal{B}$), what I mean is that for all $x\in X$ and all $B\in\mathcal{B}$ containing $x$, there is $B'\in\mathcal{B}'$ such that $x\in B'\subset B$.

:::definition
If $\mathcal{B}$ is the collection of all open intervals in the real line, $(a,b)=\{x\in\RR\mid a<x<b\}$, the topology generated by $\mathcal{B}$ is called the **standard topology** on the real line.
:::

:::definition
If $\mathcal{C}$ is the basis of all half-open intervals $[a,b)=\{x\mid a\leq x<b\}$ on the reals, then the topology generated by $\mathcal{C}$ is known as the **lower limit topology**. We denote this $\RR_\ell$.
:::

:::definition
Let $K=\{\tfrac1n\mid n\in\ZZ_{>0}\}$ and let $\mathcal{B}'$ be the collection of open intervals $(a,b)$ along with all sets of the form $(a,b)-K$. The topology generated by $\mathcal{B}'$ is called the **$K$-topology**. It is denoted $\RR_K$.
:::

The topologies of $\RR_\ell$ and $\RR_K$ are strictly finer than the standard topology, but are not comparable to one another.

:::definition
A **subbasis** $\mathcal{S}$ for a topology on $X$ is a collection of subsets of $X$ whose union equals $X$. The **topology generated by the subbasis** $\mathcal{S}$ is defined to be the collection $\mathcal{T}$ of all unions of finite intersections of elements of $\mathcal{S}$.
:::
