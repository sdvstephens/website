A **σ-algebra** is an algebra closed under countable unions (the term σ-field, resp. field, is sometimes used instead).

Note that since $\bigcap_iE_i=\big(\bigcup_iE_i^c\big)^c$, algebras (resp. σ-algebras) are also closed under finite (resp. countable) intersections. Moreover, if $\sA$ is an algebra then $\varnothing\in\sA$ and $X\in\sA$, for given any $E\in\sA$, $\varnothing=E\cap E^c$ and $X=E\cup E^c$.

An algebra $\sA$ is a σ-algebra if it’s closed under countable *disjoint* unions. Indeed, suppose $\{E_j\}_1^\infty\subset\sA$. We set

$$F_k=E_k\setminus\Big[\bigcup_1^{k-1}E_j\Big]=E_k\cap\Big(\bigcup_1^{k-1}E_j\Big)^c.$$

Thus $F_k\in\sA$ for all $k$, the $F_k$ are disjoint, and $\bigcup_1^\infty E_j=\bigcup_1^\infty F_k$.

:::example Examples
If $X$ is any set, $\sP(X)$ and $\{\varnothing,X\}$ are σ-algebras. If $X$ is uncountable, then

$$\sA=\{E\subset X\mid E\text{ countable OR }E^c\text{ countable}\}:$$

this is a σ-algebra, called the **σ-algebra of cocountable sets** or **σ-algebra of countable sets**. Point being, if $\{E_j\}_1^\infty\subset\sA$, then $\bigcup_1^\infty E_j$ is countable if all the $E_j$ are countable, xor cocountable (if some $E_j$ is).
:::

The intersection of any family of σ-algebras on $X$ is a σ-algebra.

If $\sE$ is any subset of $\sP(X)$, there is a unique **smallest σ-algebra** $\sM(\sE)\supset\sE$, namely the intersection of all σ-algebras containing $\sE$. $\sM(\sE)$ is called the σ-algebra **generated** by $\sE$.

:::lemma Lemma 1
If $\sE\subset\sM(\sF)$ then $\sM(\sE)\subset\sM(\sF)$.
:::

:::proof
$\sM(\sF)$ is a σ-algebra containing $\sE$, so it must too contain the σ-algebra generated by $\sE$, $\sM(\sE)$.
:::

Let $X$ be a metric or topological space. Then the σ-algebra generated by the family $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}$ of open sets in $X$ is called the **Borel σ-algebra** on $X$ and is denoted by $\sB_X$. Its members are called **Borel sets**. $\sB_X$ includes open sets, closed sets, and (countable) unions and intersections of said open and closed sets.

Terminology: a countable $\bigcap$ of open sets is a **$G_\delta$ set**; a countable union of closed sets is an **$F_\sigma$ set**; a countable $\bigcup$ of $G_\delta$ sets is called a **$G_{\delta\sigma}$ set**; a countable $\bigcap$ of $F_\sigma$ sets is an **$F_{\sigma\delta}$ set**, etc. $\delta$ = **Durchschnitt** und $\sigma$ ist **Summe** (intersektion und union).

The $\sB_\RR$ σ-algebra is VERY important for our study.

:::proposition
$\sB_\RR$ is generated by each of the following:

- (a) the open intervals $\sE_1=\{(a,b)\mid a<b\}$;
- (b) the closed intervals $\sE_2=\{[a,b]\mid a<b\}$;
- (c) the half-open intervals $\sE_3=\{(a,b]\mid a<b\}$ or $\sE_4=\{[a,b)\mid a<b\}$;
- (d) the open rays $\sE_5=\{(a,\infty)\mid a\in\RR\}$ or $\sE_6=\{(-\infty,a)\mid a\in\RR\}$;
- (e) the closed rays $\sE_7=\{[a,\infty)\mid a\in\RR\}$ or $\sE_8=\{(-\infty,a]\mid a\in\RR\}$.
:::

Let $\{X_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}$ be an indexed collection of sets with $X_\alpha\neq\varnothing$ for all $\alpha$, let $X=\prod_{\alpha\in A}X_\alpha$, and let $\pi_\alpha:X\to X_\alpha$ be the coordinate maps. If $\sM_\alpha$ is a σ-algebra on $X_\alpha$ for each $\alpha$, the **product σ-algebra** on $X$ is the σ-algebra generated by

$$\{\pi_\alpha^{-1}(E_\alpha)\mid E_\alpha\in\sM_\alpha,\ \alpha\in A\}.$$

We denote this σ-algebra by $\bigotimes_{\alpha\in A}\sM_\alpha$, and if $A=\{1,\dots,n\}$ we write $\bigotimes_1^n\sM_j$ or $\sM_1\otimes\cdots\otimes\sM_n$.

:::proposition
If $A$ is countable, then $\bigotimes_{\alpha\in A}\sM_\alpha$ is the σ-algebra generated by $\big\{\prod_{\alpha\in A}E_\alpha \mid E_\alpha\in\sM_\alpha\big\}$.
:::

:::proof
If $E_\alpha\in\sM_\alpha$, then $\pi_\alpha^{-1}(E_\alpha)=\prod_{\beta\in A}E_\beta$, where $E_\beta=X_\beta$ for $\beta\neq\alpha$; conversely, $\prod_{\alpha\in A}E_\alpha=\bigcap_{\alpha\in A}\pi_\alpha^{-1}(E_\alpha)$. The result thus follows from Lemma 1.
:::

:::proposition
Suppose $\sM_\alpha$ is generated by $\sE_\alpha$, $\alpha\in A$. Then $\bigotimes_{\alpha\in A}\sM_\alpha$ is generated by $\sF_1=\{\pi_\alpha^{-1}(E_\alpha)\mid E_\alpha\in\sE_\alpha,\ \alpha\in A\}$. If $A$ is countable and $X_\alpha\in\sE_\alpha$ for all $\alpha$, then $\bigotimes_{\alpha\in A}\sM_\alpha$ is also generated by $\sF_2=\big\{\prod_{\alpha\in A}E_\alpha\mid E_\alpha\in\sE_\alpha\big\}$.
:::

:::proof
$\sM(\sF_1)\subset\bigotimes_{\alpha\in A}\sM_\alpha$, obviously. For each $\alpha$, $\{E\subset X_\alpha\mid\pi_\alpha^{-1}(E)\in\sM(\sF_1)\}$ is easily seen to be a σ-algebra on $X_\alpha$ which contains $\sE_\alpha$, and therefore $\sM_\alpha$; i.e., $\pi_\alpha^{-1}(E)\in\sM(\sF_1)$ for all $E\in\sM_\alpha$, $\alpha\in A$, and hence $\bigotimes_{\alpha\in A}\sM_\alpha\subset\sM(\sF_1)$. The second ($\sF_2$) follows here from the first.
:::

:::proposition
Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be metric spaces and let $X=\prod_1^nX_i$, with the product metric. Then $\bigotimes_1^n\sB_{X_i}\subset\sB_X$. If the $X_i$‘s are separable, then $\bigotimes_1^n\sB_{X_i}=\sB_X$.
:::

:::proof
$\bigotimes_1^n\sB_{X_i}$ is generated by the sets $\pi_i^{-1}(U_i)$, $1\le i\le n$, with $U_i$ open in $X_i$. Since these are open in $X$, our Lemma 1 implies $\bigotimes_1^n\sB_{X_i}\subset\sB_X$.

Suppose now that $\{x_i^k\}_{k=1}^\infty$ is a countable dense set in $X_i$, $1\le i\le n$, and let $\sE_i$ be the collection of balls with rational radius and center at some $x_i^k$. Then every open set in $X_i$ is a union of members of $\sE_i$—in fact, a countable union, since $\sE_i$ itself is countable. Moreover, the set of points in $X$ whose coordinates are among the $x_i^k$ is a countable dense subset of $X$, and the balls of radius $r$ in $X$ are merely products of balls of radius $r$ in the $X_i$‘s. It follows that $\sB_{X_i}$ is generated by $\sE_i$ and $\sB_X$ is generated by $\{E_1\times\cdots\times E_n\mid E_i\in\sE_i\}$. Therefore $\sB_X=\bigotimes_1^n\sB_{X_i}$.
:::

:::corollary
$\sB_{\RR^n}=\bigotimes_1^n\sB_\RR$.
:::

More definitions now! An **elementary family** is a collection $\sE$ of subsets of $X$ such that:

- (i) $\varnothing\in\sE$;
- (ii) if $E,F\in\sE$, then $E\cap F\in\sE$;
- (iii) if $E\in\sE$, then $E^c$ is a finite disjoint union of members of $\sE$.

:::proposition
If $\sE$ is an elementary family, the collection $\sA$ of finite disjoint unions of members of $\sE$ is an algebra.

## IMPA Notes (Lecture)

:::definition
$\sP(\Omega)\supseteq\sJ$, where $\sJ$ is a **semi-algebra** if:

- (i) $\Omega\in\sJ$;
- (ii) if $A,B\in\sJ$, then $A\cap B\in\sJ$;
- (iii) if $A\in\sJ$, then there are $E_1,\dots,E_n\in\sJ$ with $A^c=\sum_{i=1}^nE_i$ (a disjoint union).
:::

:::example
$\Omega=\RR$, so $\RR\in\sJ$; $\{(a,b]\mid a<b,\ a,b\in\RR\}$ and $\{(-\infty,b]\mid b\in\RR\}$ and $\{(a,\infty)\mid a\in\RR\}$ and $\varnothing$ are all in $\sJ$.

**Check:** Ah—clear to see $\RR\in\sJ$ and, for all $A,B$, $A\cap B\in\sJ$, and $(a,b]^c=(-\infty,a]\cup(b,\infty)$, a disjoint union of members of $\sJ$.

This example extends to $\RR^2$ by $(a_1,b_1]\times(a_2,b_2]$.
:::

:::definition
$\sP(\Omega)\supseteq\sA$; $\sA$ is an **algebra** if:

- (i) $\Omega\in\sA$;
- (ii) $A,B\in\sA\implies A\cap B\in\sA$;
- (iii) $A\in\sA\implies A^c\in\sA$.
:::

**Obs:** If $\sA$ is an algebra, then $\sA$ is a semi-algebra.

:::definition
A **σ-algebra** is an $\sF\subseteq\sP(\Omega)$ with:

- (i) $\Omega\in\sF$;
- (ii) if $A_j\in\sF$ for all $j\ge1$, then $\bigcap_{j\ge1}^\infty A_j\in\sF$ (note that the $A_j$ may be such that $A_j=\Omega$ for all $j\ge N$, where $N$ is arbitrary);
- (iii) $A\in\sF\implies A^c\in\sF$.
:::

**Obs:** If $\sF$ is a σ-algebra $\implies$ $\sF$ is an algebra $\implies$ $\sF$ is a semi-algebra.

:::proposition
Consider $\Omega$ and $\{\sA_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}$, with each $\sA_\alpha\subseteq\sP(\Omega)$, such that each $\sA_\alpha$ is an algebra. Then $\bigcap_{\alpha\in A}\sA_\alpha$ is an algebra. (Re: σ-algebras.)
:::

:::proof
$\Omega$ belongs to each $\sA_\alpha$, thus it belongs to the intersection of all the $\sA_\alpha$‘s. Additionally, let $A,B\in\bigcap_{\alpha\in A}\sA_\alpha$, so $A,B\in\sA_\alpha$ for all $\alpha\in A$; thus $A\cap B\in\sA_\alpha$ for all $\alpha\in A$, i.e. $A\cap B\in\bigcap_{\alpha\in A}\sA_\alpha$. Finally, since $A,A^c\in\sA_\alpha$ for all $\alpha\in A$, then $\bigcap_{\alpha\in A}\sA_\alpha\ni A^c$ for all $A\in\bigcap_{\alpha\in A}\sA_\alpha$.
:::
