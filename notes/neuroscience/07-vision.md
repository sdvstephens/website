The objectives for this lecture were to understand the key anatomy of the eye, to describe the two visual pathways of perception, and to explain the perceptual outcomes associated with neurological damage.

## Light, and what different animals see

A beam of light can be separated into its wavelengths. Visible light is a narrow band of the **electromagnetic spectrum**, which runs from gamma rays (wavelengths around $10^{-3}$ nm) through X-rays, ultraviolet, visible light and infrared to radar, FM, TV, AM and AC circuits (around $10^{15}$ nm). The light visible to the human eye runs from violet (the slide’s scale starts at 350 nm) through green (500 nm) and yellow (600 nm) to red (700 nm). Other animals see beyond this band: many insects and birds can see ultraviolet, and some species of snakes can see infrared.

![The electromagnetic spectrum on a logarithmic wavelength scale, with the narrow visible band expanded from violet at 350 nm to red at 700 nm; ultraviolet is visible to many insects and birds and infrared to some snakes](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g4-em-spectrum.svg)

**Dogs** see the spectrum differently from people. To a person the spectrum runs from red (700 nm) through orange, yellow, green (500 nm) and blue to violet (400 nm). To a dog the long wavelengths look dark brown shading into pale yellow, the middle of the spectrum (around 500 nm) looks greyish white, and the short wavelengths look light to dark blue. The slide’s photographs show a puppy among red flowers and some dog toys, each as a person and as a dog would see them: in the dog’s version the reds and greens turn into muddy yellows and browns, while blues and yellows stay distinct.

**Cats** have:

- poor distance vision, about 20/100 to 20/200 (a cat must be 20 feet away to see what a person with normal vision sees from 100 to 200 feet);
- a wider visual field, 200° as opposed to 180° in humans;
- great night vision;
- less colour vision than humans.

The slide shows street and city scenes in pairs, as a person sees them and as a cat would: the cat’s versions are wider, blurrier at a distance, much less colourful, and much brighter at night.

## The eye

![Cross-section of the eye: light from an object passes through the cornea, the pupil in the iris, and the lens (controlled by the ciliary muscle), crosses the vitreous humor and lands upside down on the retina; the fovea sits in the macula, and the optic nerve leaves at the blind spot](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g4-eye.svg)

Light from an object (a pen, in the slide’s drawing) enters the eye through the **cornea** and the **pupil**, the opening in the **iris**. The **lens** focuses it; the **ciliary muscle** controls the lens. The light crosses the **vitreous humor** that fills the eyeball and lands on the **retina**, the layer at the back of the eye that contains the rods and cones. The rays cross inside the eye, so the image on the retina is upside down. The **fovea** is the point of central focus; it lies within the **macula**. The **optic nerve** leaves the back of the eye at the **blind spot**.

## The retina up close

![The layers of the retina: light enters from the ganglion-cell side and passes through the ganglion, amacrine, bipolar and horizontal cells to the rod and cone receptors at the back; ganglion-cell axons form the optic nerve](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g4-retina-layers.svg)

The retina is built in layers. The **rod and cone receptors** (each with an outer and an inner segment) sit at the back. In front of them are the **horizontal cells**, the **bipolar cells**, the **amacrine cells**, and the **ganglion cells**, whose axons run across the retina’s surface and leave the eye as the **optic nerve**. Light enters from the ganglion-cell side, so it passes through the other layers before it reaches the receptors. A stained micrograph on the slide shows the same layers in real tissue: receptors, bipolar cells, ganglion cells, and the axons from the ganglion cells.

## Rods and cones

There are two types of visual receptors.

| | **Rods** | **Cones** |
|---|---|---|
| What they perceive | black and white (they process light, not colours) | colour |
| Detail | poor at resolving details | detect details on objects |
| Where they are | mainly in the periphery of the retina | mainly in the fovea |

The slide’s electron micrograph shows rods as tall cylinders and cones as shorter, tapered cells.

Counting the cone types gives **four kinds of photoreceptors**: rods (black and white) and three types of cones, sensitive to

- long-wavelength light (**red** cones),
- middle-wavelength light (**green** cones),
- short-wavelength light (**blue** cones).

Each receptor responds to a broad range of wavelengths, with peak sensitivity at 437 nm for blue cones, 498 nm for rods, 533 nm for green cones and 564 nm for red cones.

![Relative sensitivity against wavelength from 350 to 700 nm for the four photoreceptors: blue cones peak at 437 nm, rods at 498 nm, green cones at 533 nm and red cones at 564 nm, with broad overlapping curves](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g4-spectral-sensitivity.svg)

:::definition Colour blindness
**Colour blindness** happens when one or more types of receptor doesn’t develop or function properly.

- **Red–green colour blindness**: a disorder of the red or the green cones.
- **Blue–yellow colour blindness**: a disorder of the blue cones.
- **Total colour blindness**: no cones develop or function properly.
:::

The slide shows a column of balloons three ways. With normal vision they are red, orange, yellow, green and pink against a blue sky; with red–green colour blindness the reds and greens all look yellow, olive or grey; with blue–yellow colour blindness the scene turns to pinks, reds and teals.

## Past the retina

![The visual pathway seen from above: fibres from each retina run in the optic nerve to the optic chiasm, where some cross to the other side, then to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and on to the primary visual cortex at the back of the brain; a branch goes to the superior colliculus](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g4-visual-pathway.svg)

From the retina, the **optic nerve** carries signals to the **optic chiasm**, where some fibres from each eye cross to the other side of the brain. They continue to the **lateral geniculate nucleus** (LGN), a region of the thalamus, and from there to the **primary visual cortex (V1)**. The slide’s diagram also shows a branch to the **superior colliculus**.

V1 is at the back of the brain, in the occipital lobe (the slide’s drawing labels the frontal, parietal, occipital and temporal lobes, with the primary visual cortex at the back of the occipital lobe). V1 does two things:

- it gives simple responses to visual stimuli: lines, orientations and movements;
- it routes information to different brain regions for further processing of colour, shape and recognition.

## Two visual pathways: “what” and “where”

The initial theory (1982) proposed two pathways leaving the occipital lobe (the primary visual receiving area):

- a **dorsal pathway** up into the parietal lobe, the “**where**” pathway;
- a **ventral pathway** down into the temporal lobe, the “**what**” pathway.

The evidence came from monkeys with parts of the brain removed, tested on two tasks.

:::example Object discrimination task
In phase one, the monkey sees a single object (a cone-shaped cover) with a reward hidden under it. In phase two there are two different objects, a cone and a cylinder, and the reward is under the cone again. The monkey has to work out under **what** object the reward is hidden. Monkeys with the **inferior temporal lobe** removed failed at this task.
:::

:::example Landmark discrimination task
There are two identical cone-shaped covers and a landmark (a cylinder), and the reward is hidden under the cover nearer the landmark. The monkey has to work out **where** the reward is hidden. Monkeys with the **parietal lobe** removed failed at this task.
:::

![The two monkey tasks: in object discrimination the reward stays under the same object (a cone) when a cylinder is added; in landmark discrimination the reward is under whichever of two identical cones is nearer the cylinder](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g4-monkey-tasks.svg)

The slides show a side view of the monkey brain with the visual areas V1, V2, V4, MT and STP marked, along with the inferior temporal cortex (IT), which is the area removed for the object task, and the parietal area (P), removed for the landmark task. So the object task lined up with the ventral “what” pathway and the landmark task with the dorsal “where” pathway. But this theory was not fully correct.

## “What” and “how”: Milner and Goodale (1995)

Milner and Goodale (1995) drew a more nuanced distinction between the pathways. They agreed that the ventral stream is the “**what**” stream. But they argued that the dorsal stream is a “**how**” stream, not a “where” stream: it is specialized in location *and* in how to direct actions. Their account was based on a patient, **D.F.**, who had a brain lesion to the ventral pathway.

### Dissociations

Behavioural functions may be present in some cases and absent in others. The same movement can come from different contexts. Raising your arm or hand, for example, can mean showing how many, showing that you know the answer or want to speak in a large audience, or waving at someone. These are products of different neural channels, so after a brain lesion you may be able to elicit the response in one context but not in others. (Another example from the slide: touching your nose.)

![Three contexts (show how many; show you know the answer or want to speak in a large audience; wave at someone) all lead to raising your arm or hand; a lesion cuts the channel from one context while the others still work](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g4-arm-contexts.svg)

:::definition Double dissociation
Strictly, a **double dissociation** needs two cases with opposite patterns: damage to one area impairs function A but spares function B, and damage to another area impairs B but spares A. This shows that A and B depend on separate neural channels. D.F., whose ventral damage spared her visually guided actions while impairing recognition, and patients with visual ataxia (below), whose damage spares recognition while impairing visually guided movement, together make such a pair.
:::

### Case study: D.F.

D.F. had a visual form of **agnosia** (an inability to recognize what one perceives). She could describe the basic forms and colours of objects but was unable to identify or categorize them. She could identify objects by touch, but not by vision.

The slide’s figure (from Milner and Goodale, 1995) shows model drawings of an apple and an open book, D.F.’s attempts to copy them, and her drawings of the same objects from memory. Her copies are unrecognizable fragments of lines and dots, while her drawings from memory are recognizable as an apple and a book. Yet she could not recognize what her own drawings were.

### Matching versus posting

D.F. was tested with a slot in a disc on the face of a box, in two tasks: in **static orientation matching** she had to judge the slot’s orientation and match it; in **active “posting”** she had to put a card through the slot, like mailing a letter.

![The slot apparatus, and the results of the two tasks: each line is one attempt, with the slot’s orientation at the top. In matching, D.F.’s lines point in all directions while the control’s cluster near the slot’s orientation; in posting, D.F.’s lines cluster near it just like the control’s](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g4-orientation-tasks.svg)

Each line in the results shows the orientation of one attempt, with the slot’s true orientation marked at the top. In matching, D.F.’s attempts point in every direction, while a person without brain damage matches the slot almost exactly. In posting, D.F.’s attempts cluster around the correct orientation, nearly as tightly as the control’s.

So D.F. could not perceive and match “static” orientation models, but she could produce the right orientation when imitating an actual gesture such as mailing a letter: she did better when action was involved and the task was put in context. This suggests two mechanisms:

- one mechanism for judging orientation: the **ventral pathway**, “**what**” (damaged in D.F.);
- one mechanism for coordinating vision and action: the **dorsal pathway**, “**how**” (intact in D.F.).

### The currently accepted theory

In the currently accepted theory, the ventral pathway into the temporal lobe is the “what” pathway (the object discrimination task), and the dorsal pathway into the parietal lobe is the “how” pathway (the posting task).

![The two theories side by side on a side view of the brain: from the occipital lobe, the ventral pathway runs to the temporal lobe (“what”) in both, and the dorsal pathway runs to the parietal lobe, called “where” in the initial 1982 theory and “how” in the currently accepted theory](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g4-two-streams.svg)

## When recognition or action fails

### Visual agnosia

:::definition Apperceptive and associative visual agnosia
In **apperceptive visual agnosia**, a person cannot recognize objects by shape and cannot copy drawings.

In **associative visual agnosia**, a person can copy images but cannot say what the image is, and has difficulty describing visual stimuli.
:::

The slide shows (a) an apperceptive patient’s copies of letters and numbers (A, B, X, O, 3, 4) and of filled shapes (a circle, a square, a diamond, a T-shape, a key), all of them unrecognizable scribbles; and (b) an associative patient’s copies of a tea bag, a ring and a pen, all accurate.

### Visual ataxia

**Visual ataxia** impairs visuospatial skills, which obstructs visually guided movement, and it impairs attention. Together these make up **Balint’s syndrome**. The slide’s photograph shows a man reaching for a spoon held up in front of him.

### Prosopagnosia

**Prosopagnosia** (“facial blindness”) is an inability to recognize faces. It comes from damage to the **fusiform face area**, which lies in the fusiform gyrus on the underside of the temporal lobe, along the ventral pathway. It commonly co-occurs with apperceptive visual agnosia. The slide’s photograph shows a family with their faces blurred to blank skin, suggesting what faces can look like to someone with prosopagnosia.

## Check yourself

:::question Cones (memory check)
Cones respond to…

1. Black/white
2. Color
3. Peripheral vision
4. Movement
:::solution
2, colour. Cones handle colour perception and detail and are concentrated in the fovea; black-and-white vision and the periphery belong to the rods.
:::
:::

:::question Peripheral vision (memory check)
What best describes peripheral vision?

1. High acuity, low color
2. High acuity, high color
3. Low acuity, low color
4. Low acuity, high color
:::solution
3, low acuity and low colour. The periphery is mostly rods, which are poor at resolving details and process light rather than colour.
:::
:::

:::question The ventral stream (memory check)
The ventral stream processes…

1. Object recognition
2. Object location
3. Visually-guided movement
4. Color
:::solution
1, object recognition. The ventral stream is the “what” pathway into the temporal lobe; monkeys without the inferior temporal lobe fail the object discrimination task, and D.F., with ventral damage, couldn’t recognize objects by sight.
:::
:::

:::question The dorsal stream (memory check)
The dorsal stream processes…

1. Object recognition
2. Object location
3. Visually-guided movement
4. Color
:::solution
3, visually guided movement, along with 2, object location. In Milner and Goodale’s account the dorsal “how” stream is specialized in location and in how to direct actions.
:::
:::

:::question A patient after a fall (exit discussion)
You’re a neurologist who has been brought a patient who had a bad fall and appears to have suffered brain damage. After running a few tests, you find the patient has these symptoms:

- perfectly normal acuity and colour perception;
- when shown objects, can name and describe them;
- is unable to draw objects from memory;
- when asked to reach for an object in your hand, could not;
- was not able to place an object in your hand.

What part of the brain has likely suffered damage? And what condition do you suspect they may have?
:::solution
Recognition is intact (the ventral “what” pathway works), but visually guided movement (reaching, placing, drawing) fails, which points to damage to the dorsal “how” pathway in the parietal lobe. The likely condition is visual ataxia (part of Balint’s syndrome).
:::
:::
