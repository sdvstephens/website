The objectives for this lecture were to understand the function of the inner ear in both hearing and balance, to describe how one sense can be re-routed to detect another (“sensory substitution”), and to critically evaluate synaptic plasticity in sensation and perception.

## The vestibular system

:::definition Vestibular sense
The **vestibular sense** is our sense of balance and orientation. (In the lecturer’s words, “vestibular” is just a sciency way of saying balance.)
:::

The vestibular system provides sensory information about how the head is moving. The slide’s drawing shows a head with three axes through it and names the rotation around each one:

- **roll**: rotation around the $x$ axis, which runs from front to back through the face;
- **pitch**: rotation around the $y$ axis, which runs from ear to ear;
- **yaw**: rotation around the $z$ axis, which runs from the top of the head down.

![Three axes through the head: rotation around the front-to-back x axis is roll, around the ear-to-ear y axis is pitch, and around the vertical z axis is yaw](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g7-head-axes.svg)

### The inner ear

The inner ear has two functions:

1. hearing;
2. the vestibular sense (balance and orientation).

The slide’s drawing is a cutaway of the ear in three parts: the **outer ear** (the visible ear and the external auditory canal), the **middle ear**, and the **inner ear**, which sits deep in the bone of the skull and is outlined on the slide. The inner ear holds the looping semicircular canals, the coiled cochlea, and the nerves that leave them.

### The semicircular canals

The **semicircular canals** are the vestibular sensory organ. The slide’s drawing of the inner ear divides it in two. The **vestibular system** is the three semicircular canals (labeled superior, posterior and horizontal) and the chambers at their base; the coiled **cochlea** is for **hearing**. A nerve carries signals from both to the brain.

The semicircular canals are filled with fluid, with one canal per orientation ($x$, $y$, $z$). As the lecturer put it, there are basically just three tubes of fluid in the inner ear, one for each of the three axes we can turn around: we move, and the fluid splashes around in the corresponding tube.

## Hair cells

**Hair cells** are the sensory cells of the vestibular system. They are located inside the semicircular canals and detect movement of the fluid.

Neurons come in different “flavors,” and the slide lines a hair cell up against two familiar ones: a bipolar neuron (one dendrite, a cell body and an axon) and a multipolar neuron with a myelinated axon. The hair cell has the same parts, laid out differently. Its hair-like projections stand in the place of the dendrites, its body (with a stiff **cuticular plate** along the top and a nucleus inside) is the cell body, and its base, where it passes its signal on, stands in the place of the axon. Strictly, hair cells are specialized receptor cells: a hair cell has no axon of its own and passes its signal across a synapse to a neuron of the vestibular nerve, which carries it to the brain.

![A neuron with dendrites, cell body and axon beside a hair cell: the hair cell’s hair-like projections stand in the place of the dendrites, its body is the cell body, and its base, which passes the signal on, stands in the place of the axon](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g7-hair-cell.svg)

### How hair cells are activated

Hair cells are activated by physical movement in a salt solution. The fluid in the canals is a salt solution, and the hair-like projections are *physically* attached to ion channels: a fine link runs from the tip of each projection to a channel in its taller neighbor. When the fluid moves, it bends the projections. Depending on the direction of tilt, the movement leads to **depolarization** or **hyperpolarization**:

- tilted toward the tallest projection, the links pull the channels open, positive ions flow into the cell, and the hair cell depolarizes (the same direction of change as at the start of an action potential, when Na$^+$ enters a neuron);
- tilted the other way, the channels close and the hair cell hyperpolarizes.

The depolarized hair cell then passes the signal on to the vestibular nerve, whose neurons fire action potentials to the brain. So when fluid sloshes over these cells, signals go out to the brain.

![Two bundles of hair-like projections in a staircase of heights: tilted toward the tallest, the links pull the channels at the tips open and positive ions flow in (depolarization); tilted the other way, the links go slack and the channels close (hyperpolarization)](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g7-hair-bundle.svg)

## The vestibular pathway

The neural pathway for the vestibular sense runs:

$$\text{inner ear (semicircular canals)} \longrightarrow \text{medulla} \longrightarrow \text{cerebellum}$$

The slide marks the medulla and the cerebellum on a photograph of a brain cut down the middle: the medulla is the lowest part of the brainstem, just above the spinal cord, and the cerebellum is the ridged structure behind it. The lecturer said this pathway doesn’t need to be memorized; it is there because it becomes relevant later in the lecture (in sensory substitution).

### Off balance

## Sensory substitution

### Paul Bach-y-Rita

**Paul Bach-y-Rita** (1934–2006) was, among other things, a factory worker, a fisherman and a masseur, and he attended medical school on a dare. He is recognized as the pioneer of **sensory substitution**. In his words: “We see with our brains, not with our eyes.”

:::definition Sensory substitution
**Sensory substitution** is using one sense to detect another: information that would normally come in through one sense organ is delivered through a different one, and the brain learns to perceive it.
:::

### Vision through the skin (1969)

Bach-y-Rita’s paper “Vision Substitution by Tactile Image Projection” appeared in *Nature* (vol. 221, March 8, 1969). Its first sentence: “We describe here a vision substitution system which is being developed as a practical aid for the blind and as a means of studying the processing of afferent information in the central nervous system.” (*Afferent* information is incoming sensory information.)

The slide’s photo shows the apparatus: a modified dental chair with a TV camera on a stand beside it and a panel of stimulators in the backrest. The camera’s image was turned into a pattern of vibrations on the person’s back, each stimulator acting as one pixel. A figure from the paper shows a 400-point representation of a woman’s face as it appeared on the monitor (a coarse, blurry grid of light and dark dots), next to a photograph of the face itself. The caption says that subjects could correctly identify vibrotactile stimulus patterns (patterns of vibration felt on the skin) of this level of complexity; it also notes that the blurry image on the monitor is easier to make out if you squint. In the lecturer’s summary, he put an image onto someone’s back with the stimulators as pixels, and it worked reasonably well.

### Balance through the tongue

The vision-chair idea was later applied to balance. The device stimulates the tongue electrically to provide vestibular information: it “tingles” the direction of a person’s tilt onto the tongue. It takes some practice to use, but it can give feedback about a person’s balance from a pocket-sized device.

The slide’s photo shows the device (its label reads “BrainPort Balance Device”). A small **intra-oral device** (IOD) sits on the tongue; it has an **electrode array** on one side and an **accelerometer** (a sensor of tilt and movement) on the other. A cable carrying the circuitry connects it to a handheld unit that holds the CPU, battery and electronics, with a power button and a voltage control.

The electrode array is a grid of electrodes on the tongue, and where the stimulation lands on the grid tells the user how the head is tilted. With the head upright the stimulation sits in the center (position 2). Tilting the head to one side or the other moves it to the left or right edge (positions 1 and 3), tilting back moves it toward the back of the tongue (position 4), and tilting forward moves it toward the tip (position 5).

![A grid of electrodes on the tongue with five positions marked: center (2) for the head upright, left and right edges (1 and 3) for the head tilted to either side, toward the back (4) for the head tilted back, and toward the tip (5) for the head tilted forward](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g7-tongue-array.svg)

### Why it works: shared pathways

The pathways for taste and for balance are different, but they share some architecture: both run through the medulla.

- **Taste/touch:** tongue $\to$ medulla (**solitary nucleus**) $\to$ thalamus, cortex, hypothalamus and amygdala $\to$ taste/touch.
- **Balance:** inner ear $\to$ medulla (**vestibular nucleus**) $\to$ cerebellum and the eye and head muscles $\to$ balance.

When the inner ear is damaged, the link from the inner ear to the vestibular nucleus is lost (the slide crosses it out). Through **synaptic plasticity**, some synapses in the shared location, the medulla, are rearranged so that information from the tongue reaches the vestibular nucleus, and from there the areas that process balance. In this sense the tongue substitutes for the semicircular canals.

![Two pathways side by side: tongue to medulla (solitary nucleus) to thalamus, cortex, hypothalamus and amygdala to taste/touch; inner ear to medulla (vestibular nucleus) to cerebellum and eye and head muscles to balance. The inner-ear link is crossed out as damaged, and a new connection from the solitary nucleus to the vestibular nucleus, made by synaptic plasticity, carries tongue input to balance](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g7-substitution.svg)

### “Seeing” with your tongue

The tongue can also be used to see, to a degree. The slide’s diagram (sources: Mitchel Tyler, Yuri Danilov, Wicab Inc.) follows the signal from the tongue to the brain. Electronic aids send impulses to a sensitive body part like the tongue, and the brain interprets them, enabling a blind person to “see” objects.

1. A camera on the forehead captures the image of a test symbol (an X).
2. The video output is sent to a processor.
3. The processor translates the camera’s output into a pattern of electronic pulses, which are sent to an array of electrodes held against the tongue.
4. The array, a little over an inch square, stimulates receptor cells on the surface of the tongue.

Tactile (“touch”) receptors on the tongue send impulses to the **somatosensory cortex** in response to the stimulation. The impulses are then relayed to areas of the cortex that process images, the **visual cortex** and the **visual association cortex**. In the diagram’s account, the images are analyzed and sent to the **prefrontal cortex**, where they are recognized, and the person perceives the object (the X).

![Flowchart: a camera on the forehead captures the test symbol, a processor turns it into electrical pulses, an electrode array on the tongue stimulates touch receptors, nerves carry impulses to the somatosensory cortex, they are relayed to the visual and visual association cortex, and the prefrontal cortex recognizes the object, so the symbol is perceived](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g7-tongue-vision.svg)

A video (linked on the slide) shows the device in use.

## Hearing: the sound map

Hearing was a quick section of the lecture. The cochlea contains hair cells that respond selectively to particular frequencies of sound, and they are neatly organized by tone. The slides call this “tonographical” organization; the standard term is **tonotopic**. Activation of different cells by different tones builds up our sense of hearing.

The slide’s drawing shows the cochlea “uncoiled” into a straight tube, with the stapes (a middle-ear bone) sitting on the oval window at the **cochlear base**. Seven positions are marked along the tube. The **high-frequency response** is near the base (position 2); the **low-frequency response** is at the far end, the **apex** (position 7).

The **auditory cortex** is tonotopically organized too: the sound map is maintained from the cochlea to the cortex. The slide shows the **primary auditory cortex** on the temporal lobe, surrounded by **secondary auditory cortex** (the “belt areas”). Across the primary auditory cortex lie bands responding to 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000 and 16,000 Hz; the 500 Hz end corresponds to the apex of the cochlea and the 16,000 Hz end to its base.

![The uncoiled cochlea from base (next to the stapes on the oval window) to apex, with positions 1 to 7: high frequencies respond near the base and low frequencies near the apex. Below, the primary auditory cortex has bands from 16,000 Hz down to 500 Hz; the base maps to the 16,000 Hz end and the apex to the 500 Hz end](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g7-tonotopy.svg)

Because the same map is kept all the way up, a device that delivers the right frequencies to the right places along the pathway can stand in for lost hair cells. This is what a cochlear implant does.

## Hearing loss and cochlear implants

There are two types of hearing loss:

1. **Conductive hearing loss**: damage to the parts of the ear that conduct sound into the inner ear (the outer and middle ear).
2. **Sensorineural hearing loss**: damage to the hair cells.

**Cochlear implants** work in three steps:

1. A small **microphone** on the **headpiece** (worn behind the ear) picks up sounds.
2. The sounds are converted to electrical signals by the **implantable cochlear stimulator** and fed into the cochlea.
3. An **electrode array** threaded through the round window into the cochlea stimulates the **auditory nerve** directly, bypassing the damaged hair cells.

So an implant helps with sensorineural hearing loss: it does the damaged hair cells’ job.

:::question The dorsal and ventral streams (memory test, from Lecture 7)
Describe the functional roles of the **dorsal** and **ventral** streams of visual processing. What happens when either one of these pathways is damaged? What symptoms would you expect to see in patients with damage to these regions? (Hint from the lecturer: think back to agnosia and ataxia. Which is which?)
:::solution
The ventral stream runs from the occipital lobe into the temporal lobe and is the “what” pathway: object recognition. Damage to it causes visual agnosia, an inability to recognize objects by sight (like D.F.), and damage to the fusiform face area along it causes prosopagnosia. The dorsal stream runs into the parietal lobe and is the “how” pathway: location and visually guided action. Damage to it causes visual ataxia, where a patient can recognize an object but cannot reach for it or act on it accurately. So agnosia goes with the ventral stream and ataxia with the dorsal stream.
:::
:::

:::question Detecting balance (memory check)
How do we detect our sense of balance?

1. Via depolarization of hair cells
2. Through axons projecting from the inner ear to the hindbrain
3. Integration of visual information with input from the inner ear
4. All of the above

4, all of the above. Fluid movement bends the hair cells’ projections and depolarizes them (1); the signal travels from the inner ear to the medulla in the hindbrain (2); and balance also depends on combining this input with vision (3), which is why the vestibular pathway feeds the eye muscles as well as the cerebellum.

:::question Recap: sensory substitution (exit discussion)
What is sensory substitution? *How* does sensory substitution allow one sense to be perceived through another? How does synaptic plasticity play a role in this?
:::solution
Sensory substitution is using one sense to detect another. The information comes in through the substituting sense (in the balance device, touch on the tongue) and travels along that sense’s pathway. Where that pathway shares structure with the lost sense’s pathway (both pass through the medulla), synaptic plasticity rearranges synapses so that the tongue’s input reaches the vestibular nucleus and the areas that process balance. With practice, the brain comes to perceive the tongue’s signal as balance information.
:::
:::
