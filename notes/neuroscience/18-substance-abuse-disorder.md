This lecture has three learning objectives:

1. Understand the distinction between substance use, abuse, dependence and addiction.
2. Describe key models of addiction formation.
3. Explain the role of dopamine receptor subtypes in addiction.

It opens with a memory test carried over from the previous lecture on pharmaceuticals: benzodiazepines treat anxiety very well but carry a very high risk of addiction and overdose, so why are they so addictive, and what are their withdrawal symptoms? That question is collected with the others under “Check yourself” at the end.

## The cost of drug enforcement

The dramatic increases in the cost of drug enforcement have had little effect on drug use. In fact, most addiction rates continue to rise annually. The lecturer’s verdict on the “war on drugs” (the US government’s campaign against illegal drugs, begun in the early 1970s): “War on drugs was a bust. They changed the name recently as a rebranding, but it’s still a bust.”

The slide’s chart plots two lines from 1970 to 2010. US drug control spending rises from under 1 billion dollars a year in 1970 to about 20 billion dollars a year in 2010, climbing most steeply from the mid-1980s to the early 1990s; the area under the spending curve is labeled with its total, 1.5 trillion dollars. Over the same forty years the US drug addiction rate stays flat, at between about 1% and 1.5%.

## Overdose deaths are rising

The next chart is headed “Drug overdose deaths are rising.” The lecture gives its key figure: around 75,000 people died from overdoses of synthetic opioids, such as fentanyl, in the 12 months leading up to November 2023. (Opioids are drugs that act on the same receptors as morphine; synthetic opioids are made entirely in the laboratory.) The chart plots deaths over the past year for each type of drug, from 2015 to the end of 2023:

- **synthetic opioids** other than methadone climb from about 6,000 to about 75,000, most steeply from 2019 to 2022;
- **psychostimulants with abuse potential** (mainly methamphetamine) climb from about 5,000 to about 36,000;
- **cocaine** climbs from about 6,000 to about 30,000;
- **natural and semisynthetic opioids** (morphine, codeine and prescription painkillers such as oxycodone) stay between about 10,000 and 14,000;
- **heroin** peaks at about 16,000 around 2017 and falls to about 4,000;
- **methadone** stays low, at about 3,000 to 4,000.

## What is addiction?

There are two main schools of thought on addiction. In the lecturer’s words, “There’s a bit of a philosophical debate over how we view addiction. There’s pros/cons to either side. This is an ongoing conversation.”

:::definition Brain disease model and learning model
- The **brain disease model** holds that addiction is a chronic, relapsing brain disease (relapsing: it tends to come back after periods of recovery).
- The **learning/developmental model** holds that the brain has learned a devastating habit. Telling someone they have a “broken” or “diseased” brain is unhelpful.
:::

**The brain disease model.**

- *Argument for:* brain imaging shows that addiction severely alters brain areas critical to decision-making, learning and memory, and behavioral control, for long periods of time.
- *Criticism:* it abolishes individual agency by saying people *require* medication to treat a disease, and it undermines the millions who have overcome addiction on their own.

**The learning/developmental model.**

- *Argument for:* successful treatments are built around learning new habits to replace harmful ones.
- *Criticism:* it can add to the stigma of blaming individuals for their addictions, when many cases are not the fault of the patient.

## An important distinction

The lecture separates three things: drug **abuse**, drug **dependence** and **addiction**. Simply using a substance is none of these. Abuse and addiction have criteria in the DSM (the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*, the handbook used to diagnose mental disorders).

:::definition Abuse, dependence and addiction
- **Drug abuse** is, in the lecturer’s words, “use when it starts to become a problem.”
- **Drug dependence** is physical and psychological reliance on a drug: the body and brain have come to need it. The lecture counts dependence among the *biological* factors of drug use.
- **Addiction** is defined in the DSM by a set of *behaviors*.
:::

### Substance abuse in the DSM

Substance abuse is diagnosed by **one or more** of the following in a 12-month period:

1. use leading to a failure to fulfill obligations, or to worse performance at work or school;
2. use in hazardous situations;
3. legal problems;
4. continued use despite personal and social problems.

### Addiction in the DSM

Addiction is diagnosed by **three or more** of the following in a 12-month period:

1. tolerance, with a need for increased doses to get the desired effect;
2. withdrawal symptoms (the unpleasant effects of stopping the drug);
3. the substance taken in larger amounts, or over a longer period, than intended;
4. a persistent desire, or unsuccessful efforts, to cut down;
5. a great deal of time spent getting the substance or recovering from it;
6. social or occupational activities given up or reduced because of substance use;
7. use likely to continue despite knowing there is a problem.

These two lists are the criteria of the older DSM-IV, which named the second one *substance dependence*; the current DSM-5 (2013) merges abuse and dependence into a single diagnosis, *substance use disorder*.

The lecturer’s comment: “Addiction is largely defined by a set of behaviors rather than actual dependence, which causes many problems in treatment. Effectively, someone has to get worse before they qualify for treatment to get better.”

## Addiction and dependence

At this point the lecture asks, “What’s missing here?” (see Check yourself). The next slide answers it:

- The criteria for addiction are predominantly **behavioral** factors.
- The criteria do not consider **biological** factors, including physical and psychological dependence on the drug.
- Addiction treatments are focused on these behavioral factors.

The slide ends with a second question, also in Check yourself: what do you do if you are drug-dependent but don’t show these behaviors? According to the lecturer, many treatments are built around people continuing to use and tapering off (lowering the dose step by step) over a period of several years. “But if someone’s dependent on opiates after only a month of prescriptions, what options do they have? It’s a troubling situation where we currently have virtually no support for people who are dependent, and our current system rather waits until they qualify as addicted before providing help. It’s messed up.” (Opiates here means opioid painkillers.)

## A critical gap

- Most cases of dependence and addiction are handled by general practitioners (family doctors) rather than by specialists.
- Nearly all medical facilities deal strictly with inpatient prescriptions, the drugs given while a patient is in the hospital. So none of them oversee or help with tapering and withdrawal once patients are discharged.
- Most specialized facilities offer support only for addiction; they offer none for dependence. For example, opiate addiction is treated with methadone, a long-acting opioid taken in place of the abused drug, with the intention that the patient keeps using it for years.

A critical gap therefore exists for patients who are drug-dependent but may not fully meet the criteria for addiction.

## Drug tolerance

:::definition Tolerance
**Tolerance** is a weakening response to a drug with repeated use, so that a larger dose is needed for the same effect. The lecture describes two kinds.
- **Physiological tolerance:** the liver gets more efficient at breaking down the drug before it gets to the brain.
- **Cellular tolerance:** plasticity in nerve cells makes them less responsive to the drug, through cell death, receptor downregulation (the cells remove some of their receptors for the drug) and decreased neurotransmitter release.
:::

The slide pictures physiological tolerance as a flow: a thick arrow labeled “amount of drug in blood” leads into a drawing of the liver inside the torso, and a thinner arrow leads out to a second “amount of drug in blood,” now smaller.

### Morphine tolerance

In the experiment shown, a hot light is directed at a rat’s tail, and the time the rat takes to flick its tail away from the light (the tail-flick latency) is a measure of pain: the longer it waits, the less pain it feels. Morphine decreases pain, but its pain-killing action is lost after several days of treatment.

![Line graph of tail-flick latency against days: rats treated with morphine take about 10 seconds to flick their tails away from the hot light on day 1, about 7 seconds on day 5 and only 3 to 4 seconds on day 9, approaching the roughly 2.3 seconds of rats given no morphine (dashed line); a longer latency means less pain felt.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g14-morphine-tolerance.svg)

On day 1 the morphine-treated rats take about 10 seconds to flick their tails; by day 9 they take only about 3 to 4 seconds. The dashed line is the response of rats not given any morphine, at about 2.3 seconds. The chart plots two groups of morphine-treated rats, which fall together; the slide does not say how the groups differ.

## Sensitization

:::definition Sensitization
**Sensitization** is essentially the opposite of tolerance: with repeated use, the same dose of a drug produces a much stronger action.
:::

If that action is the motivation to take drugs, continued drug use makes it much more difficult to quit. In most cases, people form

- **tolerance** to the desirable effects, and
- **sensitization** to the undesirable effects.

For example, use of opiates develops tolerance to their pain-relieving effects, but it sensitizes pain receptors, which makes withdrawal worse, and the respiratory system becomes sensitized to respiratory depression (dangerously slowed breathing).

Sensitization is “a little weird to think about,” in the lecturer’s words. Tolerance to the high does tend to increase, so a chronic user still needs more of the drug to get high. What can be sensitized are many of the negative effects that bear on overdose or withdrawal: the heart or lungs being more and more affected by the drug, stronger withdrawal symptoms, and increased cravings.

### Amphetamine sensitization

Amphetamine increases activity in rats. In the experiment shown, activity is counted as crossovers, the number of times a rat crosses from one part of its cage to another. Each rat was injected with saline (salt water, a control with no drug) at the start of the session and with amphetamine at 60 minutes, both straight into the nucleus accumbens, part of the brain’s reward system (the chart labels these injections “intra-ACC”).

![Line graph of crossovers against time over 120 minutes: after saline is injected into the nucleus accumbens at the start, activity falls to near zero in both groups; after amphetamine is injected at 60 minutes, rats with prior exposure to amphetamine peak at about 43 crossovers, against about 31 for rats getting amphetamine for the first time (F = 5.79, p < 0.02).](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g14-amphetamine-sensitization.svg)

Animals with prior exposure to amphetamine (black circles) show **greater** activity in response to amphetamine than rats getting it for the first time (white circles). Activity peaks 20 minutes after the injection, at about 43 crossovers in the experienced rats against about 31 in the first-time rats, and the experienced rats stay more active to the end of the session. The chart gives the statistical test as F = 5.79, p < 0.02, which means a difference this large would turn up by chance less than 2% of the time if prior exposure made no difference. These effects are present even 1 year after first receiving amphetamine.

## Tolerance to pleasure, sensitization to harm

The lecture sums this up: generally, we see **tolerance** to the pleasurable aspects of drugs, but **sensitization** to the unpleasant aspects.

### But why?

Drugs activate different types of dopamine receptors.

:::definition D1 and D2 receptors (as the lecture presents them)
- The **D1 receptor** carries “wanting,” or “needing.”
- The **D2 receptor** carries “liking.”
:::

Addictive drugs **sensitize**, or increase, D1 receptors and **desensitize**, or decrease, D2 receptors. Over time, the response to the drug shifts from “liking” to “needing.”

The lecturer explains: “D1 you can think of as your first order priorities. We say it’s associated with ‘wanting’, but I think ‘needing’ is more accurate.” D1 receptors are activated when you breathe after holding your breath, when you satisfy caloric needs, when you drink water, and so on. D2 is about actually liking, or enjoying, things: “If I eat oreos the calories and not starving will activate D1, while the taste activates D2.” In long-term addiction people feel that they need the drug even when they no longer “like” it, because the response to drug use has shifted from D2 to D1 receptors. (In standard accounts dopamine drives “wanting” and has little to do with “liking,” which depends mainly on the brain’s own opioids; the drop in D2 receptors in people with addiction is well documented.)

The slide shows a brain in side view. Broad gray arrows labeled “wanting” carry dopamine from the midbrain up to the striatum, nucleus accumbens, prefrontal cortex and amygdala, while a thin white “liking” path links a few small circled spots: one in the front of the brain, the nucleus accumbens, the ventral pallidum and the brainstem. Beside it is a graph of the two over time.

![Graph of relative effect against time, from initial use to addiction: “wanting” (D1 receptors sensitized) climbs steeply in an S-shaped curve, while “liking” (D2 receptors desensitized) drifts slightly below its level at first use.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g14-wanting-liking.svg)

## Two models for addiction

### The impulse disorder cycle

The first model is the **impulse disorder cycle**:

1. Do it for fun.
2. Feel bad or get anxious.
3. Try it again to remove the adverse effects.
4. Eventually develop dependence.

The lecturer’s view of it: “I don’t like this model. It’s not very informative. But I’m including it because it’s generally accepted. The second is more informative though.”

The slide’s diagram, drawn over a side view of a head, shows the cycle as three repeating stages, each tied to a brain region:

- **binge/intoxication**, tied to the **basal ganglia**, a group of structures deep in the brain (including the striatum and nucleus accumbens) involved in movement, habits and reward;
- **withdrawal/negative affect**, where negative affect means bad mood such as anxiety and irritability, tied to the **extended amygdala**, the amygdala together with closely linked neighboring regions;
- **preoccupation/anticipation**, the craving for and planning of the next use, tied to the **prefrontal cortex**.

![The impulse disorder cycle: binge/intoxication (basal ganglia) leads to withdrawal/negative affect (extended amygdala), which leads to preoccupation/anticipation (prefrontal cortex), which leads back to binge/intoxication.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g14-addiction-cycle.svg)

### The opponent-process model

The second model is the **opponent-process model**:

- use brings an alternation between highs and lows;
- over time, the highs become lower, the lows become deeper, and the baseline decreases.

:::definition Opponent process
In the **opponent-process** model, each drug-induced high (the *a* process) is followed by an opposing low (the *b* process).
- The **homeostatic point** is the normal resting level of mood (homeostasis is the body’s steady balance).
- **Allostatic points** are the new, lower resting levels that mood settles at between uses. The growing gap between them and the original baseline is the **allostatic state** (allostasis is keeping stable by shifting to a new set point).
:::

The slide’s diagram plots mood over time. After one use, mood rises above the baseline (*a*, +) and then dips below it (*b*, −) before recovering. With repeated use, each *a* peak is lower and each *b* trough deeper, and between uses mood settles at allostatic points that drift further and further below the original homeostatic point.

![Two graphs of mood. One use: mood rises above the baseline (a) and then dips below it (b). Repeated use: each high is lower and each low deeper, and between uses mood settles at allostatic points that fall further and further below the original homeostatic point; the widening shaded gap is the allostatic state.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g14-opponent-process.svg)

The lecturer: “Over time highs get less high, lows get lower, and eventually peoples’ baseline gradually decreases. In the long-term it can reach the point where even when using the drug they don’t feel as well as they did at baseline before addiction. It’s a horrible cycle.” In time people may take the drug only to relieve the withdrawal symptoms, without even “liking” it.

## Check yourself

The first question reviews the previous lecture.

:::question Memory test
Benzodiazepines are GABA agonists in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala. While very effective at treating anxiety, they carry a very high addiction and overdose risk. Why are they so addictive? And what are the withdrawal symptoms?

:::solution
**Why so addictive.** All addictive drugs trigger dopamine release, and benzodiazepines are no exception. They also work very well against anxiety in the short term, by increasing GABA activity in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, though they are not particularly effective in the long term; in the previous lecture’s words they are “very, very addictive.” This lecture’s impulse disorder cycle fits them closely: a drug that removes anxiety quickly invites taking it again when the anxiety returns.

**Withdrawal symptoms.** Insomnia, anxiety, depression, depersonalization (feeling detached from oneself, as if watching from outside), nausea, hallucinations, seizures and even death. Many of these are the drug’s calming effects in reverse: without the boosted GABA, anxiety and sleeplessness return and neurons become overexcitable, which can bring seizures. The previous lecture also notes high rates of abuse in pop culture, an 89% increase in hospital visits, and that 17% of drug overdose deaths are from benzodiazepines.
:::
:::

:::question Exit discussion
Neural plasticity plays a key role in the development of addiction. Discuss several internal and external factors that may contribute to the development of addiction. Also, consider how Hebbian principles play a role.

:::solution
**Internal factors.** Cellular tolerance (cell death, receptor downregulation, decreased neurotransmitter release) and physiological tolerance in the liver mean that more drug is needed for the same high. Sensitization strengthens the drive to take the drug, the withdrawal and the cravings. Addictive drugs increase D1 and decrease D2 receptors, shifting use from “liking” to “needing.” In the opponent-process model the baseline mood keeps falling, until people take the drug only to relieve withdrawal.

**External factors.** A short course of prescribed opiates can be enough to cause dependence; the care system offers little help with tapering and withdrawal and waits for addiction before treating; potent synthetic opioids such as fentanyl have driven overdose deaths up; and rising enforcement spending has done little to curb use.

**Hebbian principles** (from the lecture on learning and memory: “neurons that fire together wire together”). *Use it to improve it:* each dose is an intense, repeated stimulus to the dopamine reward system, which strengthens the synapses linking the drug to the urge to take it, and changes this strong can last, as amphetamine sensitization does for a year. *Use it or lose it:* activities given up for the drug lose their connections, and overcoming addiction depends on weakening drug-linked synapses while building new habits, as the learning model of addiction says. *Regional and functional specificity:* the changes concentrate in the reward circuits the drug acts on.
:::
:::
