This lecture has three goals: to describe the steps of an action potential, to explain the key properties of action potentials, and to discuss how changes in action potentials affect the brain and behavior.

## Anatomy of a neuron

A neuron receives signals on its **dendrites**, the branching extensions around the cell body. The **soma** (cell body) contains the **nucleus**. From the soma runs a single long **axon**, which is wrapped along its length in **myelin sheaths**. The axon ends in **axon terminals**, which pass the message on to other neurons. A message always travels in one direction through the cell: dendrites, then soma, then axon, then axon terminals.

![A neuron with labeled dendrites, soma, nucleus, axon, myelin sheaths, nodes of Ranvier and axon terminals; an arrow above shows the message traveling from the dendrites toward the axon terminals.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g2-neuron-anatomy.svg)

A second diagram of the same parts, used in the review questions, adds two more labels. It marks each segment of myelin as a **Schwann cell**, and it marks the short gaps of bare axon between neighboring segments as **nodes of Ranvier**.

## The action potential: it’s just ions moving around

At rest, the inside of a neuron is negative relative to the outside. Its **resting potential** is about $-70$ mV. An animation in the lecture shows this along an axon, with a row of + charges on the outside of the membrane and a row of − charges on the inside.

What changes the voltage is the movement of two ions across the membrane:

- At rest, **sodium** (Na$^+$) is concentrated *outside* the cell. You put salt on the *outside* of your food.
- At rest, **potassium** (K$^+$) is concentrated *inside* the cell. Bananas are *inside* a peel.

When a neuron is activated, these two ions temporarily trade places: Na$^+$ flows into the cell and K$^+$ flows out. The lecture’s figure here shows the **sodium–potassium pump**, a protein in the cell membrane that carries Na$^+$ and K$^+$ ions across it.

The action potential plays out in five steps, over a few milliseconds:

1. **Stimulus.** Something pushes the membrane potential up from its resting level of $-70$ mV. If the push does not carry the potential up to the **threshold** of $-55$ mV, the potential just sinks back to rest. The graph shows these small bumps as “failed initiations.”
2. **Depolarization.** Once the potential reaches threshold, Na$^+$ ions rush into the cell and the potential shoots up past 0 mV to a peak of about $+30$ to $+40$ mV (the two graphs in the lecture differ).
3. **Repolarization.** K$^+$ ions flow out of the cell and the potential falls back down.
4. **Hyperpolarization.** The potential overshoots, dipping below the resting level for a moment.
5. **Resting state.** The potential returns to $-70$ mV, ready for the next action potential.

![Graph of voltage (mV) against time (ms) showing an action potential: rest at −70 mV, a stimulus, failed initiations that stay below the −55 mV threshold, depolarization as Na+ ions enter up to a peak of +40 mV, repolarization as K+ ions leave, hyperpolarization below −70 mV, and return to the resting state; the five phases are numbered.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g2-action-potential.svg)

:::definition Phases of the action potential
- **Threshold**: the membrane potential, about $-55$ mV, that must be reached for the neuron to fire.
- **Depolarization**: Na$^+$ moves into the cell and the membrane potential rises from threshold to its peak.
- **Repolarization**: K$^+$ moves out of the cell and the membrane potential falls back toward rest.
- **Hyperpolarization**: the membrane potential dips below the resting level of $-70$ mV before recovering.
:::

## Properties of action potentials

The lecture lists five key properties.

1. **Information is communicated when a neuron fires.** The action potential travels from the cell body along the axon. The lecture illustrates this with an action potential moving along a myelinated and along an unmyelinated axon (both are drawn in the section on myelin below).
2. **Firing is a result of depolarization and repolarization.** An action potential is the rise and fall of the membrane potential traced above: Na$^+$ in, then K$^+$ out.
3. **All-or-nothing law.** A neuron either fires or it doesn’t. On the graph, a stimulus that stays below threshold produces only a failed initiation, and a stimulus that reaches threshold produces the full action potential.
4. **Action potentials propagate over long distances.** A giraffe makes the point. A motor neuron sits in the giraffe’s spinal cord, and its axon runs all the way down the leg to an axon terminal in the foot. A second panel shows that the long axon also carries signals the other way: molecules released by the target cell (1, 2) are picked up at the axon terminal (3) and carried back up the whole length of the axon to the cell body (4) and into its nucleus (5).
5. **The magnitude of a response is the frequency of firing.** Every action potential has the same size, so a neuron signals a stronger stimulus by firing more often. A weak stimulus raises the firing rate a little while it is on; a strong stimulus makes the neuron fire much faster. When the stimulus turns off, firing drops back to its occasional background rate.

![Two spike trains with a stimulus trace below each: during a weak stimulus the neuron fires a moderate number of action potentials, and during a strong stimulus it fires many more, closely spaced; outside the stimulus it fires only occasionally.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g2-firing-frequency.svg)

:::definition All-or-nothing law
The **all-or-nothing law**: a neuron either fires a full action potential or it does not fire at all. The strength of a response is carried by how often the neuron fires, its **firing frequency**, since the size of each action potential stays the same.
:::

## Tetrodotoxin: puffer fish poison

**Tetrodotoxin**, the poison of the puffer fish, works as a **sodium channel blocker**: it blocks the channels in the membrane through which Na$^+$ enters the neuron. Without Na$^+$ coming in, a neuron cannot depolarize, so it cannot fire. Tetrodotoxin causes full-body paralysis and loss of sensation, and it is more toxic than cyanide. Even so, it has increasing use in clinical and research applications.

:::example Clinical applications of tetrodotoxin
**Treating cancer pain.** A low dose of tetrodotoxin decreases the sensory signals traveling from the periphery to the brain, including pain signals. Side effects include some tingling and mild nausea.

**Treating heroin withdrawal symptoms.** Heroin withdrawal causes severe pain and illness-like symptoms. Within 3 days, micro-doses of tetrodotoxin considerably decreased withdrawal symptoms, with minimal side effects.

Dr. Anh Ho Ngoc is pictured with the cancer-pain work and Dr. Lin Kang with the withdrawal work.
:::

## Myelin

Myelin is **electrical insulation** for the axon, and it **speeds up the rate of transmission**. With the myelin sheath intact, the message moves quickly, at about 400 km/h, hopping from one gap in the myelin to the next like a kangaroo. On an unmyelinated axon the action potential has to spread continuously along the whole membrane, which is slower.

![Two axons. On the myelinated axon, curved arrows show the action potential jumping from one node of Ranvier to the next between myelin sheaths, fast at about 400 km/h; on the unmyelinated axon, a single arrow shows the action potential spreading continuously and more slowly.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g2-myelin-conduction.svg)

### When myelin is missing or damaged

What happens if you don’t make enough myelin, or your myelin is damaged?

:::example Multiple sclerosis (MS)
**Multiple sclerosis (MS)** is a **demyelinating disorder**: the myelin around axons is lost. It is caused by an immune response against myelin or by failure of the cells that produce myelin.

Symptoms:
- impaired sensation
- impaired vision
- muscular weakness
- impaired coordination

MS affects about 2.3 million people worldwide (as of the mid-2010s), close to 1 million of them in the United States.

The lecture’s figure shows a body with a lesion in the spinal cord (part of the central nervous system), causing numbness, tingling and pain below the lesion. Beside it, a normal nerve fiber is wrapped in an intact myelin sheath, while a nerve affected by MS has a damaged, frayed myelin sheath that leaves the nerve fiber exposed.
:::

:::question

- a. More depolarization
- b. More repolarization
- c. More hyperpolarization
- d. Increased firing frequency

:::solution
**d. Increased firing frequency.** By the all-or-nothing law every action potential is the same size, so the strength of a response is carried by how often the neuron fires.
