This lecture opens the course’s section on mental health. It has three learning objectives:

1. Understand the context of mental health treatment history.
2. Describe the neural basis of anxiety and stress disorders.
3. Describe how pharmaceuticals treat these disorders.

## Mental health disorders: considerations

:::definition Clinically significant
A condition is **clinically significant** when it has a profound impact on someone’s life, in multiple domains. Depression, for example, is much more than sadness: it is something prolonged that causes serious impacts in someone’s life.
:::

Two further points frame the topic:

- We don’t know much yet, but we’re working on it.
- While conditions are always improving, mental health is still stigmatized, and as a result many disorders are underrepresented and undertreated in populations.

The slide’s picture is a word cloud of the names of mental disorders (depression, anxiety, PTSD, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, ADHD, OCD, many phobias and more) in the shape of a human head.

## Mental health: a history

### Ancient civilizations

Ancient civilizations described and treated mental health disorders.

- Their accounts were based in religious belief: disorders were the “hands” of deities (the Hand of Ishtar, a Mesopotamian goddess, for example).
- Hallucinations were ascribed spiritual meanings.
- The ancient Greeks first described “melancholy”, “hysteria” and “phobia”.
- Treatments were largely developed in Persia, Arabia and the Islamic empire.

The slide shows two illustrations from medieval Arabic manuscripts: a physician offering a medicinal plant to a patient, and a man preparing a remedy under trees, beside a hanging vessel.

### The Middle Ages to the 19th century

- In the **Middle Ages**, mental illness was put down to the “Devil’s influence”, demonic possession and witchcraft. Through the 17th century, mental illness was viewed as a disconnect from the soul and morality, which led to exceptionally poor treatment of patients.
- The **19th century** was the era of “insane asylums”. Diagnoses and treatments were being widely developed, but with little standardization. The term “psychiatry” was first coined.

The slide shows an 18th-century engraving of an asylum ward: a half-naked patient being chained on the floor, other inmates around the room, and two fashionably dressed women visiting.

### The 20th century

The 20th century brought psychoanalysis, and with it several changes:

- asylums were renamed “hospitals”;
- inmates were renamed “patients”;
- clinical psychology was officially recognized as a profession, driven by World War I, which left many “shell-shocked” veterans.

The first *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders* (DSM) was developed around World War II. The term “stress” was first applied to mental disorders after its first endocrinological description (endocrinology is the study of hormones) in the 1930s. The slide’s photo shows wounded World War I soldiers being bandaged in a trench.

Many new therapies were developed in this period, though with many flaws:

- electroconvulsive therapy (passing an electric current through the brain to set off a seizure);
- insulin shock therapy (putting patients into comas with large doses of insulin);
- lobotomies (surgically cutting the connections of the prefrontal cortex);
- chlorpromazine (an early antipsychotic drug).

The photo shows nurses and a doctor holding a patient down on a bed during a treatment.

Moral and semantic debates about the concept of **mental illness** arose in the 1960s:

- some argued that mental illness was a myth;
- some saw it as a label applied to conformists and non-conformists in societies;
- the APA’s (American Psychiatric Association’s) listing of homosexuality as a mental disorder drew large criticism.

### From the 1970s on

In the 1970s, psychiatric hospitals began to close in favor of community services and true medical services. There was a pharmacological boom:

- “psychic energizers” (antidepressants);
- lithium;
- benzodiazepines.

In the 1990s and 2000s, new therapies and pharmaceuticals were developed:

- cognitive-behavioral therapy;
- a growing emphasis on individualized treatment;
- ongoing reclassification of disorders in the DSM.

The slide shows a pill bottle with tablets spilled around it.

## Anxiety and stress disorders: overview

Anxiety and stress disorders are characterized by significant feelings of anxiety and fear.

:::definition Anxiety and fear
**Anxiety** is a worry about future events. **Fear** is a reaction to current events.
:::

These disorders can present as the physical symptoms of the fight-or-flight response. They are among the most prevalent psychiatric conditions in the USA; the lecture ranks them second, after substance use disorder.

The slide shows Edvard Munch’s painting *The Scream*.

## The HPA axis

:::definition HPA axis
The **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis** (hypothalamus → pituitary → adrenal gland) is the body’s primary stress-hormone axis. The hypothalamus releases CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone), which acts on the pituitary gland; the pituitary releases ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone), which acts on the adrenal glands; and the adrenal glands release cortisol into the body.
:::

![The HPA axis: the hypothalamus releases CRH, the pituitary gland releases ACTH, the adrenal gland releases cortisol, and cortisol feeds back negatively on the hypothalamus and pituitary](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g9-hpa-axis.svg)

The HPA axis releases stress hormones in the body. Its main one is cortisol; the lecture also lists adrenaline, which the adrenal glands release as well, although adrenaline release is driven mainly by the sympathetic nervous system rather than by ACTH.

The HPA axis works very much like the HPG axis that regulates testosterone and estrogen. Like the HPG axis, it regulates itself by **negative feedback**: cortisol acts back on the hypothalamus and pituitary to reduce its own release.

Some dysregulation of the HPA axis is found across a wide range of mental health disorders.

## Neural correlates of anxiety disorders

### Endocrine mechanisms

Anxiety disorders involve dysregulated HPA-axis function. The majority of anxiety disorders have an overactive HPA axis:

- increased cortisol and other stress hormones;
- generally thought to come from impaired negative feedback on CRH release.

HPA-axis overactivation also **inhibits the HPG axis**, so high stress hormones decrease testosterone and estrogen. Bearing in mind the roles of those hormones in cognition, behavior, function and development, you can see where some symptoms of anxiety disorders start to appear.

### HPA-axis overactivation and the brain

Overactivation of the HPA axis causes neurotoxicity (damage to neurons) in the:

- prefrontal cortex (PFC);
- hippocampus;
- amygdala.

It also impairs *how* these brain regions communicate. The slide’s diagram of a head in side view labels the three regions: the prefrontal cortex at the front of the brain, and the amygdala and hippocampus deep inside it.

### Major neuroanatomy of anxiety

Anxiety involves dysregulation in two pathways: **PFC → amygdala** and **hippocampus → amygdala**.

Typically, the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus send inhibitory GABA signals to the amygdala. In anxiety, the GABA that reaches the amygdala from the PFC and hippocampus is impaired.

![Two panels: typically the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus send inhibitory GABA signals that keep the amygdala in check; in anxiety these signals are impaired and the amygdala fires inappropriately](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g9-anxiety-circuit.svg)

GABA is the “off switch” neurotransmitter: it is inhibitory. Low GABA in the amygdala amounts to a missing off switch, so the amygdala keeps firing inappropriately, which can cause a strong fear or anxiety response. These particular circuits seem to be the ones dysregulated in anxiety disorders, and the same thing appears again in depression. Think about what the circuits may be doing, given the functions of the PFC and the hippocampus, and how that gives rise to anxiety symptoms.

## Pharmaceuticals

On drugs for mental health disorders, the lecturer’s verdict: “There’s the ones that work badly, and the ones that work worse.”

:::definition Agonist and antagonist
An **agonist** increases the function of its target system. An **antagonist** decreases the function of its target system.
:::

### Pharmaceutical treatments for anxiety disorders

- **Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)** and **serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs)** act on serotonin and/or norepinephrine. They are the front-line treatment.
- **Benzodiazepines** (GABA agonists) are second-line treatments. They are not preferred because of their risk of abuse.
- There are no medications that can treat CRH dysregulation.

### How SSRIs and SNRIs work

1. Serotonin or norepinephrine is released into the synapse.
2. Normally, leftover neurotransmitter is re-absorbed into the sending neuron during **reuptake** (through the transporters in its membrane, the last step of synaptic transmission).
3. These drugs act as reuptake antagonists: they block reuptake.
4. So the neurotransmitter remains in the synapse.

![A serotonin synapse: vesicles in the pre-synaptic nerve ending release serotonin (5-HT) into the synapse toward receptor sites on the post-synaptic nerve ending, while an SSRI blocks the reuptake transporter](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g9-ssri-synapse.svg)

The slide’s diagram shows a pre-synaptic nerve ending releasing serotonin (5-HT) from its vesicles into the synapse, where it reaches the receptor sites on the post-synaptic nerve ending; an SSRI blocks the reabsorption of serotonin into the pre-synaptic ending.

### Benzodiazepines

Common benzodiazepines include Xanax and Valium.

- They increase the effectiveness of GABA, decreasing neuron excitability: they are GABA agonists.
- They have promising short-term benefits but are not particularly effective in the long term.
- They carry a high risk of abuse, dependence and overdose.

![Action potential: resting state at -70 mV, failed initiations below the -55 mV threshold, depolarization with Na+ in up to about +40 mV, repolarization with K+ out, hyperpolarization, and return to rest](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g9-action-potential.svg)

The slide recalls the action potential to show what “decreasing excitability” means. At rest (5) the membrane sits at −70 mV. A stimulus (1) that doesn’t bring the membrane to the −55 mV threshold gives only failed initiations; one that does triggers depolarization (2), as Na$^+$ ions flow in and the voltage peaks near +40 mV. Repolarization (3) follows as K$^+$ ions flow out, overshooting into hyperpolarization (4) before the membrane returns to its resting state (5). GABA moves a neuron away from threshold (it lets Cl$^-$ into the cell, making the inside more negative), so by boosting GABA, benzodiazepines make neurons less likely to fire.

## Check yourself

:::question Memory check
What does the HPA axis regulate?

1. Sex steroid hormones
2. Stress hormones
3. A & B (both of the above)
4. None of the above

:::solution
**2. Stress hormones.** The HPA axis releases cortisol, the main stress hormone; the sex steroids are regulated by the HPG axis (which an overactive HPA axis does inhibit).
:::
:::

:::question Memory check
What best describes the neurochemical state of an individual with anxiety?

1. High cortisol
2. Low GABA
3. High norepinephrine
4. Low GABA, high cortisol

:::solution
**4. Low GABA, high cortisol.** An overactive HPA axis raises cortisol, and the amygdala receives too little inhibitory GABA from the PFC and hippocampus; norepinephrine is only somewhat correlated with anxiety.
:::
:::

:::question Memory check
How is the PFC important for decreasing anxiety?

1. The PFC excites the amygdala
2. The PFC inhibits the amygdala
3. The amygdala excites the PFC
4. The amygdala inhibits the PFC

:::solution
**2. The PFC inhibits the amygdala.** The PFC sends inhibitory GABA signals to the amygdala, acting as its off switch; when that GABA is impaired, the amygdala fires inappropriately.
:::
:::

:::question Discussion
Anxiety and stress disorders have been somewhat correlated with high levels of norepinephrine. Why is increasing norepinephrine using SNRIs a front-line treatment?

:::solution
The slides leave this open. Two points from the lecture bear on it: the link is only a correlation (“somewhat correlated”), so high norepinephrine need not be what drives the anxiety, and SNRIs raise serotonin as well as norepinephrine.

:::
:::

:::question Memory check
What is the role of the hippocampus → amygdala pathway in stress disorders?

1. Non-emotional memories
2. Emotional memories
3. Non-emotional thoughts & cognition
4. Emotional thoughts & cognition

:::solution
**2. Emotional memories.** The hippocampus handles memory and the amygdala handles emotion, fear and stress, so the pathway between them carries emotional memories (thoughts and cognition belong to the PFC → amygdala pathway).
:::
:::

:::question Memory check
How do SSRIs and SNRIs increase neurotransmitter levels?

1. They act as serotonin/norepinephrine release agonists
2. They act as serotonin/norepinephrine release antagonists
3. They act as reuptake channel agonists
4. They act as reuptake channel antagonists

:::solution
**4. They act as reuptake channel antagonists.** They block reuptake, the re-absorption of leftover neurotransmitter into the sending neuron (through transporters, which the question calls channels), so serotonin or norepinephrine stays in the synapse longer.
:::
:::

:::question Exit discussion
Anxiety disorders are generally associated with low GABA and high cortisol. What other effects and symptoms would you expect to see in patients with anxiety disorders as a result? Is there a way of counteracting these effects?

:::solution
High cortisol inhibits the HPG axis, lowering testosterone and estrogen and so affecting cognition, behavior and development (both hormones support memory in the hippocampus); it is also neurotoxic to the PFC, hippocampus and amygdala and impairs their communication, while low GABA leaves the amygdala firing inappropriately, giving strong fear, anxiety and the physical symptoms of fight or flight. SSRIs and SNRIs are the front-line treatment and benzodiazepines can boost GABA in the short term (at a high risk of abuse), but no medication yet treats CRH dysregulation itself; newer therapies such as cognitive-behavioral therapy are another route.

:::
:::
