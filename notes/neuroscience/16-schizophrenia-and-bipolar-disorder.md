This lecture has three learning objectives:

1. Describe the neurobiological underpinnings of schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.
2. Explain how different drugs can treat these disorders.
3. Understand how compensatory responses can lead to a “dynamic,” ever changing neural landscape.

A compensatory response is the brain’s counter-adjustment to a lasting change in its own signaling, which pushes activity back the other way; it is taken up in the section on bipolar disorder.

## Schizophrenia: a history

- Medical descriptions of schizophrenia are rare before the 1800s.
- The late 1800s focused on distinguishing the early psychiatric disorders from one another, among them schizophrenia and bipolar disorder (then called “manic depression”).
- These disorders were largely treated with frontal lobotomies and shock therapy, two of the flawed 20th-century therapies from lecture 13: surgically cutting the connections of the prefrontal cortex, and setting off seizures or comas with an electric current or large doses of insulin.
- Diagnostic criteria were refined in the 1970s to represent the disorder more accurately.

The slide’s image is *My Eyes at the Moment of the Apparitions* by the German artist August Natterer, who had schizophrenia: two large, wide-open eyes with blue irises and gold-ringed pupils, drawn with fine lashes, float on a blank, yellowed page.

## Schizophrenia: an overview

:::definition Schizophrenia and psychosis
**Schizophrenia** is a psychiatric disorder characterized by episodes of **psychosis**, a loss of contact with reality, including:

- **hallucinations**: perceiving things that are not there, such as hearing voices;
- **delusions**: firmly held false beliefs;
- **disorganized thinking**: jumbled thought, which shows in speech that is hard to follow.
:::

Schizophrenia is often accompanied by social withdrawal, decreased emotional expression and apathy (a lack of interest or motivation).

- Onset typically begins in young adulthood, and most cases never resolve.
- Its prevalence, the share of people who have the disorder, is 0.3–0.7%.
- The suicide rate is 5%, and life expectancy is shortened by 20 years on average.
- The risk factors are both genetic and environmental. The environmental ones the lecture lists are being raised in a city, marijuana use in adolescence, infection, and prenatal malnourishment.

The slide’s photo, by its caption, is of John Nash, winner of the 1994 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, who had schizophrenia; his life was the subject of Sylvia Nasar’s 1998 book *A Beautiful Mind*. The black-and-white photo shows an older man in a tweed jacket with a striped scarf around his neck, holding a book and a pair of gloves.

## Schizophrenia: genetics and environment

:::definition Heritability
**Heritability** is the share of the differences in a trait across a population that is explained by genetic differences between people.
:::

Schizophrenia is **70–80% heritable**, but the exact gene mechanisms have not yet been identified. The slide adds that most genes identified are associated with suicidal behavior and with HPA axis function (the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal stress-hormone axis from lecture 13). In standard sources, many genes each have a small effect, and the link to the HPA axis and suicidal behavior rests on two of them, genes for stress-response proteins that help control the axis.

The developmental effects come in two groups.

**Prenatal** (before birth):

- prenatal stress, infection, maternal stress and malnutrition; maternal stress *triggers serotonin and dopamine dysregulation in the offspring*;
- schizophrenia is more likely in people born in the winter and spring.

**Postnatal** (after birth):

- childhood trauma;
- social isolation and discrimination.

:::definition Neurodevelopmental disorder
A **neurodevelopmental disorder** is one that arises from the way the brain develops, before and after birth.
:::

In the lecturer’s notes, schizophrenia is “largely considered a neurodevelopmental disorder because of the genetic and environmental factors that seem to be inducing it.”

## Schizophrenia: neural correlates

The neural basis of schizophrenia is largely still unknown, but it is considered a neurodevelopmental disorder based on interactions between genes and environment. Two neurotransmitters are implicated.

- **Dopamine:** psychosis is the misfiring of dopamine neurons. Overaction of dopamine causes paranoia (unfounded suspicion that others mean harm), delusions and hallucinations.
- **Glutamate:** there is overactivity in firing in the **thalamus**, the relay station that passes sensory information on to the cortex.

There are also morphological changes in the brain over time, that is, changes in its structure: a loss of cells and synapses.

The lecturer’s notes split the work between the two transmitters differently, and flag the question as open: “Dopamine seems to give rise to the cognitive effects, glutamate may be the origin of hallucinations??? It’s a little unclear.”

## Schizophrenia: treatments

:::definition Antipsychotic
An **antipsychotic** is a drug that reduces the symptoms of psychosis.
:::

- The first-line treatment, the one tried first, is antipsychotic medication that blocks dopamine receptors.
- 30–50% of people with schizophrenia don’t recognize that they have an illness, and don’t comply with treatment (they don’t take it as prescribed).
- 1 in 3 are treatment resistant: their symptoms do not improve enough on standard medication.

The slide shows a box of Risperdal (risperidone) 4 mg tablets, an antipsychotic, with two blister packs of green tablets.

## Bipolar disorder: an overview

:::definition Bipolar disorder
**Bipolar disorder** is characterized by periods of depression and periods of abnormally elevated mood.

- **Mania** is an elevated mood associated with psychosis.
- **Hypomania** is a less severe form of mania.
:::

Psychosis can come with mania, but standard definitions of mania do not require it.

- The prevalence of bipolar disorder is 1–3%.
- The age of onset is commonly young adulthood.
- It is commonly associated with anxiety and substance use disorders.
- 6% of people with bipolar disorder die by suicide, and 30–40% engage in self-harm. The lecture gives these as the highest known rates of all mental disorders.

The slide shows an old engraving of a woman with short dark hair in a long, loose dress, seated and gripping the cloth in her lap as she looks to one side, printed over a page of old text.

## Bipolar disorder: a history

- Bipolar disorder was first described in the 1850s as “circular insanity”: oscillations between mania and melancholia, an old name for deep depression.
- The slide says it was distinguished from “unipolar depression” (depression without manic episodes) in 1952, and only first considered distinct from depression in the DSM-III, the third edition of the *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*. Standard histories date the split between unipolar and bipolar depression to 1957; in 1952 the first DSM listed a single “manic-depressive reaction.”
- Subtypes were added in the DSM-IV: bipolar I, bipolar II and cyclothymia. (Strictly, bipolar II was the new subtype in the DSM-IV; cyclothymia was already in the DSM-III.)

:::definition The bipolar subtypes
- **Bipolar I**: at least one full manic episode.
- **Bipolar II**: episodes of hypomania and of depression, with no full manic episode.
- **Cyclothymia**: a milder, long-lasting pattern of mood swings, with hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never reach full episodes.
:::

The slide’s image is the pair of theater masks of comedy and tragedy, one smiling and one frowning.

## Bipolar disorder: neural correlates

- The **amygdala and prefrontal cortex** are overactivated.
- Dopamine circuits alternate between **over- and under-activation** between mania and depression. Early life stress causes HPA axis dysfunction, which triggers this dysregulation.
- Dopamine **increases** during mania. A compensatory response then decreases dopamine receptor sensitivity (how strongly the receptors respond to dopamine), and this leads to depression.
- Serotonin levels are decreased in both manic and depressive periods.

The slide’s image is a 3D model of the brain split down the middle, with one half set beside the other so the inner face of the second can be seen. Six regions are colored and labeled: the dorsolateral, ventrolateral and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (DLPFC, VLPFC and DMPFC), the dorsal and ventral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and the amygdala. In these names, *dorsal* means toward the top, *ventral* toward the bottom, *lateral* toward the outside and *medial* toward the midline. The **anterior cingulate cortex** is a strip of cortex that curves around the front of the corpus callosum, the thick band of fibers joining the two halves of the brain.

![Two schematic side views of the brain, front at the right. On the outer (lateral) surface, the DLPFC is marked high on the front of the brain and the VLPFC below it. On the inner (medial) surface, the DMPFC is marked at the top of the frontal lobe, the dorsal ACC as a band curving over the front of the corpus callosum, the ventral ACC below and in front of its bend, and the amygdala deep in the temporal lobe.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g12-bipolar-regions.svg)

The lecturer’s notes sum up the dopamine story: “It’s basically just alternating over- and under-activation of dopamine. Too much? Mania. Too little? Depression-like symptoms. The compensatory response to too much/too little dopamine causes the pendulum to swing in the opposite direction, and it more or less keeps swinging.”

:::definition Compensatory response
A **compensatory response** is the brain’s counter-adjustment to a lasting change in signaling. In the previous lecture, stimulants increased dopamine binding to receptors, and the compensatory response blocked the natural synthesis of dopamine, so that during withdrawal natural dopamine release stayed low for over a week. In bipolar disorder, the surge of dopamine in mania is met by a drop in dopamine receptor sensitivity, which tips the system into depression.
:::

This is the third learning objective at work: each correction overshoots and sets up the next swing.

![Cycle diagram: early life stress causes HPA axis dysfunction, which triggers dysregulation of dopamine circuits. Mania (too much dopamine) leads, through a compensatory response that lowers dopamine receptor sensitivity, to depression-like symptoms (too little dopamine), and a compensatory response swings activity back the other way to mania. Serotonin is decreased in both manic and depressive periods.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g12-dopamine-pendulum.svg)

## Bipolar disorder: treatments

In the lecturer’s words, “This is *difficult* to treat.” The lecture lists three classes of drug.

:::definition Mood stabilizer
A **mood stabilizer** is a drug that evens out mood swings in both directions, reducing manic and depressive episodes.
:::

**Mood stabilizers.** The lecture’s example is **lithium**.

- It decreases norepinephrine and increases serotonin.
- It *may* increase BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor, the protein from lecture 14 that regulates synaptic plasticity and the growth of new neurons).
- It was originally sold as an additive in 7 Up soda, which launched as “Bib-Label Lithiated Lemon-Lime Soda” (*lithiated* means containing lithium).

**Antipsychotics.**

- They decrease dopamine signaling.
- They help with manic episodes, and do not help with depressive ones. Several newer antipsychotics, such as quetiapine and lurasidone, are approved for bipolar depression as well.

**Antidepressants.**

- Most are ineffective, and there is debate over whether they actually help.

The slide shows four lithium capsules in two strengths: pale pink-and-white capsules printed “300” and orange-and-yellow capsules printed “150,” each also printed “LITH.”

## Check yourself

The first question reviews the previous lecture.

:::question Memory test
Why, from a neurochemical perspective, are dopamine-based drugs so addictive?

:::solution
Stimulants increase dopamine activity binding to receptors. This causes a compensatory response that blocks the natural synthesis of dopamine, so during the come-down (withdrawal) natural dopamine release is decreased for over a week, with a longer-term loss of motivation and attention. With extended use the “high” weakens and the “low” strengthens, so the user needs more of the drug for the same effect and has more reason to take it again to escape the low. And since dopamine signals motivational salience (lecture 3), a drug that boosts it makes taking the drug seem highly desirable.
:::
:::

:::question Memory check
How is the HPA axis associated with schizophrenia?

1. It’s underactive
2. It’s overactive
3. It’s unaffected

:::solution
**2. It’s overactive.** The slides never state this outright; everything they tie to the HPA axis points that way: genes linked to schizophrenia are associated with HPA axis function, and the risk factors are stressors (prenatal and maternal stress, childhood trauma, social isolation, discrimination), which drive the stress-hormone axis up, as in anxiety and depression.
:::
:::

:::question Memory check
Why is schizophrenia largely considered a neurodevelopmental disorder?

1. Genetic factors influence risk
2. Environmental factors influence risk
3. Both genetic & environmental factors influence risk
4. Symptoms typically arise early in development

:::solution
**3. Both genetic and environmental factors influence risk.** The lecture calls it neurodevelopmental “based on interactions between genes & environment”: it is 70–80% heritable, and prenatal and postnatal factors such as maternal stress and childhood trauma also raise the risk. Option 4 is also wrong on the facts, since onset typically comes in young adulthood.
:::
:::

:::question Memory check
What chemical imbalance is likely causing hallucinations in patients with schizophrenia?

1. Too much dopamine
2. Too much glutamate
3. Too much dopamine & glutamate
4. Too little dopamine, too much glutamate

:::solution
**3. Too much dopamine and glutamate.** The slide lists hallucinations among the effects of dopamine overaction, alongside paranoia and delusions, and the lecturer’s notes add that glutamate, overactive in the thalamus, “may be the origin of hallucinations,” while admitting that “it’s a little unclear.” Both are in excess, which rules out option 4.
:::
:::

:::question Discussion
Why may increases and decreases in dopamine between manic and melancholic episodes lead to these symptoms?

:::solution
Dopamine signals motivational salience and is involved in learning, memory and movement (lecture 3), and in lecture 14’s diagram of the monoamines it underlies alertness: working memory, motivation and clarity. Too much dopamine is what this lecture blames for psychosis in schizophrenia (paranoia, delusions, hallucinations), which matches the elevated mood and psychosis of mania. Too little resembles the stimulant come-down from the previous lecture, with its loss of motivation and attention, which matches the depressive phase. Each swing sets off a compensatory response that pushes dopamine signaling the other way, so the two kinds of symptoms alternate.
:::
:::

:::question Memory check
How does decreasing norepinephrine help alleviate symptoms in patients with bipolar disorder?

1. Decreases severity of depression symptoms
2. Decreases severity of mania symptoms
3. Decreases glutamate
4. Indirectly increases serotonin

:::solution
**2. It decreases the severity of mania symptoms.** Norepinephrine is an activating transmitter: in the previous lecture, elevated norepinephrine contributed to hyperactive ADHD symptoms, and in lecture 13 high norepinephrine was somewhat correlated with anxiety. Turning it down calms the elevated, energized manic phase. Lowering it would not relieve depression (SNRIs, serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, treat depression by raising it); lithium’s increase in serotonin is a separate effect; and the lecture tied glutamate to schizophrenia.
:::
:::

:::question Exit discussion
Most drugs treating both schizophrenia and bipolar disorder aim to decrease dopamine. What kinds of side effects are people likely to have from these drugs? Why?

:::solution
The drugs lower dopamine signaling throughout the brain, beyond the misfiring circuits, so the side effects follow from what dopamine normally does (lectures 3 and 14): less motivation and interest, foggy thinking with poorer working memory and attention, effects on learning and memory, and problems with movement, since dopamine is involved in motor function. In bipolar disorder the low-dopamine state is the depressive phase, and antipsychotics help only with mania, so pushing dopamine down can also bring on depression-like symptoms.
:::
:::

:::question Exit discussion
What does this suggest about the efficacy of these drugs at treating psychiatric disorders?

:::solution
That they are blunt instruments. They turn dopamine down everywhere to quiet psychosis and mania, while the underlying causes, which the lecture calls largely unknown and neurodevelopmental, go untreated. The lecture’s numbers bear this out: 1 in 3 people with schizophrenia are treatment resistant, antipsychotics help only with mania, antidepressants for bipolar disorder are mostly ineffective, and the lecturer calls bipolar disorder “difficult” to treat. Compensatory responses add a further limit, since the brain adjusts to any lasting push on its dopamine system. This fits the lecturer’s verdict from lecture 13 that there are “the ones that work badly, and the ones that work worse.”
:::
:::
