## ADD vs ADHD

- **ADD** (attention deficit disorder) is an outdated term that is no longer in use.
- **ADHD** (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) is the official term, even if the patient has no hyperactive symptoms.

There are two types of ADHD:

- hyperactive-impulsive ADHD;
- inattentive ADHD;

or a combination of the two.

ADHD was described in the medical literature as early as the 1700s.

The slide’s picture is a drawing of a brain seen from above: the left hemisphere is drawn whole, while the right hemisphere breaks up into horizontal streaks, as if it were scattering.

## ADHD: an overview

:::definition Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)
**ADHD** is a neurodevelopmental disorder (a disorder arising from the way the brain develops) characterized by inattention, or by excessive activity and impulsivity, at a level inappropriate for a person’s age.
:::

- People with ADHD have occasional difficulty with the regulation of emotion and of executive function (the mental skills of planning, organizing, holding things in mind and controlling impulses).
- Many can “hyperfixate” on a particular task or topic they find interesting or rewarding: they become intensely absorbed in it, often for long stretches.

The risk factors for ADHD are:

- genetics;
- smoking during pregnancy;
- low birth weight and premature birth;
- extreme neglect.

ADHD is commonly co-morbid with substance misuse: the two often occur together in the same person.

## Neural correlates

### Neurotransmitters

ADHD involves dysregulation of dopamine and norepinephrine:

- **Dopamine** is decreased in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), the midbrain region where most of the brain’s dopamine is made, and in the prefrontal cortex (PFC).
- **Norepinephrine** is increased in the PFC.

Most standard references instead describe ADHD as weaker dopamine *and* norepinephrine signaling in the PFC, and the stimulant medications below raise norepinephrine as well as dopamine.

### Anatomical changes

- The volume of the left PFC is reduced.
- Regions of the amygdala and hippocampus are smaller.
- There is asymmetry in axon routing and density between brain regions: the fibers connecting regions run and are packed unevenly.

## Treatments

Two drugs are the main treatments. (Recall from the lecture on anxiety disorders that an agonist increases the function of its target system and an antagonist decreases it.)

- **Ritalin** is a dopamine reuptake antagonist: it blocks reuptake (the sending neuron’s re-absorption of leftover neurotransmitter), so released dopamine stays in the synapse. It is the most common treatment, but it won’t work for every type of ADHD.
- **Adderall** is a dopamine release agonist: it increases the release of dopamine. It is prescribed if Ritalin is ineffective.

![A dopamine synapse: vesicles in the pre-synaptic dopamine neuron release dopamine into the synapse toward dopamine receptors on the post-synaptic neuron. Adderall, a dopamine release agonist, makes the neuron release more dopamine; Ritalin, a dopamine reuptake antagonist, blocks the reuptake transporter, so dopamine stays in the synapse.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g11-stimulant-synapse.svg)

## ADHD and diet

There have long been arguments that eating a lot of sugar leads to an ADHD diagnosis, and similar arguments about artificial sweeteners and dyes.

- The slide says these sweeteners and dyes are banned in the European Union. They are not: since 2010, EU foods containing Red 40 or five other synthetic dyes must carry a warning that they “may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children,” and artificial sweeteners remain approved.
- Fewer than a third of children see improvement with fatty acid supplements (such as omega-3 fish oil) combined with eating fewer dyes.
- That improvement is likely limited to children with food sensitivities who are also being treated for ADHD.

Overall:

- There is **no evidence** for a dietary cause, despite repeated research.
- Various elimination diets (cutting out suspected foods) have been tested, and none has been effective.
- **As of 2019, the American Academy of Pediatrics (the main professional body of US pediatricians) does not recommend overall dietary modifications.**

From the lecturer’s notes: “growing up in the 90s basically the whole culture thought ADHD came from Red 40 or just eating too much sugar. Goofballs.” Red 40 is a synthetic red dye used in foods, drinks and cosmetics, and the slide’s photo is a tub of bright red Red No. 40 pigment powder, labeled for use in lipstick, blush and makeup.

## Will taking stimulants help you study if you don’t have ADHD?

Stimulants, drugs that speed up activity in the nervous system, are the class of drugs Ritalin and Adderall belong to.

- While therapeutic for many, stimulants are widely misused as a “performance enhancer” in business and academic settings.
- The documented prevalence of misuse is 4–16%, depending on the university.
- While using “study drugs,” students report increased mood, increased motivation and increased interest.
- Many say it makes them not mind doing work, though it doesn’t make them smarter.

The lecturer’s verdict: “Basically, it works, but the quality of the work tends to not be stellar. It tends to be long, but not phenomenal. Think a ChatGPT output tbh.”

## Chronic effects

Chronic (long-term, regular) use of the medication is *necessary* for long-term improvement of outcomes for individuals with ADHD.

- It **decreases** the risk of substance abuse disorder in people with ADHD.
- It is associated with an **increased** risk of substance abuse disorder in people without ADHD.

The lecturer’s notes put it this way: “Long story short, if you’ve been prescribed a med you should probably take it, and if you haven’t been prescribed it then I’m gonna publicly shame you for contributing to our current national ADHD medication shortage.” The shortage meant here is the US shortage of stimulant ADHD medications that began in 2022. The slide’s photo shows white tablets spilling from a tipped-over orange prescription bottle.

## What happens when you come down?

:::definition Compensatory response
A **compensatory response** is the brain’s adjustment against a drug’s effect. With stimulants, the extra dopamine activity leads the brain to block its own natural synthesis of dopamine.
:::

The sequence runs as follows.

1. Consumption of stimulants leads to increased dopamine activity, with more dopamine binding to receptors.
2. This causes a compensatory response that blocks natural synthesis of dopamine.
3. The compensatory response (withdrawal) decreases natural dopamine release for **over a week**.
4. The result is a longer-term loss of motivation and attention.

Extended use weakens the “high” but strengthens the “low.”

![Flow chart of coming down from a stimulant: the stimulant increases dopamine binding to receptors (the “high”); a compensatory response blocks natural synthesis of dopamine; on coming down (withdrawal, the “low”), natural dopamine release is decreased for over a week, giving a longer-term loss of motivation and attention. With extended use, the “high” weakens but the “low” strengthens.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g11-comedown.svg)

The lecturer calls this “a little teaser for the addiction section,” adding, “This applies to an astounding amount of drugs.” The slide also carries a portrait photo captioned “Dr. Edward Castañeda” (a neuroscientist at the University of Texas at El Paso who studies how stimulants such as amphetamine change dopamine release) and a photo of tablets spilling from a pill bottle.

## Performance enhancer vs treatment

- There is a key difference between modulating a system with dysfunction and modulating a system working “typically.”
- In people with ADHD, medication restores the system to “normal,” and this **decreases** the likelihood of adult substance abuse.
- Research and refinement of pharmaceuticals is ongoing, to decrease negative outcomes and dependence.

## Check yourself

The first two questions review the previous lecture.

:::question Memory test
Describe how BDNF and Reelin are implicated in anxiety and depression.

:::solution
BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) regulates synaptic plasticity (the strengthening and weakening of synapses) and neurogenesis (the growth of new neurons), especially in the hippocampus, amygdala and PFC; Reelin regulates brain development and synaptic plasticity, and in adults it mainly strengthens GABA synapses. Both are inhibited by high stress hormones, and patients with depression have low levels of both. The chain runs: stress overactivates the HPA axis (the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal stress-hormone axis); desensitized glucocorticoid receptors (the receptors that detect cortisol) block its negative feedback; the high stress hormones are toxic to neurons and inhibit BDNF and Reelin; neurons and synapses break down, especially the GABA projections from the PFC and hippocampus to the amygdala; and that breakdown causes and perpetuates depression symptoms. The previous lecture developed this for depression, and anxiety disorders share both the overactive HPA axis and the impaired GABA from the PFC and hippocampus to the amygdala, so the same chain bears on them.
:::
:::

:::question Memory test
Why is it more likely that BDNF and Reelin are directly involved in depression than serotonin?

:::solution
The evidence lines up for BDNF and Reelin: they are repeatedly found diminished in patients with depression, effective treatments increase them (repairing neurons and synapses), and treatments that increase them work rapidly with long-term effects (ketamine acts within 2 hours). For serotonin it does not: drugs change monoamine levels quickly while the therapeutic effect takes weeks or months, drug dose is unrelated to therapeutic effect, chronic SSRI use *decreases* serotonin, monoamine levels in the brain are unrelated to having depression, and blocking tryptophan, the chemical precursor to serotonin, does not induce depression.
:::
:::

:::question Discussion
ADHD involves decreased dopamine in the VTA and PFC and increased norepinephrine in the PFC. How do these neurotransmitter dysregulations contribute to symptoms?

:::solution
Dopamine signals motivational salience, how worth pursuing something seems (lecture on hormones and neurotransmitters), so too little of it in the PFC makes ordinary tasks hard to engage with: inattention and low motivation. Norepinephrine drives physiological arousal, so too much of it fits restlessness, hyperactivity and impulsivity. In the previous lecture’s monoamine diagram, attention sits in the overlap of dopamine and norepinephrine, so dysregulating both strikes at attention. In the lecturer’s account, the balance between the two decides where a person falls on the spectrum from inattentive to hyperactive.
:::
:::

:::question Discussion
ADHD brings a smaller left PFC, smaller regions of the amygdala and hippocampus, and asymmetric axon routing and density between regions. How may these physical differences contribute to ADHD symptoms?

:::solution
Cross-check the regions’ functions with the symptoms. The PFC handles decision-making, working memory, planning, emotion regulation and self-control, so a smaller left PFC fits inattention, weak executive function and impulsivity; the chart links a smaller left anterior PFC to more emotion dysregulation. The amygdala handles emotional regulation, fear and stress, which fits the difficulty regulating emotion. The hippocampus handles memory, and its pathway to the amygdala carries emotional memories. Asymmetric axon routing and density means these regions communicate less well, including the PFC → amygdala pathway that keeps the amygdala in check.
:::
:::

:::question Discussion
Why would Adderall be effective for patients who don’t benefit from Ritalin?

:::solution
Ritalin blocks reuptake, so it can only keep in the synapse the dopamine that has already been released; it helps when the problem lies in reuptake. If a patient’s neurons release too little dopamine in the first place, blocking reuptake has little to work with. Adderall increases dopamine release itself, so it helps those patients. Which drug works shows which part of the dopamine system is not working properly: reuptake (Ritalin responders) or release (patients who need Adderall).
:::
:::

:::question Exit discussion
There is a strong association between ADHD and decreased neuronal activation in the PFC. What are the implications of this lack of activation?

:::solution
The PFC handles decision-making, working memory, planning, emotion regulation and self-control, and it sends inhibitory GABA signals that keep the amygdala in check. Too little activation there fits ADHD’s symptoms: trouble with attention and executive function, impulsivity, and difficulty regulating emotion, since the amygdala is held back less. It agrees with the lecture’s other findings, the low dopamine in the PFC and the reduced left PFC volume. By the “use it or lose it” principle from the lecture on learning and memory, circuits that are under-used also tend to weaken over time. It also explains why medication counts as treatment in ADHD: raising dopamine restores an under-active system to “normal,” where in people without ADHD the same drugs modulate a system already working typically.
:::
:::
