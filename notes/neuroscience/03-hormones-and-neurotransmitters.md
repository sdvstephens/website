This lecture has three goals: to understand the steps of synaptic transmission, to describe the main functions of key neurotransmitters, and to critically evaluate outcomes associated with neurotransmitter dysregulation. It opened with a discussion question about myelin, which is collected with the other questions at the end.

## Synaptic transmission

The action potential from the last lecture travels down the axon of one neuron, the **pre-synaptic** (“sending”) cell, to its axon terminals. There the axon terminals meet the dendrites of the next neuron, the **post-synaptic** (“receiving”) cell.

:::definition Synapse
The **synapse** is the point where an axon terminal of the sending (pre-synaptic) cell meets a dendrite of the receiving (post-synaptic) cell.
:::

![Two neurons: an action potential travels along the axon of the pre-synaptic (sending) cell to its axon terminals, which meet the dendrites of the post-synaptic (receiving) cell at a boxed point labeled synapse.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g2-synaptic-transmission.svg)

### Steps of synaptic transmission

1. The neurotransmitter, or the enzymes needed to make it, is made in the cell body.
2. It is transported down the axon to the axon terminals and stored there in small sacs called **vesicles**.
3. The vesicles release the neurotransmitter into the synapse.
4. The neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the post-synaptic cell, which triggers a response in that cell.
5. The excess is taken back into the pre-synaptic neuron through transporters in its membrane.

The lecture’s diagram also shows two enzymes that break neurotransmitter down: a degradation enzyme in the synapse itself, and a metabolizing enzyme inside the sending neuron’s terminal.

![Close-up of a synapse. Neurotransmitter made in the cell body (1) is stored in vesicles in the axon terminal (2), released into the synapse (3), binds to receptors on the receiving neuron to produce a cellular response (4), and the excess is taken back into the sending neuron through a transporter (5); a metabolizing enzyme inside the terminal and a degradation enzyme in the synapse are also shown.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g2-synapse-steps.svg)

The lecture also shows a photo of an actual synapse, taken with an electron microscope (the scale bar is 0.5 μm). The axon terminal at the top is packed with small round synaptic vesicles. Below it, separated by a thin dark line where the two membranes meet (the synapse), is a dendrite.

## What is a neurotransmitter?

:::definition Neurotransmitter
A **neurotransmitter** is a chemical messenger that a neuron stores in vesicles in its axon terminals and releases into the synapse, where it binds to receptors on the receiving cell.
:::

## Neurotransmitters and hormones

### GABA and glutamate

**GABA** is **inhibitory**: it decreases action potentials. When GABA binds to GABA receptors, Cl$^-$ (chloride) flows into the receiving cell more readily.

**Glutamate** is **excitatory**: it increases action potentials. When glutamate binds to glutamate receptors, Na$^+$ flows into the receiving cell more readily.

![Two spike trains: with GABA the neuron fires few action potentials, and with glutamate it fires many, closely spaced.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g2-gaba-glutamate.svg)

:::remark
This ties back to the action potential. Na$^+$ entering the cell is depolarization, which pushes the neuron toward threshold, so glutamate makes the receiving cell fire more. Cl$^-$ carries a negative charge, so letting it in makes the inside of the cell more negative and moves it away from threshold, so GABA makes the receiving cell fire less.
:::

The lecture shows the chemical structure of each of the next three neurotransmitters. Notice how much dopamine and norepinephrine resemble each other.

![Skeletal chemical structures of dopamine (a benzene ring with two HO groups and a two-carbon chain ending in NH2), norepinephrine (the same, with an extra OH on the chain), and serotonin, also called 5-HT or 5-hydroxytryptamine (an indole ring with an HO group and a two-carbon chain ending in NH2).](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g2-monoamine-structures.svg)

### Dopamine

Dopamine is mainly produced in a region called the **ventral tegmental area**, and it is processed throughout the limbic system. Dopamine is often described as the brain’s “reward” chemical. The lecture’s more careful description is that dopamine signals **motivational salience**.

:::definition Motivational salience
**Motivational salience** is the perceived desirability or aversiveness of an outcome: how much something seems worth approaching or avoiding.
:::

Dopamine is also involved in learning and memory, and in motor function.

### Serotonin

Serotonin is also known as **5-HT** (5-hydroxytryptamine). About 90% of the body’s serotonin is produced in the gastrointestinal tract; the remaining 10% is produced in the brainstem’s **raphe nuclei**.

Serotonin is involved in:
- mood
- anxiety
- memory formation
- digestion
- sleep
- sexual inhibition

### Norepinephrine

Norepinephrine is produced by very few cells, primarily in a small cluster of neurons called the **locus coeruleus**. Only about one millionth of the neurons in the brain produce it, but those neurons send projections everywhere.

Norepinephrine is involved in physiological arousal and wakefulness, including:
- the fight-or-flight response
- stress and anxiety
- attention

### Acetylcholine

The lecturer introduced this one as “a neurotransmitter we’ll probably never talk about again. But it does EVERYTHING”: nearly every function you can think of has acetylcholine involved.

Acetylcholine is highly prevalent in the peripheral nervous system (the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord). It is released at **neuromuscular junctions**, where nerves meet muscles, to control movement.

It is also produced in several brain regions involved in:
- learning, memory and attention
- arousal
- muscle movement
- cardiac function and blood pressure

The lecture’s brain diagram shows acetylcholine pathways fanning out from the septal nuclei and the substantia innominata, near the front of the brain, to the cortex, cingulate gyrus, hippocampus and amygdala, and from a cluster in the brainstem up to the thalamus and down the brainstem.

Alzheimer’s disease and dementia have been associated with low acetylcholine levels. Acetylcholine signaling is also the target of many poisons: some natural toxins work by blocking it, while nerve agents work by stopping acetylcholine from being broken down, so that it builds up at the synapse.

### Oxytocin

Oxytocin is involved in:
- pair-bonding
- affiliation
- reproduction
- smooth muscle contraction

Oxytocin is a small peptide: a chain of nine amino acids (the building blocks of proteins), Cys-Tyr-Ile-Gln-Asn-Cys-Pro-Leu-Gly-NH$_2$, closed into a ring by a sulfur–sulfur (S–S) bond between its two cysteines (Cys).

![Oxytocin drawn as a chain of nine amino acids, Cys, Tyr, Ile, Gln, Asn, Cys, Pro, Leu, Gly, ending in NH2, with an S–S bridge joining the first and sixth amino acids (the two cysteines).](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g2-oxytocin-sequence.svg)

### Vasopressin

Vasopressin is involved in:
- water retention
- vasoconstriction (narrowing of blood vessels)
- aggression and dominance
- pair-bonding and monogamy

The lecture also shows vasopressin’s chemical structure: like oxytocin, it is a short chain of amino acids closed into a ring by a sulfur–sulfur bond.

## Testosterone and estrogen

Testosterone and estrogen are *not* just “male” and “female” hormones! Estrogen is “processed” testosterone: the body makes estrogen from testosterone. Both hormones are involved in the development of the reproductive organs and in reproductive endocrine (hormonal) processes: puberty, spermiogenesis (the final stage of sperm production) and ovulation.

The two hormones share most of their functions:

| Testosterone | Estrogen |
|---|---|
| Sexual development | Sexual development |
| Stereotyped male sexual behavior | Stereotyped female sexual behavior |
| Dominance & aggression | Dominance & aggression |
| Stress regulation | Stress regulation |
| Memory | Memory |
| Puberty (voice deepening, male genital development) | Puberty (physical growth, muscle development, ova & sperm maturation) |

### Estrogen is essential for memory

:::example Estrogen and memory
Rats with their ovaries removed have impaired memory ability. Supplementing them with synthetic estrogen restores memory and decreases anxiety behavior. (Dr. Emese Renczés is pictured with this work.)

Estrogen deficiencies cause cell death in the hippocampus: when the hippocampus’s estrogen receptors are not activated, its mitochondria (the cell’s energy-producing structures) degenerate. (Dr. Rukmani Pandey is pictured with this work.)
:::

### Testosterone is also essential

:::example Testosterone and memory
Decreased testosterone impairs memory, while supplementing testosterone increases memory ability. There’s a limit: too much testosterone starts to decrease memory performance again. (Dr. Karlye Rodriguez-Wisdom is pictured with this work.)

This effect is mediated by testosterone maintaining synaptic function in the hippocampus. (Dr. Seppan Prakash is pictured with this work.)
:::

## Check yourself

:::question
Describe three symptoms that someone will likely have if their brain is not producing enough myelin, or if the myelin sheath is damaged. Why is myelin important to these functions?

:::solution
From multiple sclerosis (previous lecture): impaired sensation, impaired vision, muscular weakness and impaired coordination. Myelin insulates the axon and speeds up transmission, so without it the signals behind sensing, seeing and moving slow down or fail.
:::
:::

:::question
Why are dopamine-based drugs addictive? What happens if you have a deficiency in dopamine?

:::solution
Dopamine signals motivational salience and supports learning, so a drug that boosts dopamine makes taking it seem highly desirable and teaches the brain to seek it again. A deficiency would show up in what dopamine regulates: motivation, learning and memory, and motor function.
:::
:::

:::question
What happens if you have too little serotonin? Too much?

:::solution
Either one disturbs what serotonin regulates: mood, anxiety, memory formation, digestion, sleep and sexual inhibition. For example, since serotonin promotes sexual inhibition, too much would be expected to lower sex drive.
:::
:::

:::question
How might oxytocin be associated with giving birth?

:::solution
Oxytocin drives smooth muscle contraction and is involved in reproduction, so it can drive the contractions of labor. Its role in pair-bonding and affiliation also fits the bond between parent and newborn.
:::
:::

:::question
What happens if you take a drug that shuts down vasopressin?

:::solution
Vasopressin makes the body retain water, so blocking it means the body holds on to less water and loses more as urine, risking dehydration. It would also be expected to affect behaviors vasopressin supports, such as aggression and pair-bonding.
:::
:::

:::question
Where are neurotransmitters released from, and where do they bind to?

- a. Soma, axon
- b. Axon, soma
- c. Dendrites, axon terminals
- d. Axon terminals, dendrites

:::solution
**d. Axon terminals, dendrites.** Neurotransmitter is released from the axon terminals of the sending cell into the synapse and binds to receptors on the dendrites of the receiving cell.
:::
:::

:::question
Which of the following does **dopamine** regulate?

- a. Happiness
- b. Reward
- c. Pair bonding
- d. Stress

:::solution
**b. Reward**, or more precisely motivational salience: how desirable or aversive an outcome seems.
:::
:::

:::question
Which of the following does **oxytocin** regulate?

- a. Happiness
- b. Reward
- c. Pair bonding
- d. Stress

:::solution
**c. Pair bonding.**
:::
:::

:::question
Which of the following does **norepinephrine** regulate?

- a. Happiness
- b. Reward
- c. Pair bonding
- d. Stress

:::solution
**d. Stress.** Norepinephrine drives physiological arousal, including the fight-or-flight response and stress and anxiety.
:::
:::

:::question
You’re an endocrinologist who treats patients with low levels of estrogen. What symptoms would you likely see in your patients?

- a. Decreased sex drive
- b. Memory impairments
- c. Shorter height
- d. All of the above
- e. A & B only

:::solution
**d. All of the above**, going by this lecture: estrogen supports sexual behavior and memory, and drives physical growth at puberty. So low estrogen can show up as decreased sex drive, memory impairments and, when it happens during puberty, reduced growth.
:::
:::

:::question
Many people will have the misfortune of getting hangovers at some point in their life, and vasopressin is a big part of why. Based on what you’ve learned, how is vasopressin implicated in how we get hangovers? (Hint: alcohol inhibits vasopressin.)

:::solution
Vasopressin makes the body retain water. Alcohol inhibits vasopressin, so after drinking the body loses more water than usual and becomes dehydrated, which is a big part of a hangover.
:::
:::
