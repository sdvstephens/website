This lecture has three learning objectives:

1. Understand the basic foundations of drug addiction.
2. Learn the mechanisms of action of common pharmaceuticals.
3. Discuss the benefits and harms of different medications.

It opens with a memory test on the previous lecture on schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, about the neural factors behind hallucinations and delusions. That question is collected with the others under “Check yourself” at the end.

## What makes a drug addictive?

The lecture poses the question and answers it on the next slide: **all addictive drugs trigger dopamine release.** As the lecture on hormones and neurotransmitters put it, dopamine signals motivational salience, that is, how desirable or aversive an outcome seems, and it is the transmitter of the mesolimbic reward system from the lecture on learning and memory. The theme comes back through the lecture: amphetamines release dopamine directly, and opioid use, alcohol and caffeine all increase its release.

## Drugs: a mantra to remember

> “There are the drugs that work badly, and the ones that work worse.”

This is the same verdict the lecturer gave on the medications for anxiety and depression, now promoted to a mantra.

## Drugs: key terms

:::definition Agonist and antagonist
- An **agonist** increases the function of a target. Amphetamine, for example, is a dopamine *release* agonist: it increases the release of dopamine.
- An **antagonist** decreases the function of a target. Fluoxetine (Prozac), for example, is a serotonin *reuptake* antagonist: it decreases reuptake, so more serotonin stays in the synapse.
:::

These are the definitions from the lecture on anxiety disorders. The target can be any step of synaptic transmission (release, reuptake, a receptor), so an antagonist of reuptake ends up raising the level of a neurotransmitter.

The slide’s diagram shows the same two terms at the level of a single receptor. **Agonists** are drugs that occupy receptors and activate them. **Antagonists** are drugs that occupy receptors but do not activate them, so they block receptor activation by agonists. In three panels, an agonist alone binds the receptor and gives full activation inside the cell; an agonist and an antagonist together compete for the receptor and give less activation; and an antagonist alone sits in the receptor and gives no activation.

![Three panels, each a cell with a receptor on its surface. Agonist alone: the agonist binds the receptor and the cell is fully activated. Agonist plus antagonist: the two compete for the receptor and the cell is less activated. Antagonist alone: the antagonist occupies the receptor without activating it, and there is no activation.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g13-agonist-antagonist.svg)

## SSRIs and SNRIs: depression and anxiety

- **SSRI** stands for selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor.
- **SNRI** is expanded on the slide as “selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor.” It stands for serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor: SNRIs block the reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine.

How they work was covered in the lectures on anxiety disorders and depression: they block reuptake, so serotonin or norepinephrine stays in the synapse.

On whether they work, the slide’s answer is “Kinda.” Both kinds of drug improve symptoms, but:

- they take several weeks to work;
- the improvement doesn’t scale with dosage.

These are two of the criticisms of the monoamine hypothesis from the lecture on depression, which also discussed how much of the effect may be an amplified placebo effect. The lecturer’s own note is blunter: “They’re pretty ineffective for depression/anxiety though.” The slide’s photo is a close-up of green-and-cream capsules printed “PROZAC 20 mg.”

The slide then asks what side effects you think are common (see Check yourself).

## SSRI and SNRI side effects

The side effects listed are:

- anxiety;
- lack of joy, emotional numbness;
- increased suicidal behavior (the lecture on depression added that the risk is highest in children, teenagers and young adults);
- shakiness;
- digestive issues;
- dry mouth;
- decreased sex drive and sexual function: **30–75%** of people taking these drugs have sexual dysfunction.

The slide’s infographic, “Reported Side Effects of Long-Term Antidepressant Use,” pictures five of them as cartoons: sexual problems (a couple in bed), weight gain (a person holding their belly), reduced positive feelings (a person under a rain cloud), feeling dependent on the medication (pills spilling into a hand) and feeling emotionally numb (a heart frozen in a block of ice).

## SSRIs: a diversity issue

:::definition Serotonin syndrome
**Serotonin syndrome** is a dangerous excess of serotonin activity, usually from an overdose or from combining drugs that raise serotonin. Its symptoms are fever, tremors, seizures, GI (gastrointestinal, that is, digestive tract) failure and muscle breakdown.
:::

Serotonin syndrome is rare, but it can be fatal. The lecture gives 6 deaths from 1985 to 1995, and 234 overdoses between 1988 and 1992.

The effectiveness of SSRIs also varies by race. The lecture gives the share of patients for whom they work as:

- **84%** for white individuals;
- **67%** for Latinx individuals;
- **50%** for Black individuals;
- unclear data for other groups.

The reason given is genetic differences between populations in the serotonin transporter (the reuptake protein that SSRIs block), differences that weren’t tested in clinical trials. This decreases efficacy and increases risk in non-white patients. It is the same kind of problem as the MAO gene from the lecture on depression, where a person’s genes can make these drugs ineffective or even dangerous. The lecturer’s note: “Diversify your clinical trials, please!”

## Benzodiazepines: anxiety

Benzodiazepines, met in the lecture on anxiety disorders, are **GABA agonists**.

- Their binding increases GABA activity in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, which decreases anxiety. This is the inhibitory “off switch” that the anxiety circuit is missing.
- They cause toxic oxidative stress in the liver (damage to cells from reactive forms of oxygen).
- They carry a high risk for addiction.

In the lecturer’s words, they “work very well for depression/anxiety in the short-term, but are very, very addictive.” The slide’s photo shows two boxes of Xanax (alprazolam) 0.25 mg, one of them open with a blister pack of white tablets.

The slide asks what withdrawal effects you think are likely (see Check yourself).

## Benzodiazepine addiction

The withdrawal symptoms are:

- insomnia;
- anxiety;
- depression;
- depersonalization (feeling detached from yourself, as if watching yourself from outside);
- nausea;
- hallucinations;
- seizures;
- death.

The lecturer’s note calls this “one of the two drugs you can die from the overdose. This and alcohol.” Read with the list above, which ends in death, and with the alcohol slides later on, the point is about withdrawal: benzodiazepines and alcohol are the best-known drugs whose withdrawal alone can be fatal. (Overdose of other drugs can kill too, as the opioid and amphetamine slides show.)

Benzodiazepines also show high rates of abuse in pop culture. The lecture gives an **89%** increase in hospital visits (the slide does not give the period), and says **17%** of drug overdose deaths are from benzodiazepines. The slide’s photo shows a smiling young man with tattoos across his face and neck.

## Amphetamines: attention disorders

Amphetamines act as dopamine **release agonists**, or as dopamine **reuptake antagonists**.

- **Low** doses increase attention and wakefulness.
- **High** doses give a euphoric high, hyper-focus and psychosis (a loss of contact with reality, with hallucinations or delusions).

Their effect on addiction depends on who takes them:

- medicinal use in patients with ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) *decreases* the likelihood of substance abuse disorder;
- recreational use in people without ADHD is associated with a *greater* likelihood of substance abuse disorder.

The slide’s photo is a pharmacy bottle of Ritalin LA, 20 mg methylphenidate extended-release capsules. Methylphenidate, the drug in Ritalin, is a stimulant, though chemically it is not an amphetamine. It works by the second route, blocking dopamine reuptake, while amphetamines such as Adderall mainly increase dopamine release. The lecture on attentional disorders drew the same line, calling Ritalin a dopamine reuptake antagonist and Adderall a dopamine release agonist.

## Amphetamines: dependence, withdrawal and overdose

Amphetamines carry high rates of dependence and withdrawal.

- There is a **compensatory response**: the brain adjusts to counteract the drug, so greater dosages are needed to obtain the same effect.
- Withdrawal symptoms appear within 24 hours and last 3–4 weeks. They include fatigue, depression, increased appetite, decreased motivation and insomnia.
- Overdose leads to about **4,000** deaths a year in the USA, according to the slide. The CDC’s recent counts are much higher: about 35,000 overdose deaths in 2023 and 29,000 in 2024 involved stimulants of this group (methamphetamine, amphetamine and methylphenidate together).

The slide’s chart, from the New Mexico Department of Health, shows amphetamine-related emergency room visits in New Mexico per 100,000 people. They dip from 19.6 in 2010 to about 15 in 2011, then climb steadily to 56.1 in 2014, nearly three times the 2010 rate.

![Line chart of amphetamine-related emergency room visits in New Mexico per 100,000 people, 2010–2014: 19.6 in 2010, about 15 in 2011, about 26 in 2012, about 36 in 2013 and 56.1 in 2014.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g13-amphetamine-er-visits.svg)

## Opiates: pain

*Opiates* are drugs made from the opium poppy, such as morphine; *opioids* is the wider term, which also covers synthetic drugs such as oxycodone and fentanyl. The lecture uses both words.

Opioids treat pain in two ways:

1. They block pain signals from reaching the brain, via GABA in the spinal cord. This is the pain gate from the lecture on touch, taste and smell, where the body’s own opioids block pain signals in the spinal cord before they reach the brain.
2. They increase GABA in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, the same calming effect as the benzodiazepines.

On top of this, their **use triggers a large dopamine release**. The slide’s photo is a bottle of OxyContin (oxycodone hydrochloride, controlled-release) 10 mg tablets, with tablets spilled in front of it.

The slide asks why opiate-based drugs are so addictive (see Check yourself).

## Opiates: addiction and overdose

- **3.2%** of American adults abuse opiates.
- There are about **47,000** deaths a year from opioid overdose in the USA alone, **70%** of drug-related deaths. That is the 2017 count, the last bar in the slide’s chart; the CDC counted about 79,000 opioid overdose deaths in 2023 and 54,000 in 2024.
- Massachusetts had **978** opioid-related deaths in 2025, the first year under 1,000 since 2013.
- Overdose comes largely from **organ depression**, here meaning the slowing of an organ’s function: respiratory and cardiac depression, in which breathing and the heart slow until they fail.

The slide has two images. One is a photo of a penny beside a few tiny grains of white powder, the kind of picture often used to show how small a lethal dose of fentanyl can be. The other is a chart of U.S. drug overdose deaths involving any opioid, by gender, from 1999 to 2017. Deaths rise every year, from 8,048 in 1999 to 18,515 in 2007 and 47,600 in 2017, with the steepest climb after 2014. In every year men account for two to three times as many deaths as women.

![Bar chart of U.S. drug overdose deaths involving any opioid, 1999–2017, rising every year from 8,048 in 1999 to 18,515 in 2007 and 47,600 in 2017, with the steepest rise after 2014; lines for men and women show men at two to three times the women’s count in every year, reaching about 32,000 and 15,000 in 2017.](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g13-opioid-deaths.svg)

## Alcohol

Alcohol is a **GABA agonist in the prefrontal cortex**.

- It also binds to opioid receptors. (Alcohol acts on them indirectly: it triggers the release of the body’s own opioids, the endorphins, which bind the receptors.)
- It increases dopamine release, forming the foundation for addiction.

**Blocking GABA receptors prevents the loss of sobriety during drinking**: without its action on GABA, alcohol no longer makes you drunk. The slide’s photo shows bottles and glasses of wine, spirits and beer against an orange background.

## Alcohol abuse

Alcohol withdrawal can be extreme, even fatal. When drinking stops, two things drive it:

- compensatory glutamate release (glutamate being the main excitatory transmitter);
- a lack of activation of opioid receptors.

The result is seizures, convulsions and pain.

The lecture’s figures on alcohol abuse:

- 17% of men and 8% of women (the slide does not say what these percentages count; in context, the share affected by alcohol abuse);
- 6 deaths per day from alcohol abuse;
- 40% of hospital beds occupied by alcohol-related cases;
- an average loss of 30% of life expectancy.

The six deaths a day is the CDC’s count for alcohol poisoning alone; its count of all deaths from excessive drinking is about 178,000 a year. The slide’s photo is a family snapshot pinned to a board: an older woman, a young man holding a baby, a young woman and an older man in glasses, standing outside a house.

## Caffeine

Caffeine is the most widely consumed psychoactive drug (a drug that acts on the brain to change mood, perception or behavior).

It is an **adenosine receptor antagonist**.

- Adenosine activation is associated with sleep and relaxation, so blocking its receptors keeps you awake.
- Caffeine increases activation of the sympathetic nervous system, the arousing branch of the autonomic nervous system.
- It indirectly allows the release of dopamine, norepinephrine and serotonin.

The slide’s picture is a still of Grogu, the small green alien popularly called “Baby Yoda” from the TV series *The Mandalorian*, sipping from a cup.

## Caffeine withdrawal

- Europe recognizes caffeine as an addictive substance; America does not.
- Tolerance develops rapidly.
- 24 hours without caffeine often leads to headache, fatigue and flu-like symptoms.
- Overdose is uncommon, but it can be fatal.

The slide’s diagram shows the main symptoms of caffeine *overdose*, labeled on an outline of the body:

| Where | Symptoms |
|---|---|
| Central (brain) | irritability, anxiety, restlessness, confusion, delirium, headache, insomnia |
| Visual | seeing flashes |
| Ears | ringing |
| Skin | increased sensitivity to touch or pain |
| Heart | rapid heartbeat, irregular rhythm |
| Muscular | seizures, trembling, twitching, overextension |
| Respiratory | rapid breathing |
| Gastric | abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting (possibly with blood) |
| Urinary | frequent urination |
| Systemic | dehydration, fever |

## Pharmaceuticals: a diversity issue

Pharmaceuticals are generally optimized for a **white, male consumer base**.

- Black individuals are represented in only 4.5% of clinical trials.
- Asian and Hispanic individuals are represented in only 2%.
- There is almost zero Indigenous representation.

:::definition Adverse drug reaction
An **adverse drug reaction** is a harmful, unintended effect of a medication.
:::

Women have a significantly greater risk of adverse drug reactions than men. The slide’s graphic puts it at “2x risk”: females have nearly double the risk of developing an adverse drug reaction compared to men, drawn as two arrow-shaped bars, the women’s about twice as tall as the men’s.

- **80% of drug recalls are due to adverse effects in women**, largely because the drug was only tested in men.
- Women were banned from clinical trials from 1977 to 1993. More precisely, a 1977 FDA guideline excluded women who could become pregnant from early-phase trials, and it was lifted in 1993.

## Check yourself

The first question reviews the previous lecture.

:::question Memory test
Describe the neural factors that give rise to hallucinations and delusions in schizophrenia. What neurotransmitters and brain areas are implicated, and *how* do they cause these symptoms?

:::solution
The neural basis is largely still unknown; schizophrenia is considered a neurodevelopmental disorder, arising from interactions between genes and environment. Two neurotransmitters are implicated:

- **Dopamine:** psychosis is a misfiring of dopamine neurons, and overaction of dopamine causes paranoia, delusions and hallucinations.
- **Glutamate:** overactive firing in the **thalamus**.

In the lecturer’s notes, dopamine seems to give rise to the cognitive effects, and glutamate may be the origin of the hallucinations, though “it’s a little unclear.” Over time the brain also changes in structure, with a loss of cells and synapses. In line with the dopamine account, the first-line treatment is antipsychotic medication that blocks dopamine receptors.
:::
:::

:::question Discussion
SSRIs and SNRIs improve symptoms of depression and anxiety. What side effects do you think are common?

:::solution
The lecture lists anxiety; lack of joy and emotional numbness; increased suicidal behavior; shakiness; digestive issues; dry mouth; and decreased sex drive and sexual function, with 30–75% of patients having sexual dysfunction. The slide’s infographic adds weight gain and feeling dependent on the medication.
:::
:::

:::question Discussion
Benzodiazepines are GABA agonists with a high risk for addiction. What withdrawal effects do you think are likely?

:::solution
The lecture lists insomnia, anxiety, depression, depersonalization, nausea, hallucinations, seizures and even death. Most of these are the opposite of the drug’s calming effect: with the extra GABA gone, the brain is left overexcited, as the alcohol slides explain for compensatory glutamate release. Along with alcohol, benzodiazepines are among the few drugs whose withdrawal alone can kill.
:::
:::

:::question Discussion
Why are opiate-based drugs so addictive?

:::solution
All addictive drugs trigger dopamine release, and opioid use triggers a *large* dopamine release, so taking the drug becomes highly desirable. On top of that, opioids block pain signals in the spinal cord and increase GABA in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, relieving pain and calming anxiety. Stopping takes away the activation of opioid receptors, which brings pain, as the alcohol withdrawal slide notes.
:::
:::

:::question Exit discussion
A hypothetical new drug is being produced that acts as an antagonist on glutamate in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala. What disorder is this drug likely treating? What side effects are likely?

:::solution
The slides leave this open. Glutamate is excitatory, so a glutamate antagonist would quiet activity in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, the same direction as the GABA agonists in this lecture (benzodiazepines, alcohol).

- **Disorder.** The likeliest target is an anxiety disorder, where the amygdala fires inappropriately. Bipolar disorder is another candidate, since the previous lecture tied it to overactivation of the amygdala and prefrontal cortex. One caution from the lecture on depression: prefrontal glutamate neurons drive the GABA neurons that hold the amygdala in check, so blocking glutamate in the PFC could weaken that brake.
- **Side effects.** Expect what a damped prefrontal cortex and amygdala would give: sedation, slower thinking and decision-making, and blunted emotions. By the lecture’s pattern, the brain would also mount a compensatory response, such as increased glutamate release, so tolerance, dependence and a withdrawal with rebound anxiety, insomnia and seizures are likely, as with benzodiazepines and alcohol.
:::
:::
