This first lecture has three learning objectives:

1. Critically evaluate common neuroscience misconceptions.
2. Identify the major anatomy of neurons and key brain regions.
3. Analyze major functional neuroanatomy through disorders.

## Fact or fiction?

The lecture opened with a pop quiz: four popular claims about the brain, each to be judged fact or fiction, and why.

**“Some people can be considered ‘left-brained’ or ‘right-brained.’”** Fiction. The slide shows the poster version of this myth: a brain split down the middle, the left half drawn as a plain outline and labeled analytical thinking, numbers, language, reasoning, logic, and science and math; the right half splashed with paint and labeled emotional intelligence, imagination, expression, art awareness, intuition and creativity.

**“The average person does not use 100% of their brain capacity, only about 30% on average.”** Fiction. The slide pairs the claim with a functional brain scan (one slice from the side, one from above) in which only a few patches light up red. Patches like these mark where activity changed during a task; the rest of the brain is working too.

The next slide, “These images are bogus, sorry ☹,” takes on a meme that circulates online. It sets a colorful “normal brain” scan beside an “ADHD brain,” a “PTSD brain,” a “bipolar brain” and a “depressed brain,” each captioned with a dismissive remark (“It’s just a made up ailment to give kids an excuse for not paying attention,” “Get over it lots of people have it worse”). The scan comparisons are bogus.

**“Learning is facilitated by the creation of new neurons.”** Fiction, in the lecturer’s verdict. Learning comes from the brain changing in response to experience, which is the next claim.

**“The brain changes (is ‘plastic’) in response to the environment.”** Fact.

:::definition
The brain is **plastic**: it changes in response to the environment and to experience. This capacity for change is called **plasticity**.
:::

## Say hello to your brain

The human brain

- has about 80 billion neurons;
- performs, by a popular estimate, about a billion billion ($10^{18}$) calculations per second;
- is always changing based on experience.

## Neurons

![A neuron with its parts labeled: dendrites, nucleus, soma (cell body), myelin sheaths, axon and axon terminals, with the direction the message travels](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g1-neuron.svg)

A neuron has four main parts, and a message always travels through them in the same direction, from left to right in the figure:

- the **dendrites**, the branches that take in information;
- the **soma** or **cell body**, which contains the **nucleus**;
- the **axon**, a long fiber leaving the cell body, wrapped along its length in **myelin sheaths**;
- the **axon terminals**, the branched endings at the far end of the axon.

In the lecturer’s words, a neuron takes in information at its dendrites, processes it in the cell body, sends an **action potential** (its electrical signal) down the axon, and “spits” **neurotransmitters** (chemical messengers) out of the axon terminals onto the next neuron.

## All kinds of neurons

There are a few “flavors” of neuron, told apart by how many processes (branches) leave the cell body and where.

![Four types of neuron: unipolar, bipolar, pseudounipolar and multipolar, with the cell body, myelin, dendrites, axon and sensory receptors labeled](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g1-neuron-types.svg)

- **Unipolar**: a single process leaves the cell body. In the figure it is myelinated and branches at its far end.
- **Bipolar**: two processes leave the cell body from opposite sides. One end branches into dendrites; the other ends at a sensory receptor.
- **Pseudounipolar**: the cell body sits off to one side on a short stalk, joined to one long process that runs in both directions. One end branches into sensory receptor endings.
- **Multipolar**: many dendrites leave the cell body, along with a single myelinated axon.

Under the microscope, real neurons look like a dense forest: cell bodies with long, thin processes running between them (on the slide, lit up in fluorescent green).

## Two nervous systems

:::definition
The nervous system has two divisions.

- The **central nervous system (CNS)** is the brain and the spinal cord.
- The **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** is everything else: the neurons that run through the rest of the body.
:::

As the lecturer put it, all you are is a brain and spinal cord piloting a meat suit. The PNS is “just everything else”: you’ve got neurons everywhere.

![Tree diagram of the nervous system: peripheral and central (brain and spinal cord); the peripheral splits into autonomic and somatic, and the autonomic into sympathetic and parasympathetic](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g1-nervous-system.svg)

The peripheral nervous system divides further:

- the **autonomic** nervous system controls the self-regulated action of the internal organs and glands;
- the **somatic** nervous system controls voluntary movements of the skeletal muscles.

The autonomic nervous system in turn has two branches: the **sympathetic** (arousing) and the **parasympathetic** (calming).

On the slide, the diagram is flanked by two body outlines: the peripheral nervous system, with nerves running out to every part of the body, and the central nervous system, only the brain and spinal cord. A further slide (“This is u”) shows the CNS on its own: a classic anatomical drawing of the brain and spinal cord with the spinal nerves branching off, and a photograph of a real human brain and spinal cord dissected out with their nerves attached.

### Receptors in the periphery communicate with the CNS

Sensory receptors out in the body pick up information and pass it to the central nervous system. The slide gives two examples.

**The skin.** A block of skin is drawn with its outer layer, the **epidermis**, over the deeper **dermis**, and with these receptors in it:

| Receptor | What it senses |
|---|---|
| Free nerve endings | pain, heat, cold |
| Merkel disks | touch |
| Krause end bulbs | touch |
| Root hair plexus | touch |
| Meissner corpuscles | touch |
| Pacinian corpuscles | pressure |
| Ruffini endings | pressure |

**The eye.** Light enters through the cornea, pupil and lens and lands on the **retina** at the back of the eye; the **macula** is a small patch of the retina, and the **optic nerve** leaves from the back of the eye. A close-up of the retina shows light passing through layers of retinal neurons to reach the photoreceptors, the **rods** and **cones**, which sit against the **retinal pigment epithelium** at the very back.

## Brain regions

### Cerebellum and brainstem

The cerebellum and brainstem handle

- basic biological function, the “life support system”;
- reflex movement;
- balance;
- eye movement control.

The slide’s drawing shows the brain from the side with the cerebellum labeled: the finely ridged structure tucked under the back of the brain, behind the brainstem.

Which division of the nervous system do these regions likely regulate? The peripheral nervous system: broadly all of it, but mainly the autonomic part.

### Prefrontal cortex

The prefrontal cortex is the front part of the frontal lobe. Its subregions are named by anatomical direction: **dorsal** means toward the top and **ventral** toward the bottom; **anterior** (or **rostral**) means toward the front and **posterior** (or **caudal**) toward the back; **lateral** means toward the side and **medial** toward the middle.

| Subregion | Functions |
|---|---|
| Dorsolateral | decision-making, working memory |
| Lateral | working memory, reasoning, planning, imagination |
| Ventrolateral | motor control |
| Ventromedial | emotion regulation, self-control |
| Orbitofrontal | emotion regulation, reward, reward/punishment assessment |

The slide’s figure shows the brain from the side (front to the left) with regions of the frontal lobe colored and labeled with their Brodmann area (BA) numbers, a standard numbering of cortical regions. At the front tip is the lateral frontopolar cortex (BA 10); behind it are the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (BA 9/46) above and the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (BA 47/45/44) below; further back come the anterior premotor cortex (BA 8), the ventral anterior premotor cortex (BA 44/6), the premotor cortex (BA 6) and the primary motor cortex (BA 4).

Why were frontal lobotomies (surgery that cuts into the frontal lobes) once a popular, but highly flawed, method for treating psychosis? The lecturer’s answer: “No think = no think bad thoughts????” Damaging the prefrontal cortex shuts down thinking in general: the disturbing thoughts go, and so do the decision-making, planning, emotion regulation and self-control in the table above.

### Amygdala

The **amygdala**, a small structure deep inside the brain, handles

- emotional regulation;
- fear and stress.

Why is stress important? It “makes you not die”: stress responses are what keep you alive in the face of danger.

### Amygdala subregions

- The **basolateral** and **central amygdala** receive input from the broader limbic system (see below) and handle the expression of fear and anxiety.
- The **medial amygdala** handles behavior, hormonal control, and the expression of social anxiety.

![Inputs and outputs of the amygdala’s lateral, basal, central and medial nuclei, with each output labeled by the behavior it serves](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g1-amygdala-circuit.svg)

The figure maps the amygdala’s connections. It shows four nuclei: the lateral (LA) and basal (BA) amygdala, which together form the basolateral amygdala; the central amygdala (CeA); and the medial amygdala (MeA). Connections are of three kinds: glutamatergic (excitatory), GABAergic (inhibitory) and neuromodulatory.

- **Inputs.** The associative cortex, thalamus, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex and brainstem send glutamatergic inputs to the LA, BA and CeA. Neuromodulatory inputs come from the ventral tegmental area (VTA), dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN), locus coeruleus (LC) and basal forebrain (BF) to the BA, CeA and MeA.
- **Inside the amygdala.** The LA projects to the BA, CeA and MeA, and the BA to the CeA and MeA.
- **Outputs of the BA** (glutamatergic): entorhinal cortex (EC), for freezing; medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), for anxiety and freezing; hippocampus, for anxiety; nucleus accumbens (NAc), for anxiety and reward; bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), for anxiety and fear.
- **Outputs of the CeA** (GABAergic): substantia nigra pars lateralis (SNL), for appetitive and aversive learning; locus coeruleus (LC), for anxiety and aversive behavior; nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS), for autonomic regulation; ventral tegmental area (VTA), for reward; periaqueductal gray (PAG), for pain and freezing; parabrachial nucleus (PBN), for feeding; basal forebrain (BF), for motor behavior; hypothalamus, for hormones; and the BNST.
- **Outputs of the MeA** (GABAergic): the NAc, BNST, hypothalamus and BF.

The class then did an activity, *Personal Space Invaders*, which sits among the amygdala slides.

### Hippocampus

The **hippocampus**, shown on the slide as a curved structure deep in the lower part of the brain, handles

- memory storage and retrieval;
- spatial memory and encoding.

Damage to the hippocampus causes **amnesia**, a loss of memory.

The hippocampus also communicates with the amygdala. This matters for survival: you have to remember the thing that hurt you or made you feel bad, so that you can avoid it.

### Thalamus

The **thalamus** is the brain’s sensory and routing center, and it is also involved in sleep and wakefulness. The slide’s figure is a view of the inner face of the brain with the thalamus, hypothalamus, frontal lobe, olfactory bulb, amygdala and hippocampus labeled.

:::definition
**Synesthesia** is the perception of one sense through another, for example hearing colors.
:::

How might synesthesia work? One possibility: the thalamus sends axons carrying one sense to the wrong sensory cortex, the area of cortex that handles a different sense.

### The limbic system

:::definition
The **limbic system** is the brain’s system for emotion and memory. The slide’s figure shows the thalamus, amygdala and hippocampus deep inside the brain, beneath the cortex.
:::

### Hypothalamus

The **hypothalamus** is the brain’s gateway to the periphery: it runs the **neuroendocrine system**, through which the nervous system controls the release of hormones. It is involved in

- stress;
- sexual behavior;
- aggression;
- development;
- social behaviors.

The slide’s figure shows (a) the brain cut down the middle, with the pineal gland, hypothalamus, pituitary gland and spinal cord labeled, and (b) a close-up of the hypothalamus with the pituitary gland hanging from it on a stalk, the **infundibulum**; the pituitary has an anterior and a posterior lobe.

Why is communication between the thalamus and the hypothalamus so important? You have to respond hormonally to what you sense in the outside world: the thalamus routes the sensory information, and the hypothalamus turns it into a hormonal response.

### Pituitary

The **pituitary** gland

- releases hormones into the bloodstream;
- activates organs and glands in the periphery: the adrenal glands (stress), the gonads (reproduction, development), the heart and lungs, and digestion.

What happens if the adrenal gland receives too much stimulation from the pituitary? It floods the body with stress hormones, chiefly cortisol.

Neuroscience isn’t just the brain! A comic from The Awkward Yeti closes this part: a hooded figure (the hypothalamus, presumably) tells the little pituitary, “You wield great power, Pituitary. Use it wisely! Go now, release the hormone armies and bring balance to the realm!”, and when the pituitary only squeaks, boots it out the door.

## Check yourself

The first three questions refer to this neuron.

![An unlabeled neuron with arrows A, B and C pointing to three of its parts](/notes/neuroscience/figures/g1-neuron-quiz.svg)

:::question
What is the part of the neuron marked A?
:::solution
The nucleus, inside the cell body (soma).
:::
:::

:::question
What is the part of the neuron marked B?
:::solution
The axon terminals, the branched endings of the axon that pass the message on to the next neuron.
:::
:::

:::question
What is the part of the neuron marked C?
:::solution
The dendrites, the branches that take in information.
:::
:::

:::question
What is a key function of the amygdala?

- (a) Sensory processing
- (b) Cognition
- (c) Memory
- (d) Stress

:::solution
(d) Stress. The amygdala handles emotional regulation, fear and stress; memory is the hippocampus’s job, and sensory routing is the thalamus’s.
:::
:::

:::question
What is a key function of the hippocampus?

- (a) Sensory processing
- (b) Cognition
- (c) Memory
- (d) Stress

:::solution
(c) Memory. The hippocampus handles memory storage and retrieval, and spatial memory and encoding.
:::
:::

:::question
A patient has damage to the amygdala. Which symptom will they most likely have?

- (a) Difficulty discriminating sensory stimuli
- (b) Confusion and impaired memory
- (c) Decreased or no fear response
- (d) Inability to make or execute plans

:::solution
(c) Decreased or no fear response, since the amygdala handles fear. Impaired memory points to the hippocampus instead, and trouble with plans to the prefrontal cortex.
:::
:::

:::question
A patient has damaged connections between the hypothalamus and the pituitary. Which symptom will they likely have?

- (a) Impaired physical and sexual development
- (b) Very little interest in social interaction
- (c) Decreased or no fear response
- (d) All of the above
- (e) None of the above

:::solution
(d) All of the above. The pituitary is the hypothalamus’s route to the periphery, so cutting the link disrupts the hormonal side of everything the hypothalamus controls: development and sexual behavior (through the gonads), social behaviors, and the stress response (through the adrenal glands).
:::
:::

:::question Exit discussion
You’re a neurologist working at a hospital. A patient is brought in after a biking accident with the following symptoms:

- poor memory of the event, and confusion;
- difficulty completing a puzzle;
- makes inappropriate comments on your clothing;
- a general sense of apathy.

What region(s) of the brain have likely been damaged? What evidence supports your conclusions?
:::solution
Most likely the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex. The poor memory points to the hippocampus (memory storage and retrieval); the trouble with the puzzle points to the dorsolateral and lateral prefrontal cortex (working memory, reasoning, planning); and the inappropriate comments and apathy point to the ventromedial and orbitofrontal cortex (self-control, emotion regulation, reward assessment).
:::
:::
