A sequence starts $2, 6, 12, 20, 30, \dots$. What is the $100$th term, and what polynomial
generates it? Subtract adjacent terms, then subtract again — the pattern collapses to a constant,
and that constant tells you both the degree and the formula.

## Discovery

1. Given a sequence $a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots$, define the **difference** $\Delta a_k = a_{k+1} - a_k$.
   Compute the difference sequence of $2, 6, 12, 20, 30$. Then take the difference *of that*
   sequence ($\Delta^2$). Keep going. What happens, and after how many rounds?
2. Suppose $a_k = k^2$. Compute $\Delta a_k = (k+1)^2 - k^2$ as a polynomial in $k$ — what degree?
   Then compute $\Delta^2 a_k$. In general, what does one application of $\Delta$ do to the degree
   of a polynomial sequence?
3. Iterate step 2: if $a_k$ is a polynomial of degree $d$, after how many differences do you reach
   a constant, and after how many do you reach zero?
4. Read a formula off the **leading edge** of the difference table (the values $\Delta^j a_0$).
   Recall $\binom{k}{j}$ is a degree-$j$ polynomial in $k$. Guess how to combine the top-row
   entries $\Delta^j a_0$ with binomial coefficients to rebuild $a_k$.

### The Result

Let $\Delta a_k = a_{k+1} - a_k$ and $\Delta^{j+1} = \Delta(\Delta^j)$. If $(a_k)$ is a polynomial
in $k$ of degree $d$ with leading coefficient $c$, then:
- $\Delta a_k$ is a polynomial of degree $d - 1$;
- $\Delta^d a_k = c\cdot d!$ is a **nonzero constant**, and $\Delta^{d+1} a_k = 0$.

Moreover (**Newton's forward-difference formula**), for such a sequence,
$$a_k = \sum_{j=0}^{d} \binom{k}{j}\, \Delta^{j} a_0,$$
which reconstructs the polynomial from the top-left diagonal of the difference table. Conversely, a
sequence is generated by a degree-$d$ polynomial iff its $(d+1)$-th differences all vanish.

### Proof

**Degree drop.** Write $a_k = c\,k^d + (\text{lower degree})$. Then
$$\Delta a_k = c\big((k+1)^d - k^d\big) + \Delta(\text{lower}).$$
By the binomial theorem $(k+1)^d - k^d = d\,k^{d-1} + \binom d2 k^{d-2} + \cdots$, a polynomial of
degree $d-1$ with leading coefficient $cd$. The differenced lower-order part has degree $\le d-2$.
So $\Delta a_k$ has degree exactly $d-1$ and leading coefficient $cd$. Inducting, $\Delta^d a_k$
has degree $0$ and leading coefficient $c\cdot d(d-1)\cdots 1 = c\,d!$, and one more difference of a
constant is $0$.

**Newton's formula.** Both sides are polynomials in $k$ of degree $\le d$. It suffices to check
they agree at the $d+1$ points $k = 0, 1, \dots, d$ (then uniqueness of interpolation, via the
Factor Theorem, forces equality). At $k = m$, the identity $\Delta^j a_0 = \sum_{i=0}^{j}
(-1)^{j-i}\binom{j}{i} a_i$ and the Vandermonde/telescoping identity $\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom mj
\Delta^j a_0 = a_m$ hold — indeed this is the finite analogue of the Taylor expansion, provable by
induction on $m$: assuming it for $m$, $\sum_j \binom{m+1}{j}\Delta^j a_0 = \sum_j
\left[\binom mj + \binom{m}{j-1}\right]\Delta^j a_0 = a_m + \sum_j \binom{m}{j-1}\Delta^j a_0 = a_m
+ \Delta a_m = a_{m+1}$, using Pascal's rule and $\sum_j \binom{m}{j-1}\Delta^j a_0 = \Delta a_m$.
$\blacksquare$

### Worked Instance

Sequence $2, 6, 12, 20, 30, \dots$. Build the difference table (leading edge in bold):
$$\begin{array}{lccccc}
a_k: & \mathbf{2} & 6 & 12 & 20 & 30 \\
\Delta: & \mathbf{4} & 6 & 8 & 10 \\
\Delta^2: & \mathbf{2} & 2 & 2 \\
\Delta^3: & \mathbf{0} & 0
\end{array}$$
The second differences are constant ($2$) and third differences vanish, so $a_k$ is a quadratic
(degree $d = 2$). By Newton's formula with $\Delta^0 a_0 = 2,\ \Delta^1 a_0 = 4,\ \Delta^2 a_0 =
2$:
$$a_k = 2\binom k0 + 4\binom k1 + 2\binom k2 = 2 + 4k + 2\cdot\frac{k(k-1)}{2} = k^2 + 3k + 2 = (k+1)(k+2).$$
Then $a_{99}$ (the $100$th term, $k$ from $0$) is $100\cdot 101 = 10{,}100$.

### Pitfalls

- Constant $d$-th differences signal degree $d$; but you must see the $(d+1)$-th differences hit
  **zero** to be sure the pattern is polynomial (finitely many terms can be fit by many
  polynomials).
- The leading coefficient is $\Delta^d a_0 / d!$, not $\Delta^d a_0$ itself — divide by the
  factorial.
- Newton's formula uses the **top-left** diagonal $\Delta^j a_0$, indexed from the start; mixing in
  a different column gives a shifted, wrong polynomial.

## Connections

Prereqs: the Factor Theorem / polynomial uniqueness (to justify Newton's formula). Finite
differences are the discrete calculus twin of derivatives ($\Delta \leftrightarrow d/dx$) and the
data-first counterpart of Lagrange interpolation; they power summation formulas and detecting the
degree of a mystery sequence. Recognition trigger: "guess the formula for a sequence," or "a
polynomial is defined by a table of values."
