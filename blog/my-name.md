# My name

My legal—that is to say my birth—name is Samuel Stephens. On academic papers and in public presence I maintain the moniker “S. D. V. Stephens,” signing off all emails with “SDVS.” I have added a singular initial and altered the content of another. David became a borrowed and playfully pretentious facsimile of Erasmus’ forename, Desiderius; and the added “V” for Vichaama: an attempt at heritage reclamation. Borrowing a metaphor from Don DeLillo, it (SDVS) has added an air of false dignification—a signifier, as if I wore a borrowed suit coat, slightly too large, tailored for a taller, more full man, with hopes that my adult body might somehow grow into it. 

I live in the shadow of the suit coat. I deem it inspiration and aspirational, a motivation; at times, however, it becomes heavy, hot, and unwieldy.

But what am I to think of it myself?   Do I carry it without thought, as if natural? Do I look in the mirror and see this man, coat and all, and identify? 

Do I love him? 

When a name matches who you want to be, who you want to become, you are satisfied. When a name is all that you can currently change in order to get closer to your inner Elizabet (ref: Bergman’s Persona), your inner ideal, then such a name feels bittersweet, deistic, too old, too new, false, aspirational, inspiration, depressing, and more real than you are yourself. Desperate are we, some of us, for a rebirth, a name is a plot, and as a plot it invariably begs to end in death—here, for yourself. A new name is an attempt at pheonixian suicide, praying, demanding for an infernal, radical rebirth: total palingenesis.
