# A Dream


I had a rather odd dream a little less than a week ago. At some imaginary, unnamed, and presumably reputable academic institution, a respected professor of American literature gave a lecture on post-modern American literature, the content of which eludes me, as those pesky details in dreams often do. DeLillo’s *White Noise* was not specifically mentioned here, although DeLillo, in general, was. Following this lecture of dream-length— that is to say something which has no temporal markers for measurement— while packing up her material the professor chatted with some students, myself included. The particulars of our conversation continued on along a walk to her office, this time just her and I. 

We were conversing on the role and legitimacy of satire and burying authorial convictions within said satire: how are we to parse and differentiate the elements of satire that are face-value and ironic (this answer, of course, being boiled down to a developed sense of critical thinking and cultivated media literacy through exposure, reflection, and education; but that is nothing new). This topic led to the main contents of the dream: we argued and discussed satire in *White Noise*.

She posited that it was indeed *entirely* a satire. That the convictions held and simulacral oddities between our world and the world of *White Noise* were not merely to create a sense of fuzzy yet alien familiarity, but, too, to emphasize that it is like the simulacra: an ironic, bastardized (but in this case quite intentional) reproduction; the word encapsulating that: satire. I argued that the work weaseled in genuine intellectual (and somewhat reprehensible) positions to such a degree that the book used satire as a shield and vehicle to probe at something more, and that the critiques fell flat, the ideas were superficial, and the analysis provided was circling a truth and dramatically missing the mark. She told me, though: that was the point! The critique of the book is not a critique of modernity, it is not a critique of capitulation or capitalism or etc, etc. it was instead a critique of the critiques! The academic was the perfect vessel: his field of study obviously pseudo-intellectual (Hitler studies; there was literally just one book that was used—*Mein Kampf*); the very idea that the field became world renowned; the fact that the college was literally called “The-College-On-The-Hill” and that throughout the book no character is truly a caricature of anything or anyone, nor were said characters starkly unique; instead the conglomerated as a boring mass of bundled contradictions, though certainly human, nonetheless ignorant. Mis/dis-information was frequently perpetuated by characters with great levels of education; and the entire character of Murray, especially near the end, constantly clarifying that themselves, as academics, are given not only the privilege but the right to speak on the matters of the mind and theory and society while contemplating from a point entirely devoid of society and societal participation: they were above others who had to engage with social theory as praxis.  The family perfectly embodied the middle-class-thinking-themself-bourgeois dynamic; they were entirely reified and as such came to view economic base and superstructure as entirely alien, transcendent, and deistic; completely beyond the veil that contains human capability. They had clearly failed at recognizing their subject-object consciousness to such an extreme degree that, by the end, when this nebulous mass intensified its growth inside of Jack, the nebulousness— specified within the text itself as the most notable characteristic of the growing mass —was there to literally be the object itself in the subject-object relationship; Jack, through the last several choices in the book, has the opportunity to alter the form of the mass to be one of subject, but instead it became one fully of object. The mass is not even mentioned in the last few chapters, it is not cared about, it is never brought up again: Jack is hollow. He has become a full object. But this was the entire point of the book! It was a story of becoming object. The critique of critiques thereby that held the author’s most pointed intentions was that pseudointellectualization and analysis among the academy and intellectual society, all too often, instead of bringing about subject-object (or from a Marxist perspective, class) consciousness, brought about a new, strengthened, more reified version of this consciousness, and submitted themselves to be analysts to an ineffable and entirely autonomous capitalistic machine. The intellectual(s) are comforted by their education, capabilities, surroundings, resources, and brilliance; but such educational and intellectual prowess does not make one correct—it gives them, instead, a more complex manifold in which to be wrong. 

By the end of this fruitful and aethereal conversation, I had been entirely and utterly convinced to forfeit my preceding position on this book, and upon waking, I realized that, indeed, she was right! Perhaps the analysis of the mass here may be a stretch of sorts, it doesn’t really matter in this instance as it is besides the point, but the overall content and its conclusions had changed my mind, quite literally, overnight. How both totally strange and wonderful! Dreams be dreaming frfr.
