August 26th,  2025. In the thick and muggy air, a film of humidity laid on my skin: warm and oddly comforting. Voices passed behind me, coagulating into a single, soft, and ambient hum. Dusk was well underway. A soft *auréole* around the lamps that lined the concrete forecourt of Harvard’s Science Center lit the surface of my journal. As I sat upright, orthogonal to an ash wood-slatted sculptural lounger, the slats slowly pressed their shape into my legs and rear. I began to write. A friend and I planned a rendezvous for an hour ago  at this exact spot, to attend a comedy show. He hadn’t shown up. While attempting a proof on boolean lattice structures, I felt a vibration in my pocket. I grabbed my phone to check; it read, “*I’m so sorry! I just forgot*.” The simple and remorseful statement inspired me: I forewent my proof for the time being and began to fervently write prose. 

I so deeply feared being forgotten. To be forgotten is to die a second death. The first contains some dignity, even in the most tragic and unfortunate circumstances. The second holds irrevocable finality, and, for brief moments, though we may persist as people yet to experience our first death, our second death can make itself known; and, for a brief, yet existentially terrifying moment, we are rendered nonexistent in the memory of our loved ones. My pen acquainted this knowledge to the pages, inking over a great number of them. 

I looked up, taking a breath from writing, allowing my hands to relax. I saw the stars in the sky. Immediately they transfixed me. How was it that in this city, these stars still shone so bright? Vega was the brightest star I could see in the sky. Its radiance filled the heavens and its awe was so great that tears fell down my cheeks. I felt that it could flood the whole night sky and still never blind me. I was compelled to lie down, my back forming to the lounger’s contoured undulating surface, its impressions upon my legs and rear now on my back. 

My gaze never left Vega. Suddenly and violently, Vega’s light swelled, filling up the whole sky, the entire world, all of the past and present—all that has ever been. Birthing a veridical hallucination, the star allowed me to peer through its eyes and see the panoply of people and peoples within itself. 

The climate was tropical. I was in southern Mexico. I saw *La Bestia, El Tren de la Muerte*; a small family, parents and a daughter, harboring dreams of a better life, clung to the top of the train. They would never make it to their destination. There was no human who harbored memories of them left in the world; Vega never forgot them.
A shift—I was then in a small room, in Brussels, Belgium, 1848. The air was filled with smoke, tobacco, and a strong miasma, no doubt indicative of a home belonging to someone tragically afflicted with *hidradenitis suppurativa*—what, in the 19th century, was called “excruciating boils”. A bearded man in his late 20s was furiously writing; he dreamt of a better world, a new way of organizing society. He somehow knew that what he was writing would go on to radically change the world. It would allow people to hope, to dream of a more equal future, a better future, a new future. Vega saw him.
A radical shift again—I was outside of my childhood home. It was night. I was standing outside in the grassy twenty acres of wooded Texas lands. There was a slight breeze that carried a chill through the forest.  I looked up and saw the stars, they were so much brighter out here. I looked towards my former home and saw my 13-year-old self standing barefoot outside, crying. My parents had left me outside as punishment during the night. They would later go on to forget that such an event happened. Vega did not forget. 

I came to. I was once again outside in that same forecourt. Tears streamed down my face. I was silent. Vega had shown me something impossibly beautiful. Time passed in a flurry, and abruptly it was an hour later. My friend arrived, immediately and profusely apologizing for forgetting me and the comedy show. I no longer cared. A transcendent insight took place here, under the eyes of the stars. The stars see the ugly of humanity, the forgotten, the dead and deprived: those who had their second death. The stars hold the memories of the forgotten. I know that now. I told my friend to not be saddened and that I completely forgive him. I told him to lie next to me and look up at the stars. I pointed to Vega, noting that its light spanned 25 light-years to arrive at our eyes; we discussed how utterly honored we felt in that moment to have the star's light shine upon us. As we both looked up I realized, faintly, I could see him in the star. He looked at me; I at him. Yet, we both laid parallel, both with eyes fixed on the star. We saw each other in the stars. 

When I returned home, I opened my journal and noted my imaginative metaphysical celestial encounter. I understood now what my subconscious told me through the eyes of the stars: regardless of second deaths, the stars do not forget. Until time inevitably causes the last star in the sky to lose light, and until every celestial body has dimmed and become quiet, the stars will remember me.
